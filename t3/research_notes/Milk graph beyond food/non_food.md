# Non-food products made from milk (technical, industrial, household, cosmetic, agricultural)

Scope note: about 20 web searches and no full-page fetches; most facts rest on search-result summaries of the named pages. The "Catalogue" under the last question lists 30+ candidate nodes with parent, step, era, use, notes and a milk-defining verdict, and marks the six most striking with ★. Existing nodes reused as parents include Skimmed milk, Skimmed milk powder, Acid casein, Rennet casein, Caseinate, Casein glue & paint, Galalith, Lanital, Sweet whey, Acid whey, Whey powder, Whey permeate, Lactose and Brined cheese.

**Proposed rule for "milk product or mere ingredient":** draw a node when a milk fraction is the *structural or functional base* (binder, polymer, fibre, substrate, feedstock that becomes the product), so that the product would not exist without it. Leave out products where milk is one optional additive in a recipe built on something else, such as "milk soap" (the base is a fat saponified with lye), cosmetics with "milk protein" on the label, or carp boilies. A middle category is a *conversion* product: whey → lactic acid → PLA, or whey → ethanol. The milk sugar there is the feedstock, but the end product is chemically identical to the same thing made from maize. These are still honest "made from" edges and worth showing, since that is the point of a "what milk becomes" graph.

## Casein materials (glue, paint, tempera, plastics, fibres, coatings, label glue, leather, films)

### Takeaway
Casein, the curd protein (Acid casein / Rennet casein), was the first industrial polymer made from milk. It went into wood glue (the standard aircraft glue of WWI, and used in early Mosquitos), paints, horn-like plastic (Galalith/Erinoid buttons and pens), wool-like fibres (Lanital, Aralac, now QMilch), paper and leather coatings, and ice-water-proof beer-label glue. Synthetics displaced most of these uses after about 1940, but casein label glue, leather finishes and milk paint survive.

### Cited Findings
**Casein glue / aircraft**
- Casein glue had seen little use in the US until about 1916–17. Wartime demand for a water-resistant glue for military aircraft then drove its rapid production. The Forest Products Laboratory's gluing research began with laminated wood for airplane propellers — [FPL Report 280 (reissued 1961)](https://ir.library.oregonstate.edu/jspui/bitstream/1957/882/1/FPL_280ocr_rev.pdf); [Forest Products Journal](https://forestprod.org/?p=83107)
- During WWI the FPL's central aircraft work was developing water-resistant airplane glues. After the war casein stayed the most popular airplane adhesive, but its durability in damp conditions was doubtful. A 1920 NACA subcommittee asked for a waterproof glue, and the FPL tested preservatives for casein — [UW digital library, plywood stressed-skin history](https://search.library.wisc.edu/digital/AMXJK6GX7TO7WS8U/text/ACCSLDEK5AVJLZ83)
- In 1920 NACA published "Glues Used in Airplane Parts", compiled by FPL staff, covering how casein glue is made and its strength — [NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19930091128/downloads/19930091128.pdf)
- The de Havilland Mosquito was first bonded with casein glue (casein + slaked lime + water + sodium hydroxide) and later with urea-formaldehyde Aerolite. Sources disagree on why. One calls it a performance upgrade ([People's Mosquito](https://www.peoplesmosquito.org.uk/2015/11/25/the-mosquito-at-75/)); others link it to glue failures in tropical service ([Solarspell/Wikipedia copy](https://solarspell-dls.sfis.asu.edu/mea/wikipedia/wp/d/de_Havilland_Mosquito.htm); [Tego film, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tego_film))

**Casein / milk paint, whitewash, tempera**
- The traditional milk paint recipe is milk (casein) + lime + pigment. Lime and casein react into a tough paint that bonds to wood. The modern revival dates from Charles Thibeau's 1974 recipe (Old Fashioned Milk Paint) — [Woodworker's Journal](https://www.woodworkersjournal.com/milk-paint-past-future/); [PCI Magazine](https://pcimag.com/articles/99075-old-fashioned-milk-paint-celebrates-40-years)
- Myth check: architectural historian Richard Candee calls the colonial "brickdust and buttermilk" paint story a myth. Recipes dated after 1800 were cited as "colonial", and he names the French Revolution period as the start of extensive use of casein house paint — [Historic New England PDF](https://images.historicnewengland.org/rs/12782/pdf/default.pdf); summarised in [Sawmill Creek](https://sawmillcreek.org/goto/post?id=2438977)
- Casein tempera is milk protein used as the binder instead of egg. Around 1900 a tempera revival among artists, mainly in Germany, Switzerland and Italy, included home-made "cheese- and milk-based concoctions" — [IIC review of *Painting in Tempera, c.1900*](https://www.iiconservation.org/node/6177); [Rhiannon Piper](https://www.rhiannonpiper.com/articles/tempera-paint-history-chemistry-technique-and-legacy)

**Galalith / casein plastic**
- In 1893 Auguste Trillat found that formaldehyde makes casein insoluble. Wilhelm Krische (a Hanover printer) and Adolf Spitteler then developed a horn-like plastic while looking for a washable white school board. It was patented in 1898 or 1899 (sources differ) and shown as "Galalith" (Greek "milk stone") at the 1900 Paris Exposition. The story of a cat knocking formaldehyde into milk is probably untrue — [Wikipedia: Galalith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galalith); [Ganoksin](https://www.ganoksin.com/article/galalith-jewelry-milk-stone/)
- Curing was very slow: about a year for a 25 mm sheet. The plastic was machined into buttons because it took a wide range of colours and polished well. It was also sold as Erinoid (UK), Lactoid and Aladdinite. The Galalith companies merged in 1904 into Internationale Galalith-Gesellschaft Hoff & Co. — [Ganoksin](https://www.ganoksin.com/article/galalith-jewelry-milk-stone/); [Australian button history](https://www.austbuttonhistory.com/?p=12876); [CAMEO: casein plastic](https://cameo.mfa.org/wiki/Casein_plastic)
- Erinoid was made at Stroud, Gloucestershire — [GSIA Journal 1999 reprint](https://gsia.org.uk/sites/reprints/1999/gi199944.pdf)

**Casein fibres**
- Lanital: Antonio Ferretti's 1935 patent, made by SNIA Viscosa in Milan. Skim milk is skimmed, the casein coagulated, dissolved, pushed through spinnerets, hardened and cut. Output was 94 t in 1936 and 760 t in the first seven months of 1937, and it was expected to cover only 20–25% of Italy's wool needs. It was listed by decree among the "autarchic" fibres after the League of Nations sanctions of 1935 — [Nature 1937 report](https://nature.com/articles/1401090b0.pdf); [Atlas Obscura](https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/lanital-milk-dress-qmilch); [Fondazione Zegna](https://www.fondazionezegna.org/en/2021/01/12/zegnas-autarchic-fabrics-sanctions-and-problem-solving/); [TIME archive](https://time.com/archive/6757849/business-lanital)
- An earlier German precursor (Todtenhaupt, about 1904–1916) is mentioned. The dates differ by source — [TIME archive](https://time.com/archive/6757849/business-lanital); [Atlas Obscura](https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/lanital-milk-dress-qmilch)
- Aralac (US): National Dairy Products Corporation made it from skim-milk casein, with about 5 million lb/yr capacity (1942). It went into fur and wool felts (hats) and blends, and cost more than rayon but less than wool. Generic name: azlon — [JTI Abstracts 1942](https://cybra.lodz.pl/Content/26824/JTI_Abstracts_February_vol_XXXIII_1942.pdf); [Wikipedia: Milk fiber](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Aralac)
- Casein fibres were weak when wet and prone to mould. They were displaced by acrylics after WWII — [CAMEO: casein fiber](https://cameo.mfa.org/wiki/Casein_fiber); [Wikipedia: Milk fiber](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Aralac)
- QMilch (Hanover, Anke Domaske, about 2011) is casein from unsellable milk, kneaded with other natural ingredients and spun. Domaske says about 6 L of milk goes into a dress. Claims are largely the company's own — [BIOÖKONOMIE.DE interview](https://biooekonomie.de/en/actors/interviews/milking-it-textiles-made-casein); [Atlas Obscura](https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/lanital-milk-dress-qmilch)

**Paper coating, leather finishing, label adhesives**
- Casein was long the main binder in coated (and cast-coated) paper. It was replaced by starch and styrene-butadiene latexes because of cost, uneven supply and microbial spoilage. Casein paper-coating patents include US2374862 (filed 1942) — [US4302367 patent](https://patents.google.com/patent/US4302367); [US2374862](https://patents.google.com/patent/US2374862A/enFirst)
- Leather: by 1920 casein was the main binder in leather finishes (pigmented finishes from about 1910). Nitrocellulose and synthetic resins followed. Casein glazing finishes are still used, applied and then polished with heat and pressure. A Novo Nordisk patent hardens casein with transglutaminase — [Smithsonian MCI](https://mci.si.edu/node/1265284); [US5531795](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5531795); [Negroni / Sanyo tannery](https://www.negronistore.com/en/blogs/negronijournal/tannery-trails-exploring-tannery-sanyo-leather-part-2)
- Beer-bottle labels: beer is chilled in ice water, so labels must survive 1–3 days of immersion. Casein-based adhesives were developed for this "many years ago" and long dominated. H.B. Fuller still sells a casein label glue (Swift®tak 3570) for returnable and one-way beer bottles, and Henkel markets casein-free alternatives — [US4675351 patent](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/4675351); [H.B. Fuller](https://www.hbfullerproducts.com/products/swift-tak-3570); [Packaging Insights / Henkel](https://www.packaginginsights.com/news/henke-introduces-new-generation-of-labeling-adhesives-for-glass-bottle.html)

**Casein films / edible packaging**
- USDA ARS (Peggy Tomasula, Laetitia Bonnaillie; ACS meeting 2016) made a casein film blocking oxygen up to 500× better than plastic film. Citrus pectin improved its strength and humidity resistance. It can be made from milk powder, wrapped like cling film or sprayed on. Launch was predicted "within three years", and I found no evidence it reached market — [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/innovation/heres-food-wrapper-you-can-eat-180960287/); [Biofuels Digest](https://www.biofuelsdigest.com/bdigest/?p=67287)

### Inferences
- Granularity: "Casein glue & paint" is one existing node. I suggest splitting it into **Casein glue** and **Casein paint**, with Milk paint and Casein tempera as children of Casein paint or siblings. Glue (lime + alkali, for wood) and paint (lime + pigment, for walls and furniture) are different products with different uses. If the graph must stay small, keep the combined node and add only "Beer-label glue" as its child.
- Ambiguous parents: traditional milk paint and whitewash used **whole or skimmed milk / buttermilk / curd** directly, not industrial casein. In the graph I would hang **Milk paint** off *Skimmed milk* (or *Acid curd*), and **Casein paint (industrial)** off *Acid casein*. Galalith is made from **Rennet casein** (rennet casein is the standard feed for casein plastic; acid casein is used for glues). That was not confirmed by a fetched source in this session, so it is flagged.
- Lanital and Aralac are both made from **Acid casein** made from **Skimmed milk**. Aralac and QMilch can be *trade-name notes on the Lanital node* ("Casein fibre: Lanital, Aralac, Merinova, QMilch"), unless the graph wants a modern node. QMilch is distinct in era and process, so it could be a separate leaf.
- Erinoid is a trade name of Galalith, so it is one node.

### Gaps
- No confirmation in this session that Galalith is specifically made from rennet casein rather than acid casein. This is standard dairy-technology knowledge, but no source was fetched for it.
- Whether casein was ever used for billiard balls: no source found. Billiard balls of the period were celluloid or other plastics, and I would not include casein without evidence.
- Precise date of the Mosquito's switch from casein to Aerolite: sources do not agree.

## Whey and lactose: non-food uses

### Takeaway
Whey's non-food fates are mostly *disposal-turned-product*. Sweet whey is a feed and a fermentation feedstock (ethanol, lactic acid → PLA). Acid whey from Greek yoghurt is spread on fields, fed in small amounts or digested to biogas. Lactose is the classic tablet filler (covered by the medical researcher). The most striking fact is that whey's cheese-making cousin, **cheese brine**, is sprayed on Wisconsin roads to melt ice.

### Cited Findings
- New York's environmental agency (DEC) lists the legal outlets for whey as farm-animal feed, land application as fertiliser, anaerobic digestion (usually followed by land application), wastewater treatment, and processing into supplements. Land application of whey has been regulated under Part 360 for more than 20 years — [NY DEC whey management](https://dec.ny.gov/environmental-protection/help-for-businesses/farms-agriculture/whey-management)
- Acid whey from Greek yoghurt: about 150 million gallons/yr in the US, about 4 gal milk per gallon of yoghurt. Cows dislike it because of its acidity, so only small amounts go into feed. Chobani sends most to farmers as feed, some to land application on farms with nutrient plans, and a little to digesters. It is not toxic, but its oxygen demand is harmful if dumped in streams — [Grist / Modern Farmer](https://grist.org/food/the-down-side-of-greek-yogurt-seas-of-fish-killing-toxic-byproduct/); [Food Dive](https://www.fooddive.com/news/chobani-and-dannon-address-acid-whey-concerns/134823)
- Anaerobic digestion of dairy waste and whey into biogas — [Waste Today](https://www.wastetodaymagazine.com/article/rew1014-anaerobic-digestion-dairy-biowaste)
- Capro-X "Whey Away" turns acid whey into bio-oils for specialty chemicals — [DairyReporter](https://www.dairyreporter.com/Article/2019/09/13/Capro-X-reimagines-Greek-yogurt-waste)
- Whey alcohol: Black Cow Vodka (Dorset, UK) ferments whey lactose with a special yeast into a "milk beer" and distils it. About 20 L of whey gives about 1 L of vodka — [Delicious magazine](https://www.deliciousmagazine.co.uk/?p=42442); [Agriland](https://www.agriland.co.uk/farming-news/video-british-farmer-makes-vodka-from-milk/). Note: that is a drink, so food; the non-food branch is industrial/fuel ethanol from whey permeate. Carbery (Ireland) is widely cited for it, but this session found no source for that, so it is a gap.
- Whey → lactic acid → PLA: whey lactose is fermented to lactic acid, purified, turned into oligomer, then lactide, then ring-opening polymerisation to PLA — [Horban et al. 2017 (UFV)](https://periodicos.ufv.br/jcec/article/download/2412/1732/16271). Acid whey is one of three case-study feedstocks for D-lactic acid (needed for heat-resistant PLA) — [Alexandri et al. 2019, Food Technol. Biotechnol.](https://doaj.org/article/4130a209e96b4ddfae75ab242fd63ac2). Whey permeate → lactic acid → PLA (thesis) — [Nicolau, U. Chile](https://repositorioslatinoamericanos.uchile.cl/handle/2250/4264407). The high price of lactic acid is what holds PLA back, and that is why cheap feedstocks like whey are attractive — via [Abdel-Rahman et al. 2013, as summarised](https://scitepress.org/PublishedPapers/2022/120038)
- **Cheese brine de-icer:** Polk County, Wisconsin has used waste brine from cheese-making (F&A Dairy, mozzarella/provolone) since 2009, as pre-wet on rock salt. It cut salt use 30–40% and saved $40,000 in the first year. Volumes are 30,000–38,000 gal/winter. Milwaukee ran a pilot. The idea is credited to Polk County highway commissioner Emil "Moe" Norby. The drawback is the smell — [NPR 2014](https://www.npr.org/sections/thetwo-way/2014/01/21/264562529/cheese-to-the-rescue-surprising-spray-melts-road-ice); [Discover](https://discovermagazine.com/environment/wisconsin-uses-cheese-brine-to-keep-roads-clear-of-ice); [WUWM](https://www.wuwm.com/environment/2013-12-10/to-tackle-icy-streets-milwaukee-experiments-with-cheese-brine)

### Inferences
- Cheese brine is **not whey**. It is the salt brine in which cheese (mozzarella, provolone, feta-type) is salted, so it picks up whey solids. Its parent should be a cheese node: *Brined cheese (feta)* or *Pasta filata (mozzarella)*, with the edge read as "used brine of". This is an unusual edge type ("by-product of" rather than "made from"). Flag it as ambiguous, but it is too good to omit.
- Suggested nodes: **Whey feed** (pig and cattle swill, from Sweet whey / Acid whey); **Whey fertiliser / land-spread whey** (from Acid whey); **Biogas** (from Acid whey or Whey permeate); **Whey ethanol** (from Whey permeate; Black Cow vodka as food note); **Lactic acid** (from Whey permeate or Lactose) → **PLA** (from Lactic acid). Lactic acid is generic; mark it as "conversion product".
- Pharmaceutical lactose: a note on the existing *Lactose* node; no new node in this scope.

### Gaps
- Whey protein films and coatings (rather than casein): no specific source fetched. The literature exists (WPI films), but nothing was cited here.
- Industrial fuel ethanol from whey permeate (Carbery, Ireland; plants in New Zealand): not verified this session.
- Whey for de-icing as such: nothing found. The de-icing product is *cheese brine*, not whey.

## Cosmetics and household

### Takeaway
The famous "Cleopatra's donkey-milk bath" has no ancient source. The ancient bather was **Poppaea Sabina** (Nero's wife), per Pliny. The Cleopatra version comes from 1930s Hollywood: Claudette Colbert played both Poppaea (1932, milk-bath scene) and Cleopatra (1934). Milk as **invisible ink** is ancient (Ovid). Milk soaps and cosmetics are real but are ingredient uses, not milk products.

### Cited Findings
- No ancient source shows Cleopatra bathing in milk. The ancient attribution is to Poppaea Sabina. The myth is traced to Claudette Colbert's roles as Poppaea (*The Sign of the Cross*, 1932, milk-bath scene) and Cleopatra (1934) merging in popular memory — [Ursin, *Thersites* 2021, "The mother of chemical peeling"](https://thersites-journal.de/index.php/thr/article/view/95); BASF's cosmetics portal likewise says it was "probably not Cleopatra" — [BASF Care Creations](https://www.carecreations.basf.com/core-competencies/face-care/trends-research/the-famous-donkey-milk-baths)
- Pliny's account of Poppaea keeping 500 she-asses that had recently foaled, milked for her bath, is quoted secondhand — [RomanArmyTalk thread](https://www.romanarmytalk.com/rat/showthread.php?tid=30336) (primary text not checked: Pliny, *Natural History* 11.96 / 28.50 — unverified reference)
- Ovid (*Ars Amatoria*): "Characters written in fresh milk are a well-known means of secret communication. Touch them with a little powdered charcoal and you will read them." So the message was developed with charcoal dust, not heat — [Science News, review of Macrakis, *Prisoners, Lovers, and Spies*](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/prisoners-lovers-and-spies-reveals-secrets-invisible-ink)
- The famous heat-developed prison letters (Jesuit John Gerard, Tower of London) used **orange juice**, not milk — [Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/prisoners-lovers-and-spies-reveals-secrets-invisible-ink)

### Inferences
- **Donkey-milk bath / milk bath:** one node "Milk bath" off *Milk*, with the myth note. The draw is the story. Process step: none (used as is), which strains the "one processing step" edge rule. Possibly better as an annotation than a node.
- **Milk soap** (goat's-milk soap): milk is a minor ingredient in a lye + fat recipe, so don't draw it, or draw it dashed. The same goes for "milk protein" shampoos and lotions (hydrolysed milk protein, lactic acid AHA peels).
- **Invisible ink** is a lovely leaf from *Milk* (the step: write with it and dust with charcoal). It is a use, not a product. Include it if the graph tolerates "use" leaves.

### Gaps
- Milk as a traditional cleaner or polish (for patent leather, silverware, piano keys, house plants' leaves): no source found this session.
- Photographic or darkroom uses of milk (casein in early photographic processes, "milk prints"): no source found. Unverified; do not include.
- No concrete market or ingredient data on milk soaps and cosmetics was gathered.

## Agricultural and biological uses

### Takeaway
Diluted milk is a scientifically tested **fungicide against powdery mildew** (Bettiol 1999: as good as synthetic fungicides on zucchini). Non-fat dry milk is the standard **"Blotto" blocking buffer** in molecular biology (1984). **Skim milk agar** is a classic protease test. **Calf milk replacer** went from skim-milk-based (1950s–60s) to mostly whey-based now. Milk proteins (casein, lactalbumin) are a staple of **carp-fishing boilies**.

### Cited Findings
- Bettiol, *Crop Protection* 18 (1999): fresh cow's milk sprayed at 5–50% on greenhouse zucchini against *Sphaerotheca fuliginea*. Twice-weekly sprays above 10% gave about 10% disease severity, versus 9% for fenarimol/benomyl and 56% for water. Weekly sprays needed 20–50%. Above 30%, harmless mould grew on the leaves. The mechanism is unclear (germicidal effect or a film on the leaf) — [ECHO summary](https://echocommunity.org/resources/3e8e70b3-685e-4edc-93c5-a5c489382a54); [Vegalab](https://www.vegalab.com/crop-science/knowledge/myths/milk-spray); [agrar.de](https://www.agrar.de/agenda/bettiol.htm)
- BLOTTO = "Bovine Lacto Transfer Technique Optimizer". Johnson, Gautsch, Sportsman & Elder, *Gene Anal. Tech.* 1:3–8 (1984): 5% non-fat dry milk in PBS (+ azide) blocks nitrocellulose blots better than BSA or gelatin, and cheaply. Caveats: phosphoproteins (casein) and biotin interfere with phospho-specific and avidin detection. It is still sold commercially (Rockland BLOTTO) — [Wikipedia: Blotto (biology)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blotto_(biology)); [Rockland](https://www.rockland.com/categories/supporting-reagents/blotto-immunoanalytical-grade-non-fat-dry-milk-B501-0500/)
- Skim milk agar: skim milk supplies casein, and protease-producing colonies (e.g. *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, *Bacillus*, *Serratia*) clear a halo around themselves — [HiMedia technical sheet](https://www.himedialabs.com/media/TD/M1745.pdf); [ASM image gallery](https://asm.org/image-gallery/casein-hydrolysis); [J Clin Pathol 1970](https://jcp.bmj.com/content/23/2/172)
- Calf milk replacer: in the 1950s–60s it was mostly skim milk powder (skim protein about 80% casein, 20% whey). Now it is mostly whey-based in the US and EU. A 1973 Idaho bulletin capped dried whey at 30–35%. Skim inclusion has fallen from about 60% to about 10% in some products — [Milk Specialties](https://www.milkspecialties.com/?p=5979); [U. Idaho bulletin](https://objects.lib.uidaho.edu/uiext/uiext20215.pdf); [Farmers Journal / Volac](https://farmersjournal.ie/volac-s-whey-based-milk-replacers-deliver-top-calf-performance-604757)
- Carp bait: commercial "milk protein" boilies use rennet casein, calcium caseinate, lactalbumin and WPC (e.g. RG Baits Vita-lac) — [Angling Direct](https://www.anglingdirect.co.uk/rg-baits-vita-lac-shelf-life-boilies); [Haith's](https://haiths.com/blogs/fishing/simple-or-complicated)

### Inferences
- **Milk spray (fungicide):** parent *Milk* (or *Skimmed milk*; skim and whey are also used in garden practice, but this session's source tested fresh whole milk); step "dilute 1:10 with water". Milk is the active material, so it qualifies as a milk product.
- **Blotto / blocking buffer:** parent *Skimmed milk powder*; step "dissolve at 5% in buffered saline". Milk is the defining material. ★
- **Skim milk agar:** parent *Skimmed milk powder* (or Skimmed milk); step "mix with nutrient agar". Milk is the defining substrate.
- **Calf milk replacer:** parents are *Skimmed milk powder* and *Whey powder*; step "blend with fat, vitamins". One node, two parents. It sits on the food–feed line.
- **Boilies:** milk proteins are a main but optional base, so drawing them is borderline. I'd leave them out or mark them as an "ingredient" leaf off Caseinate.

### Gaps
- Pig feeding on whey (historic Swiss and English cheese-dairy pig keeping) was not sourced in this session, though DEC lists feed as an outlet.
- Milk used in cell culture media beyond agar (e.g. litmus milk medium): not searched.

## Anything else surprising, and the full catalogue

### Takeaway
The best "surprise" leaves are cheese-brine road de-icing, Galalith's origin as a school blackboard, Lanital as a fascist autarky fibre, and Blotto. Casein billiard balls and milk in photography could not be confirmed and should be dropped.

### Cited Findings
- Galalith grew out of a search for a white, washable school "blackboard" — [Ganoksin](https://www.ganoksin.com/article/galalith-jewelry-milk-stone/); [Wikipedia: Galalith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galalith)
- Casein had industrial uses from the 19th century in adhesives, paints and paper/board coatings. They were largely replaced by petroleum products, though industrial casein production continues — [CAMEO: casein plastic](https://cameo.mfa.org/wiki/Casein_plastic)
- Cheese brine de-icer: see the whey section — [NPR](https://www.npr.org/sections/thetwo-way/2014/01/21/264562529/cheese-to-the-rescue-surprising-spray-melts-road-ice)
