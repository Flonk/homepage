# Where "milk product" ends: a defensible inclusion rule for the "what milk becomes" graph

## Codex Alimentarius CXS 206-1999 (General Standard for the Use of Dairy Terms): milk, milk product, composite milk product, and where the line falls

### Takeaway
Codex sets up a four-step ladder, and it is the cleanest boundary any authority offers. (1) **Milk** is a mammary secretion of *milking animals*. (2) A **milk product** is milk plus any processing. (3) A **composite milk product** is one where milk-derived matter is "an essential part in terms of quantity" and nothing non-milk replaces a milk constituent. (4) **Other foods** fall into two groups: those where milk is *characterising* may mention milk descriptively, and those where it is not may name it only in the ingredient list. Codex gives no general percentage. Individual standards set numbers instead, for example ≥40% fermented milk for "drinks based on fermented milk".

### Cited Findings
Verbatim text below is from the FAO/WHO *Milk and Milk Products* (2nd ed., 2011) compilation, pp. 176–179 — [FAO](https://www.fao.org/4/i2085e/i2085e00.pdf):
- **2.1 Milk** "is the normal mammary secretion of milking animals obtained from one or more milkings without either addition to it or extraction from it, intended for consumption as liquid milk or for further processing." The word "milking animals" leaves out human milk and cell-culture "milk". The word "normal" arguably leaves out colostrum (see the US section).
- **2.2 Milk product** "is a product obtained by any processing of milk, which may contain food additives, and other ingredients functionally necessary for the processing." This is why rennet, cultures, salt and emulsifying salts do not disqualify a product.
- **2.3 Composite milk product** "is a product of which the milk, milk products or milk constituents are an essential part in terms of quantity in the final product, as consumed provided that the constituents not derived from milk are not intended to take the place in part or in whole of any milk constituent."
- **2.4 Reconstituted milk product**: made by adding water back to the dried or concentrated form "to re-establish the appropriate water to solids ratio". **2.5 Recombined milk product**: made by combining "milkfat and milk-solids-non-fat in their preserved forms with or without the addition of water". Under 4.4, both may carry the normal product name if the consumer is not misled. They count as milk products.
- **4.2.2 / 4.3.3**: milk or milk products "modified … by the addition and/or withdrawal of milk constituents" keep the name, with a description of the modification. Standardised, skimmed, protein-adjusted and similar products therefore stay inside.
- **4.5**: a composite milk product may use "milk" or a milk-product name together with "a clear description of the other characterizing ingredient(s) (such as flavouring foods, spices, herbs and flavours)".
- **4.6.2**: the traditional-usage exception covers names whose "exact nature … is clear from traditional usage", which is the escape hatch for names like coconut milk and peanut butter.
- **4.6.4**: some products that are *not* composite milk products still "contain milk … which are an essential part in terms of characterization". For these, "milk" may appear only in the description of the true nature of the product, and only if non-milk constituents do not replace milk constituents. Then: "If however the final product is intended to substitute milk, a milk product or composite milk product, dairy terms shall not be used." For products where milk is "not an essential part in terms of characterization", dairy terms "can only be used in the list of ingredients".
- **Section 2 footnote**: "This Standard replaced the Code of Principles Concerning Milk and Milk Products." This is the main historical shift. The older Code of Principles dated from the 1950s–60s Codex/FAO dairy programme; the date is not verified here.
- A numeric threshold exists only inside specific standards. CXS 243-2003 §2.4: "Drinks based on Fermented Milk are composite milk products, as defined in Section 2.3 of [CXS 206] … contain a minimum of 40% (m/m) fermented milk." CXS 243 also names flavoured fermented milks (with fruit, cereals, honey, chocolate, nuts, coffee, spices) as composite products — [FAO compilation](https://www.fao.org/4/i2085e/i2085e00.pdf).
- Codex groups certain blends in its milk compilation but treats them as their own category: "Blend of evaporated skimmed milk and vegetable fat" (CXS 250-2006), "Blend of skimmed milk and vegetable fat in powdered form" (CXS 251), and "Blend of sweetened condensed skimmed milk and vegetable fat" (CXS 252). Vegetable fat replaces milkfat, so these fail the 2.3 proviso. They are milk-based but not milk products — [FAO compilation, contents](https://www.fao.org/4/i2085e/i2085e00.pdf).
- Codex standards whose subject is a single milk constituent are milk products in their own right: Whey powders (CXS 289: "Whey powders are milk products obtained by drying whey or acid whey"), Edible casein products (CXS 290), and Milkfat products (CXS 280) — [FAO compilation](https://www.fao.org/4/i2085e/i2085e00.pdf).
- India's FSSAI adopts the composite concept and gives examples such as fruit shrikhand and flavoured fermented milks — [FSSAI Dairy chapter 2.1](https://www.fssai.gov.in/upload/uploadfiles/files/2_%20Chapter%202_1%20(Dairy%20products%20and%20analogues).pdf) (from the search snippet; not fetched in full).

### Inferences
- The two Codex tests that are usable for a graph are: (a) **essential part, by quantity, as consumed**, and (b) **no replacement of a milk constituent**. Test (b) is binary and easy to apply. Test (a) needs a working threshold from the author. "Milk-derived matter is the largest single component, or ≥ ~40% of the product" is defensible because 40% is the only general-purpose number Codex itself uses.
- Codex sorts products into three bins that the graph can reuse: milk product → composite milk product → "contains milk, characterising" (4.6.4) → "contains milk" (ingredient list only). A strict graph keeps the first two. A generous graph could add the third as dashed or "fate" edges.

### Gaps
- I could not access the current Codex page for CXS 206 (fao.org returned 403). The text quoted is the 2011 compilation. I found no evidence of a later amendment to sections 2–4, but I did not verify this.
- The Codex standard has no general numeric definition of "essential part in terms of quantity".

## EU: Regulation 1308/2013 Annex VII Part III, Decision 2010/791/EU, and CJEU C-422/16 TofuTown (2017)

### Takeaway
The EU copies the Codex architecture with one notable change. A composite product qualifies if milk is essential "either in terms of quantity **or for characterisation**", which is looser than Codex 2.3. Its reserved list explicitly names **caseins**, whey, butteroil and AMF as milk products. TofuTown confirms that "milk" needs an animal origin and that adding "soya"/"plant" does not fix a plant product's name. Decision 2010/791 lists the traditional exceptions (coconut milk, peanut butter, cocoa butter, cream crackers, ice-cream, etc.). These are naming exceptions, not dairy classifications.

### Cited Findings
- CJEU C-422/16 (14 June 2017), para 22: Annex VII Part III point 1 defines milk as "exclusively the normal mammary secretion obtained from one or more milkings"; para 23: milk is "an animal product", so the term cannot in principle designate a purely plant-based product — [EUR-Lex, judgment](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62016CJ0422).
- Para 24: point 1(b) permits modifications "restricted to an addition and/or withdrawal of natural milk constituents", which excludes total replacement by a plant product — [EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62016CJ0422).
- Para 25: point 2 defines milk products as "products derived exclusively from milk", and substances needed for manufacture may be added only if they do not replace any milk constituent. Whey, cream, butter, buttermilk, cheese, yogurt and similar names are reserved for milk products — [EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62016CJ0422).
- Para 32: point 3 (composite products) does not apply to plant products because they contain no milk or milk products — [EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62016CJ0422).
- Operative part: the regulation precludes "the term 'milk' and the designations reserved … exclusively for milk products" for purely plant-based products "even if those terms are expanded upon by clarifying or descriptive terms indicating the plant origin", "unless that product is listed in Annex I to Commission Decision 2010/791/EU". Soya/tofu are not on the list. Listing "crème de riz" (French) does not extend to "rice cream" — [EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62016CJ0422).
- Decision 2010/791/EU, Annex I, English entries: Coconut milk; "Cream …" or "Milk …" for spirituous beverages (cream sherry, milk sherry); Cream soda; Cream-filled biscuits (custard cream, bourbon cream); Cream-filled sweets or chocolates (peppermint cream, crème egg); Cream crackers; Salad cream; Creamed coconut and similar fruit/nut/vegetable products; Cream of tartar; Cream/creamed soups; Horseradish cream; **Ice-cream**; Jelly cream; Table cream; Cocoa butter; Shea butter; Nut butters (peanut butter); Butter beans; Butter puffs; Fruit cheese (lemon cheese, Damson cheese). Leberkäse appears in the German list and crème de riz in the French list. The decision was adopted under point III(1) of Annex XII of the predecessor Regulation 1234/2007 — [EUR-Lex 32010D0791](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32010D0791).
- Point 3 of Annex VII Part III, which covers composite products, and the full point 2(a) reserved list (whey, cream, butter, buttermilk, butteroil, caseins, anhydrous milk fat (AMF), cheese, yogurt, kephir, koumiss, viili/fil, smetana, fil, rjaženka, rūgušpiens) could not be fetched; the EUR-Lex consolidated text did not load the annexes. The wording given in the Takeaway ("essential part either in terms of quantity or for characterisation") is my recollection of the regulation text and should be checked against [Regulation 1308/2013 on EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2013/1308/oj/eng) before quoting.

### Inferences
- "Ice-cream" being on the exceptions list means that in the UK/Ireland English list the *name* is not reserved for dairy. Ice cream is not thereby a non-dairy product, but EU law does not guarantee that "ice cream" means dairy. The graph should include dairy ice cream on composition grounds, not because of the name.
- The exceptions list is a ready-made "false friends" list. Every entry on it is OUT of the graph (coconut milk, cocoa butter, peanut butter, cream of tartar, cream soda, butter beans, fruit cheese). Milk sherry and cream sherry contain no milk.
- The EU reserved list puts **caseins** inside "milk products". HS customs puts casein in Chapter 35. For a "what milk becomes" graph, the food-law view (casein is a milk product) is the right one.

### Gaps
- Annex VII Part III verbatim text was not retrieved (EUR-Lex annex section did not load).
- I did not research the status of later EU proposals on reserving dairy or meat terms (e.g. the 2020 "Amendment 171", or 2025–26 CMO amendments).

## US: FDA standards of identity (21 CFR 131/133/135) and the HS tariff boundary (Ch. 4 vs 17/18/19/21/35/39)

### Takeaway
The FDA definition of milk is narrower than Codex. It is limited to **cows** and is "practically free from colostrum". Other species' milks and colostrum are therefore outside the FDA "milk" standard. The HS tariff draws a tidy chapter boundary, but it is a customs convenience. It moves lactose (>95%), whey protein >80%, casein, ice cream, infant formula and all "replaced constituent" products **out** of Chapter 4. That cuts too much for a "what milk becomes" graph. It works better as a cross-check for what counts as a product that *contains* milk.

### Cited Findings
- 21 CFR 131.110(a): milk is "the lacteal secretion, practically free from colostrum, obtained by the complete milking of one or more healthy cows". Beverage milk must be pasteurized or ultrapasteurized and contain ≥8¼% milk solids not fat and ≥3¼% milkfat. Fat separation, adding cream or concentrated/dried milk, and homogenization are permitted — [Cornell LII 21 CFR 131.110](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/131.110); [eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-131/subpart-B/section-131.110) (current as of 20 July 2026 per the search result).
- 21 CFR 1210.3, a different rule from the Federal Import Milk Act regulations, excludes milk obtained within 15 days before and 5 days after calving — [search result citing eCFR/Vermont testimony](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2020/WorkGroups/House%20Agriculture/Bills/H.81%20definition%20and%20sale%20of%20milk/Witness%20Testimony/W~E.B.%20Flory~Information%20on%20the%20definition%20of%20milk~2-13-2019.pdf) (snippet only).
- A US Customs ruling (HQ 964528, revoking NY B85847 on "Bovine Dried Colostrum") relied on the FDA milk definition to conclude that colostrum is not the product commonly sold as milk — [TariffLens listing](https://www.tarifflens.ai/rulings/964528) (search snippet only; page returned 403, so the final heading is unverified).
- FDA plant-based milk labelling: the Feb 2023 draft guidance, "Labeling of Plant-based Milk Alternatives and Voluntary Nutrient Statements", would let plant beverages use "milk" with the plant source named (e.g. "soy milk") plus voluntary nutrient statements. A further January 2025 draft covers plant-based alternatives to animal foods generally. I found no evidence that either was finalised as of 2026 — [FDA draft guidance PDF](https://fda.gov/media/165420/download); [Bureau Veritas](https://www.cps.bureauveritas.com/newsroom/us-fda-issues-draft-plant-based-food-labeling-guidance). The US therefore tolerates "almond milk" as a *name* without treating it as a dairy product.
- **HS Chapter 4 notes** (Schedule B 2017 rendering of the HS):
  - Note 1: "The expression 'milk' means full cream milk or partially or completely skimmed milk."
  - Note 2: butter = natural, whey or recombined butter "derived exclusively from milk", 80–95% milkfat. Dairy spreads have milkfat as the only fat, between 39% and less than 80%.
  - Note 3: whey concentrates with added milk or milkfat are cheese (0406) if milkfat is ≥5% of dry matter, dry matter is 70–85%, and the product is moulded or mouldable.
  - **Note 4 exclusions**: (a) whey products with >95% lactose (anhydrous, dry basis) → **17.02**; (b) "products obtained by replacing one or more natural constituents of milk (e.g., butyric fats) by another substance (e.g., oleic fats)" → **19.01 or 21.06**; (c) albumins including whey-protein concentrates with >80% whey protein (dry basis) → **35.02**, and globulins → **35.04**.
  - Headings: 0401 milk and cream (not concentrated or sweetened); 0402 concentrated or sweetened; 0403 buttermilk, yogurt, kephir, fermented or acidified milk; 0404 whey and "products consisting of natural milk constituents, n.e.s."; 0405 butter, milk fats, dairy spreads (ghee goes in 0405.90); 0406 cheese and curd.
  - Source: [US Census Schedule B Ch. 4](https://www.census.gov/foreign-trade/schedules/b/2017/c04.html).
- Note 4(b) is the HS twin of the Codex "no replacement" proviso. Filled milk, vegetable-fat creams and cheese analogues leave dairy for Ch. 19/21 — [Census Ch. 4](https://www.census.gov/foreign-trade/schedules/b/2017/c04.html).
- **Lactose**: heading 1702 covers "Other sugars, including chemically pure lactose …". 1702.11 is for ≥99% lactose (anhydrous, dry basis) — [Census Ch. 17](https://www.census.gov/foreign-trade/schedules/b/2017/c17.html).
- **Ch. 19**: 1901 includes food preparations of goods of headings 04.01–04.04 (with cocoa limits). 1901.10 is "Preparations suitable for infants or young children, put up for retail sale" (infant formula). 1901.90 includes "Malted milk; articles of milk or cream not specially provided for" — [Census Ch. 19](https://www.census.gov/foreign-trade/schedules/b/2017/c19.html).
- **Ch. 21**: 2105 "Ice cream and other edible ice, whether or not containing cocoa". 2106 includes "Non-dairy coffee whiteners" and "Other cream or milk substitutes" — [Census Ch. 21](https://www.census.gov/foreign-trade/schedules/b/2017/c21.html).
- **Ch. 35**: 3501 covers "Casein, caseinates and other casein derivatives; casein glues" (3501.10 casein; 3501.90 other, with casein glues at US 3501.90.20). The US also places milk protein concentrate at 3501.10.10 — [USITC Ch. 35 archive](https://usitc.gov/tata/hts/archive/0000/000c35.pdf); [Tandom 3501.90](https://tariffs.tandom.ai/hts-catalog/3501.90/other) (third-party).
- **Ch. 39**: 3913 covers "natural polymers … and modified natural polymers (for example, hardened proteins …)", which is where hardened casein (Galalith) goes. A Spanish-language Ch. 4 text lists "hardened casein (heading 39.13)" among Ch. 4 exclusions — [Flexport HS 3913](https://www.flexport.com/data/hs-code/3913-natural-polymers-for-example-alginic-acid-and-modified-natural-polymers-for-example-hardened-proteins-chemical-derivatives-of-natural-rubber-not-elsew/); [El Salvador Ch. 4 PDF](https://transparencia.mh.gob.sv/downloads/pdf/DC5429_CAP%2004%205a%20Enm-AFEC.pdf) (search snippet).

### Inferences
- HS reads as an ordered sort: **Ch. 4** = milk or milk constituents still in their natural form → **Ch. 17/35/39** = an isolated milk constituent treated as a commodity (sugar, protein, plastic) → **Ch. 18/19/21/22** = preparations where milk is one ingredient. For the graph, Ch. 4, Ch. 17 lactose, Ch. 35 casein/whey protein and Ch. 39 Galalith are all "made from milk, defining substance is a milk constituent" (IN). Ch. 18/19/21/22 products need the composite test.
- A rule based only on HS would drop lactose, casein, WPI, ice cream and processed-cheese-like preparations. That is clearly too narrow for "what milk becomes", so HS should not be the rule.
- The FDA's cow-only definition would drop goat and sheep cheese. Use the Codex "milking animals" root instead.

- The WCO Explanatory Notes for 3501/3913/1806/2208 were not retrieved. HS placement of cream liqueur (2208) and milk chocolate (1806) is my own knowledge, not fetched.
- The final heading in the colostrum customs ruling is unverified.

## Dairy-science textbooks (Walstra; Fox & McSweeney): products vs ingredients vs by-products

### Takeaway
Classic dairy-technology texts treat as "milk products" the things made by *separating, concentrating, fermenting or coagulating* milk: liquid milk, cream, butter, concentrated milks, powders, protein preparations, fermented milks and cheese. The 2nd-edition Walstra did not include ice cream or infant formula. The 2025 3rd edition reportedly adds them, which shows the field's boundary widening toward composite and nutritional products. Chemistry texts (Fox et al.) organise by constituent (lactose, proteins, fat, salts), which supports including isolated constituents and their derivatives.

### Cited Findings
- Walstra, Wouters & Geurts, *Dairy Science and Technology* 2nd ed. (2006) product chapters: "Milk for Liquid Consumption; 17. Cream Products; 18. Butter; 19. Concentrated Milks; 20. Milk Powder; 21. Protein Preparations; 22. Fermented Milks", then cheese. There is no ice cream chapter — [BL catalogue](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/018426794); [WUR record](https://research.wur.nl/en/publications/dairy-science-and-technology-second-edition/).
- Walstra et al., *Dairy Technology* (1999), covers a similar list: milk for liquid consumption, cream products, concentrated milks, milk powder, protein preparations, butter, fermented milks, cheese — [BL catalogue](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/018860370).
- A 3rd edition listing (2025, Huppertz et al.) reportedly adds "10 new chapters" including milk in the dairy chain, primary milk production, **ice cream**, **infant formula products** and **medical nutrition products** — [Bookshare listing](https://bookshare.org/browse/book/6477163) (single source; unverified in detail).
- The University of Guelph's open *Dairy Science and Technology* e-book has an ice cream chapter — [Guelph](https://books.lib.uoguelph.ca/dairyscienceandtechnologyebook/chapter/ice-cream/).
- Fox, Uniacke-Lowe, McSweeney & O'Mahony, *Dairy Chemistry and Biochemistry* 2nd ed. (2015). Chapters begin with production and utilisation of milk, then lactose, lipids, proteins and salts, covering the chemistry of cheese, fermented milks and dairy processing — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/978-3-319-14892-2).

### Inferences
- The textbook core (separate, concentrate, dry, ferment, coagulate, churn) matches Codex 2.2 "milk product". Ice cream and infant formula are the classic frontier: added to the 3rd ed. Walstra, but in HS 21/19 and (infant formula) built on vegetable fat.
- "Protein preparations" (casein, caseinates, WPC/WPI, MPC) are core dairy products in the textbooks even though HS moves them out. This supports including casein, and therefore casein glue and Galalith, as made-from-milk nodes.

- I found no IDF formal definition document distinct from Codex. IDF largely drives Codex dairy standards, but this is not verified here.

## Precision-fermentation ("animal-free") dairy proteins, lab-grown milk, human milk, colostrum: in or out of "milk"?

### Takeaway
No legal system classifies precision-fermentation whey or casein as "milk" or a "milk product". It is a novel-food or GRAS ingredient made by a fungus. Every legal definition (Codex "milking animals", EU "mammary secretion obtained from … milkings", FDA "complete milking of … healthy cows") excludes it, and the same wording excludes cell-cultured "milk". These products are best drawn as **analogues pointing at** milk nodes, not as nodes made from milk.

### Cited Findings
- FDA GRN 000863 (Perfect Day, 2020): β-lactoglobulin "produced by Trichoderma reesei" for use as a protein source at up to 35%. FDA had "no questions" on its GRAS conclusion. It is not intended for infant formula. FDA said that "β-lactoglobulin" is not its recommendation as a common or usual name — [FDA letter](https://fda.gov/media/136751/download); [GRAS notice](https://fda.gov/media/136754/download); [FoodNavigator-USA](https://www.foodnavigator-usa.com/Article/2020/04/14/Perfect-Day-secures-no-objections-letter-from-FDA-for-non-animal-whey-protein).
- Press coverage reports that FDA said the protein needs the same allergen labelling as milk. I could not verify the exact letter wording — [The Spoon](https://thespoon.tech/fda-approves-perfect-days-animal-free-whey-protein-as-safe-to-eat/).
- Imagindairy (precision-fermented whey) also received an FDA GRAS "no questions" letter (Jan 2024) — [FoodNavigator](https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2024/01/17/imagindairy-receives-gras-nod-from-fda-acquires-industrial-scale-precision-fermentation-lines/).
- EU: EFSA's 2022 novel-food opinion on β-lactoglobulin concerns BLG *isolated from bovine whey*, not fermentation-derived BLG — [EFSA Journal via PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8990528/). The UK ACNFP first reviewed Perfect Day's *fermentation* BLG (GM T. reesei) in June 2025 — [ACNFP](https://acnfp.food.gov.uk/print/pdf/node/18391). I found no EU authorisation of a precision-fermentation dairy protein, and no EU ruling on dairy terms for one.
- Lawsuit: organic and anti-GMO groups sued Perfect Day over "animal-free dairy" claims — [AgFunderNews](https://agfundernews.com/perfect-day-hit-with-lawsuit-from-organic-anti-gmo-groups-over-animal-free-dairy-claims).
- Definitions that exclude non-animal or non-milking sources: Codex 2.1 "milking animals" — [FAO](https://www.fao.org/4/i2085e/i2085e00.pdf); EU via TofuTown para 22–23 ("animal product") — [EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62016CJ0422); FDA "healthy cows" — [LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/131.110).

### Inferences
- Precision-fermentation BLG is molecularly identical to a milk protein and is allergenic like milk, but it has no "made from" edge to milk. In a "made from" graph it fails test 1 (origin). Show it at most as a side-note or a dashed "imitates" link.
- Cell-cultured milk (mammary cells in a bioreactor) is closer in origin but is not "obtained by milking". It is OUT of the legal definitions; at most a dashed analogue.
- Human donor milk is biologically milk. Codex ("milking animals") and FDA ("cows") exclude it from the *dairy* definition. It should be a separate root, not a child of cow milk.
- Colostrum: FDA excludes it from milk, Codex's word "normal" arguably excludes it, and 21 CFR 1210.3 excludes the peri-calving window. Hyperimmune colostrum is therefore OUT of a strict "milk" tree. If the author wants it, it belongs under a sibling root "colostrum", not under "milk".

### Gaps
- I did not establish 2026 EU novel-food status for any fermentation-derived dairy protein. Treat as unverified.
- I found no source on regulatory status of cell-cultured milk (e.g. Biomilq, Wilk, Brown Foods).

## Proposed inclusion rule for the graph, with 30+ worked edge cases

### Takeaway
**Proposed rule (Codex 2.2/2.3, extended to non-food):** A thing is a node if it comes from milk or from another node by processing (origin), its defining substance is milk-derived (milk-derived matter is the main component by quantity, or the component without which it would not be that product), and nothing non-milk stands in for a milk constituent (no substitution). Products that merely have milk among their ingredients are out. So are commodity chemicals that would be identical whatever their feedstock (ethanol, lactic acid, PLA, biogas). The author may show these as dashed "fate" leaves, not as nodes. The reasoning: origin follows Codex 2.1 and TofuTown; substance follows Codex 2.3 and the HS Ch. 4 vs 19/21 split; no-substitution follows Codex 2.3, EU point 2 and HS Note 4(b); and the feedstock test explains why lactose, lactulose, casein and Galalith stay in while whey ethanol and PLA go out.

### Cited Findings
- The rule components map to the sources above: origin from Codex 2.1, EU point 1 and TofuTown paras 22–24; "defining substance by quantity" from Codex 2.3 and CXS 243's 40% figure; "no substitution" from Codex 2.3, 4.6.4, EU point 2 and HS Ch. 4 Note 4(b); "ingredient-list only" from Codex 4.6.4 last paragraph — [FAO](https://www.fao.org/4/i2085e/i2085e00.pdf); [EUR-Lex TofuTown](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62016CJ0422); [Census Ch. 4](https://www.census.gov/foreign-trade/schedules/b/2017/c04.html).

### Inferences — the four tests as a checklist
1. **Origin**: does a real "made from" chain lead back to milk from milking animals? If not, OUT (plant milks, coconut milk, milk of magnesia, precision-fermentation protein, cell-cultured milk).
2. **Substance**: is milk-derived matter the bulk (largest component, or roughly ≥40% as consumed), or the thing that makes the product what it is (casein in glue, lactose as a sugar)? If not, OUT (butter cookies, milk chocolate, milk bread).
3. **No substitution**: does anything non-milk replace milkfat, milk protein or lactose? If yes, OUT (infant formula with vegetable oil, filled milk, non-dairy creamer, cheese analogue).
4. **Feedstock-specific** (non-food and chemical nodes only): would the output be identical if made from corn or sugar? If yes, OUT or dashed (ethanol, lactic acid, PLA, biogas). If the output exists only because milk does (lactose, lactulose, GOS, casein plastics), IN.

### Gaps
- The ~40% "essential by quantity" working threshold is my extrapolation from CXS 243. No authority sets a general figure.
- Recipe-dependent items (kishk, cheesecake, cream liqueur, cheese sauce) need a stated reference recipe. I found no compositional surveys for them.
- The EU milk-chocolate minimum milk solids (Directive 2000/36/EC) and the US ice-cream standard (21 CFR 135.110) were not fetched in this session.
