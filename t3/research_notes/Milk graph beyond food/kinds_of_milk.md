# Kinds of milk: source milks and sub-milks for the "what milk becomes" graph

Scope: kinds of milk that exist *before* processing begins. They split by lactation stage, by fraction within one milking, by species, and by non-mammal "milks". The graph currently has one root "Milk" plus a sibling root "Colostrum".

Composition figures are % (g/100 g) unless stated. They vary a lot by breed, season and stage, so read them as typical values. A review of minor dairy species found between-breed differences of up to 4 g/100 g in total fat within buffalo, yak, mare and camel milk ([Medhammar et al. 2012, via FAO AGRIS](https://agris.fao.org/search/ar/records/65df3fa5b766d82b1800de9d)).

## 1. Lactation stages: colostrum, transitional, mature, late-lactation, witch's milk

### Takeaway
Colostrum is the only lactation stage that has its own traditional products, so it is the only one that earns a node. Transitional milk is a gradient between colostrum and mature milk, and I found no products made specifically from it. Late-lactation ("stripper") milk mattered to old butter-makers, but only as a quality worry. Witch's milk is a medical curiosity and belongs in a note.

### Cited Findings
- Human milk: colostrum is "a thin yellowish fluid" rich in protein and antibodies, made in the first few days, and it changes gradually into mature milk. Early milk is thin and very sweet; later milk is thicker and creamier ([Wikipedia: Breast milk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breast_milk)).
- Mature human milk is about 4.5% fat, 0.8–0.9% protein, 7.1% carbohydrate (mostly lactose) and 0.2% ash. Per 100 mL that is 3.2–4.8 g fat, 0.8–1.4 g protein, 6.7–7.8 g carbohydrate ([Wikipedia: Breast milk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breast_milk)).
- Pig colostrum is similar to cow colostrum in protein, fat and lactose ([Wikipedia: Pig milk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pig_milk)).
- Late lactation: in 1897 Iowa researchers tested a common belief among butter-makers that some milk from "fresh" cows is needed for good butter flavour. They compared butter from "stripper" cows (milked six months or more) with butter from fresh cows ([Iowa State repository](https://dr.lib.iastate.edu/entities/publication/03bf11bb-f85b-465d-bf61-74f4c953c3e2)). Note that "stripper" here means a cow late in lactation, which is different from "strippings" in Section 2.
- **Witch's milk** (neonatal galactorrhoea) is milk secreted from the breasts of newborns of either sex. It affects about 5–6% of infants aged 0–12 weeks, mostly in the first two weeks, and was not seen in infants born before 34 weeks. It is linked to high prolactin and to exposure to the mother's hormones. The name comes from a 17th-century European belief that witches' familiars fed on it. It has no use, and squeezing the breasts risks mastitis ([Wikipedia: Witch's milk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witch%27s_milk); the page carries a notice that parts may be AI-generated).

### Inferences
- **Node judgement:** keep "Colostrum" as its own root. Transitional milk belongs in a note on the Colostrum→Milk edge ("the change takes days"), because nothing is made from it specifically. Late-lactation milk is also a note: it is richer and saltier, and historically it was blamed for poor butter. Witch's milk is a note, and it is one of the more striking ones for a general reader (milk from a newborn boy or girl).
- From general knowledge, not verified this session: colostrum products include beestings pudding (UK), *junnu/ginna/kharvas* (India) and Scandinavian colostrum "cheese" baked in the oven. These are colostrum children, which the existing root already covers.

### Gaps
- I found no source giving human "transitional milk" day boundaries (commonly said to be about day 5 to two weeks). This needs a lactation-physiology source.
- I found no product made specifically from transitional milk.

## 2. Within-milking fractions: foremilk, hindmilk, strippings (Nachgemelk)

### Takeaway
Fat rises steadily through a single milking. The last milk ("strippings") can hold four to five times the fat of the first. Irish farmers kept the strippings apart for butter, so this was a real practice, but it is a fat gradient rather than a different milk. It belongs as a note on the butter/cream path, not as a node.

### Cited Findings
- A 1910 New Zealand dairy article: the first milk drawn may test as low as 1.2–1.5% fat, and the last pint as much as 6–7%. Leaving out the last portion lowers the fat test ([Papers Past, 1910](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/CL19101220.2.35)).
- Irish folklore record (Schools' Collection): the first portion went to the calf and the rest was kept for butter. The last milk, called strippings, "is the richest in cream or butter-fat" ([dúchas.ie](https://www.duchas.ie/en/cbes/4427966/4361893/4466924)).
- Slovenian 1993 study: strippings averaged 0.73 kg per cow per milking, or 8.2% of total milk. Machine stripping raised the fat of the total milk from 3.67% to 3.81% ([Hrčak](https://hrcak.srce.hr/index.php/hr/clanak/140509)).
- Human milk: the article says the old foremilk/hindmilk split is misleading because there are not two kinds of milk; fat simply rises during a feed ([Wikipedia: Breast milk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breast_milk)).

### Inferences
- **Node judgement:** do not make foremilk, hindmilk or strippings nodes. A one-line note on Milk→Cream or Milk→Butter works well, e.g. "the last cupful of a milking is 4–5× fattier; Irish farmers saved the strippings for butter."
- Strippings were also a classic fraud: selling the low-fat fore-milk and keeping the strippings. That explains why the 1910 article talks about the fat "test". This is my own inference.

### Gaps
- I found no German-language source for a specific *Nachgemelk* tradition, such as Alpine cheeses made from strippings. Searching for Nachgemelk, Abstreifmilch or Stripp-Milch in German dairy history is the next step.
- I did not confirm any named cheese made specifically from strippings.

## 3. Species milks: composition and the products that depend on them

### Takeaway
Species is the axis that really splits the graph. Some products cannot be made, or by law may not be made, from cow's milk: mozzarella di bufala (buffalo only, by law), kumis/airag (mare), Pule (donkey + goat), moose cheese, reindeer cheese, chhurpi (yak, among others). Camel and donkey milk resist ordinary rennet because they have little kappa-casein, which shows that "cheese" depends on the milk as well as the method.

### Cited Findings — per species

**Cow** (*Bos taurus*), the default "Milk" root. Breed changes the fat level: Jersey milk is about 4.7–4.8% fat against about 3.7% for Holstein (lay and breed-society sources only) ([Guernsey cattle society](https://guernseycattle.com/?p=6); [washtenawcd page](https://water.washtenawcd.gov/is-jersey-milk-whole-milk.html)).

**Water buffalo** (*Bubalus bubalis*; Italian *bufala*, Hindi *bhains*). Very rich, ~10% fat. Mozzarella di Bufala Campana DOP must be made exclusively from whole fresh buffalo milk of the Italian Mediterranean breed, processed within 60 hours of the first milking ([DOP summary, gob.mx PDF](https://www.gob.mx/cms/uploads/attachment/file/245697/IT_Mozzarella_di_Bufala_Campana.pdf); [Consorzio](https://www.mozzarelladop.it/bufala-campana/manufacture)). In 2017 Campania protested when a cow's-milk mozzarella from Puglia got protected status: "the only DOP mozzarella is — and must remain — ours, exclusively made with buffalo milk" ([The Local](https://www.thelocal.com/20171229/cheesed-off-italian-regions-highly-strung-over-mozzarella)).

**Camel** (*Camelus dromedarius*, *C. bactrianus*; Arabic *haleeb naga*, Kazakh *shubat* for the fermented form). Similar to cow milk in gross composition, but it **does not set well with rennet**. Kappa-casein is about 3–4% of casein in camel milk against 13–15% in cow milk. Camel κ-casein has a different cleavage site, and the micelles are larger (~380 nm, about twice bovine). Bovine chymosin gives a weak curd, or flakes with no firm coagulum ([Hrčak review](https://hrcak.srce.hr/279662); [FAO](https://www.fao.org/4/t0755e/t0755e02.htm); [PubMed 25726113](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25726113)). The fix is camel chymosin. Chr. Hansen's FAR-M is fermentation-produced camel chymosin with about 2× the clotting activity of bovine chymosin on bovine milk and a 7× higher clotting-to-proteolysis ratio. It also clots donkey milk, which was previously "considered impossible to coagulate" ([DairyReporter 2015](https://www.dairyreporter.com/Article/2015/04/02/FAR-M-camel-milk-coagulant-clots-donkey-milk-too-says-Chr-Hansen/)). "Camelbert" appears only as a photo caption in that article. I found no process details.

**Mare** (*Equus caballus*). Low fat (1.6) and protein (2.7), high lactose (6.1). This high sugar is why it is fermented into a drink rather than curdled into cheese: **kumis** / *airag* (Mongolia, also *tsegee*) / *qımız* (Turkic). Kumis is 0.7–2.5% alcohol, can be freeze-concentrated, and is distilled into *arkhi*. Mare's milk has more sugar than cow's or goat's milk, so it reaches more alcohol than kefir. Industrial kumis uses cow's milk fortified with sucrose or modified whey to imitate mare's milk ([Wikipedia: Kumis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kumis)).

**Donkey/jenny** (*Equus asinus*). Very low fat (1.2), protein 1.7, lactose 6.9 ([Medhammar 2012](https://agris.fao.org/search/ar/records/65df3fa5b766d82b1800de9d)). **Pule** cheese from the Zasavica reserve, Serbia (founded 1997 by Slobodan Simić to protect Balkan donkeys) is about 60% donkey and 40% goat milk, aged about a month. About 25 L of donkey milk makes 1 kg of cheese. A jenny gives 1.5–2 L/day. Reported prices run from about $600 to over $1,000/lb (press figures) ([Port News](https://www.portnews.com.au/story/4102896/serbian-donkeys-cheese-costs-a-mozza/); [Food Republic](https://www.foodrepublic.com/1636294/worlds-most-expensive-cheese-pule-donkey); [Atlas Obscura](https://assets.atlasobscura.com/foods/pule-donkey-cheese)).

**Yak and dri** (*Bos grunniens*; "yak" strictly means the male, and the milking female is the *dri*/*'bri*). About 7% fat, 5.2% protein. Products include **chhurpi** (Himalayan hard chhurpi is a very low-moisture milk-protein concentrate; whole milk is the *least* preferred, because the fat goes to butter first), yak butter and butter tea ([NBU thesis ch. 2](https://ir.nbu.ac.in/bitstream/123456789/1085/6/06_chapter_02.pdf); [ILRI yak](https://agtr.ilri.org/sites/all/files/library/docs/yakpro/SessionG2.html); [Cheese Professor](https://www.cheeseprofessor.com/blog/yak-milk-cheese-nepal)). Mithun milk is also made into chhurpi in Arunachal Pradesh ([Scroll.in](https://scroll.in/article/1083595/arunachal-pradesh-mithun-milk-churpi-could-improve-farmer-incomes)).

**Reindeer** (*Rangifer tarandus*; Sámi). Milk is ~22% fat / 10% protein. Sámi **reindeer cheese** (*renost*) took the milk of at least ten reindeer for one plate-sized cheese. It is on the Slow Food Ark of Taste because reindeer give so little milk ([Wikipedia: Reindeer cheese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reindeer_cheese); [Slow Food](https://www.fondazioneslowfood.com/en/ark-of-taste-slow-food/reindeer-cheese/)).

**Moose** (*Alces alces*). Älgens Hus in Bjurholm, Sweden (Christer and Ulla Johansson) milks three moose and makes about 300 kg of cheese a year in three styles (rind, blue, feta-style; feta sells best). The price is about US$1,000/kg per Wikipedia (dead-linked citations) or 6000 SEK/kg per Lonely Planet. A Russian producer, GamEat, also exists ([Wikipedia: Moose cheese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moose_cheese); [Lonely Planet](https://www.lonelyplanet.com/points-of-interest/algens-hus/1415867); [Dagens PS](https://www.dagensps.se/weekend/algen-som-klarar-kriget-och-fetaosten/)).

**Human**. The first formally documented human milk bank opened in Vienna in 1909 (Theodor Escherich). Boston followed in 1910 or 1911, depending on the source ([PMC9016667](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9016667/); [EMBA Austria](https://europeanmilkbanking.com/country/austria/); [Wikipedia: Human milk bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_milk_bank)). Banks screen donors and usually pasteurise, and some countries supply donor milk on prescription ([Wikipedia: Breast milk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breast_milk)).

**Pig** (sow). ~8.5% fat, watery, "gamey". Not used because sows are uncooperative or aggressive, milking lasts only about 15 seconds (vs ~10 min for a cow), and the milk comes from 8–16 small teats. Yield is about 13 lb/day (vs ~65 lb for a cow), no machine fits a sow, and sows cannot conceive while lactating ([Wikipedia: Pig milk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pig_milk)). Let-down needs nursing piglets ([enviroliteracy](https://enviroliteracy.org/animals/why-cant-you-milk-a-pig/); popular source). **Pig-milk cheese oddity:** a Dutch farmer's team of up to ten people spent dozens of hours milking and, after several failures, made a few kg. It was described as "chalky and a little bit salty" and sold for about $2,300/kg to a charity buyer (2015). Chef Edward Lee made a pig-milk ricotta ([Wikipedia: Pig milk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pig_milk); [Daily Meal](https://www.thedailymeal.com/eat/why-don-t-we-eat-pig-milk-cheese)).

**Seal / whale**. Hooded seal milk is about 60% fat, the fattiest known. Some low-quality sites claim harp seal instead ([UTAS](https://www.utas.edu.au/about/news-and-stories/articles/2017/471-heres-why-fat-is-everything-in-the-coldest-place-on-earth)). Baleen whale milk is 30–50% fat at mid-lactation ([PubMed 10882306](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/10882306)). There is a 1955 J. Dairy Research analysis of blue whale milk, but I could not read its figures ([record](https://arch.neicon.ru/xmlui/handle/123456789/212078)). These have no food use, so they go in a note.

### Inferences
- Camel and donkey are the clearest cases for "the milk decides the product": low κ-casein means no firm rennet curd, and it took a camel-specific enzyme to make camel cheese practical.
- Mare and donkey milk are low in fat and casein and high in lactose. With little to curdle and plenty of sugar, they ferment into drinks (kumis) rather than cheese. The Kumis article gives only the sugar half of this explanation; the casein half is my inference from the low-protein figures.
- Sheep and goat are major dairy species, but this session collected no cited composition or product data for them. From general knowledge (unverified here): Roquefort (sheep only, by law), Pecorino, Manchego, feta (sheep, up to 30% goat), chèvre. They should still be species nodes.

### Gaps
- Cited figures for cow, goat and sheep composition are missing. The Park & Haenlein/Medhammar full tables were behind redirects or paywalls. Use FAO "Milk and dairy products in human nutrition" (2013) for those rows.
- The camel FAO page was not read in full, and I did not verify the Camelbert producer (thought to be a Mauritanian dairy, Tiviski; unverified).
- I did not verify Sámi reindeer milking today, only the historical accounts.

## 4. Non-mammal "milks"

### Takeaway
At least six non-mammal lineages feed their young a secreted "milk": pigeons, flamingos and emperor penguins (crop milk), *Diploptera* cockroaches, *Toxeus* jumping spiders, discus fish (skin mucus), caecilian amphibians (both skin-feeding and a true cloacal milk, Science 2024), and tsetse flies (milk gland). What links them is function rather than ancestry: a parent makes a nutrient- and often antibody-rich secretion after hatching or birth, sometimes under prolactin. None has a human food use. Cockroach milk crystals were proposed as a supplement but never developed beyond the lab.

### Cited Findings
- **Crop milk** (pigeons and doves, flamingos, male emperor penguins), apparently evolved independently in each group. Both sexes produce it in pigeons and flamingos; only males in emperor penguins. It is a pale-yellow semi-solid, higher in protein and fat than cow or human milk, with **no carbohydrate**. It is controlled by **prolactin**, the same hormone as in mammals. It is holocrine (fat- and protein-laden cells slough off the crop lining), whereas mammal milk is an apocrine emulsion. It contains IgA antibodies and bacteria (Gillespie et al. 2012) ([Wikipedia: Crop milk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop_milk)). No human use.
- **Cockroach milk** (*Diploptera punctata*, the Pacific beetle cockroach, a live-bearer): the embryos receive a fluid that forms protein crystals in their gut. The crystals mix protein, sugars and fatty acids and are glycosylated and lipid-bound. A crystal is estimated to hold more than 3× the energy of the same mass of dairy milk ([Banerjee et al. 2016, IUCrJ 3:282–293, via ScienceAlert](https://www.sciencealert.com/scientists-think-cockroach-milk-could-be-a-new-superfood-and-we-wish-we-were-kidding); [Ig Nobel write-up](https://agscience.org.nz/cockroach-milk-proteins-churn-out-an-ig-nobel-win/)). The team planned yeast production as a possible protein supplement, and toxicity to humans was not established ([Fortune 2016](https://fortune.com/2016/07/27/cockroach-milk-health-benefits)). "Superfood" coverage is press extrapolation.
- **Jumping spider milk** (*Toxeus magnus*, Chen et al., *Science* Nov 2018, Xishuangbanna Tropical Botanical Garden, CAS): the mother secretes droplets from her epigastric furrow. Spiderlings drink them until about 20 days, then suck directly, and keep drinking until about 40 days (subadult), well past independence. Press reports say the milk has about 4× the protein of cow's milk (unverified against the paper) ([XTBG](https://en.xtbg.ac.cn/ns/es/201811/t20181130_201667.html); [Sci.News](https://www.sci.news/biology/spider-milk-06673.html); [Asian Scientist](https://www.asianscientist.com/2018/12/in-the-lab/jumping-spider-milk/)).
- **Discus fish** (*Symphysodon* spp.): both parents feed fry on skin mucus for about 3 weeks after hatching. The protein, cortisol, immunoglobulin and ion content of the mucus changes over the breeding cycle "much like mammalian milk", and weeks 3–4 look like a weaning conflict. Fry grow faster with parents. The mucus passes on gut microbes, and also contaminants such as cadmium ([J Exp Biol summary](https://cob.silverchair.com/jeb/article-pdf/213/22/i/1271677/isi-ii.pdf); [ScienceDaily 2010](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/10/101029104603.htm); [Sci. Rep. 2017, PMC5507859](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5507859/)).
- **Caecilians**, two separate phenomena:
  - *Skin feeding* (*Boulengerula taitanus*, Kupfer et al. 2006, Nature 440:926–929): the mother's outer skin becomes thick and lipid-rich, and hatchlings peel it off with special deciduous teeth. It was also found in *Siphonops annulatus* (2008) ([Springer/Nature](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/nature04403); [AmphibiaWeb](https://amphibiaweb.org/species/1837)).
  - *Milk* (*Siphonops annulatus*, Science 383:1092–1095, March 2024): hatchlings drink a lipid- and carbohydrate-rich milk from the mother's cloaca for about 2 months. It is made by hypertrophied glands in the oviduct and released when the babies touch her and make sounds ([Naturkundemuseum Stuttgart](https://www.naturkundemuseum-bw.de/en/press/detailansicht/egg-laying-caecilian-amphibians-produce-milk-for-their-young); [Butantan repository](https://repositorio.butantan.gov.br/handle/butantan/5278?mode=full)).
- **Tsetse fly** (*Glossina*): the larva grows inside the mother on milk from a modified accessory gland (the "milk gland"). Milk gland proteins MGP2–10 are the larva's main amino-acid source and help keep the milk stable, a role the authors compare to mammalian caseins. They exist only in *Glossina*, which makes them candidate targets for tsetse control. Sphingomyelinase in the milk activates only in the acidic larval gut ([PLOS Genetics 2014 / PMC3998940](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3998940); [PMC3406556](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3406556); [PLoS NTD 2014](https://doaj.org/article/1151f961554e4892bc47a061a5dfa5cb)).

### Inferences
- Shared features with mammal milk: a parent-specific secretion fed after birth or hatching; it is rich in protein and lipid; it often carries immune factors (IgA in crop milk, immunoglobulin in discus mucus); it is under prolactin control (shown for birds); it has casein-like functional proteins (tsetse); and it ends in weaning (discus, spider). None of these is milk in the mammary sense.
- The only "human use" is indirect: tsetse milk proteins as a target for controlling sleeping-sickness vectors (a proposal, not deployed), and cockroach crystals as a speculative supplement.
- **Node judgement:** leave these out of the processing graph, since nothing is made from them. One "Other milks" side note, or a single grey off-graph node listing them, would be the most delightful aside in the post.

### Gaps
- I did not read the primary *Toxeus* or *Diploptera* papers, so the composition multiples (4× protein, 3× energy) are press-level.
- I found no source on prolactin control of discus mucus, although it is widely asserted.

## 5. "Milks" that aren't milk: the boundary

### Takeaway
The boundary is a mammary (or analogous parental) secretion = in. White liquids named by analogy = out. A one-line note can list them.

### Cited Findings
- I collected no sources this session on plant milks, coconut milk, milk of magnesia, milk thistle or milk of lime.

### Inferences
- Plant milks (soy, oat, almond), coconut milk, milk of magnesia (a magnesium hydroxide suspension), milk of lime (a calcium hydroxide suspension), milk thistle (a plant named for the white veins on its leaves), and also latex/"milk" of dandelion and fig all share only the colour, or an opaque suspension. They should be out of the graph. They could appear in a single "named for the colour" note. Soy milk is the only borderline case, because tofu is made from it by curdling, a direct parallel to cheese. It would make a nice footnote, but it is still out.

### Gaps
- None of this is cited. If the post makes claims about these items, it needs sources.

## 6. Breed / feed variants (A2, hay milk, Jersey vs Holstein)

### Takeaway
Confirmed: these are not separate nodes. They change fat level, one protein variant, or flavour, but they go through the same processing steps. At most they deserve notes on the cow "Milk" root, and hay milk deserves one because it has EU legal status.

### Cited Findings
- **A2 milk:** A2 is a genetic variant of β-casein that differs by breed. Canadian Dairy Network tests found the A2A2 genotype in about 35% of Holsteins, 65% of Jerseys and 53% of Guernseys ([CDN 2016 PDF](https://www.cdn.ca/images/uploaded/file/A2%20Milk%20Article%20-%20December%202016.pdf)). Later Lactanet data give 86.4% for Guernsey and 77.8% for Jersey ([Dairy Producer](https://www.dairyproducer.com/updated-casein-genetics-a2-kappa-dairy/)).
- **Jersey vs Holstein:** about 4.7% vs 3.7% butterfat (lay sources) ([washtenawcd](https://water.washtenawcd.gov/is-jersey-milk-whole-milk.html); [Guernsey cattle](https://guernseycattle.com/?p=6)).
- **Hay milk (Heumilch):** registered as a Traditional Speciality Guaranteed by Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2016/304 of 2 March 2016, on Austria's application. The registered names are Heumilch, Haymilk, Latte fieno, Lait de foin and Leche de heno ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg_impl/2016/304)). Cows are fed grass, legumes, grain and hay, with no silage and no GM feed ([Verbraucherzentrale Südtirol](https://www.consumer.bz.it/de/print/pdf/node/222)). Hay milk is about 15% of Austrian milk production against about 3% in Europe ([Lebensmittel Praxis](https://lebensmittelpraxis.de/laenderreports/17088-laenderreport-oesterreich-wo-die-kuehe-friedlich-grasen.html?start=1)).

### Inferences
- A processing graph splits where the *process* or *product* changes. A2, Jersey and hay milk all flow into the same butter, cheese and yogurt, so they are notes on the "Milk" root.
- My best guess for why silage-free milk matters to cheesemakers is the risk of clostridial "late blowing" in long-aged hard cheeses (e.g. Emmentaler, Gruyère rules). This is unverified this session. If confirmed, it is a good one-line note on the hard-cheese edge.

### Gaps
- I did not verify the silage and late-blowing link.

### Most interesting for a general reader (⭐)
1. **Camel milk won't make a proper cheese with ordinary rennet**, and it took a camel's own chymosin, brewed by fermentation, to fix it. That enzyme then also curdled "uncurdleable" donkey milk.
2. **Kumis/airag**: mare's milk is too sugary and too low in casein to make good cheese, so the steppe turned it into a mildly alcoholic drink (0.7–2.5%), and then distilled it.
3. **Strippings**: the last pint of a milking is 4–5× as fatty as the first, and farmers saved it for butter.
4. **Moose cheese**: three moose, one farm, about 300 kg a year.
5. **Witch's milk**: newborn babies of either sex sometimes lactate.
6. **Crop milk and friends**: male emperor penguins, pigeons and flamingos make "milk" with no sugar in it. Spiders, cockroaches and egg-laying amphibians do something similar.

### Recommendation for the graph
- Keep **Milk** and **Colostrum** as roots.
- Under Milk, add species sub-roots only where a product *requires* that species: **Buffalo** (mozzarella di bufala), **Sheep** (Roquefort, pecorino), **Goat** (chèvre), **Camel** (camel cheese via camel chymosin, shubat), **Mare** (kumis). Fold donkey into Mare as "equid milk" (with Pule) if space is tight. Yak is a strong optional sixth (chhurpi).
- Everything else (lactation stages, strippings, breeds, hay milk, pig, seal, moose, reindeer, human) becomes notes on the nearest node. Non-mammal milks become one off-graph aside.
