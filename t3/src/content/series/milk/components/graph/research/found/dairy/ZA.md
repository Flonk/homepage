## Amasi (amasi; maas, emasi, mafi in some sources)
- Animal(s): Mostly cow (incl. commercial recombined milk); some goat milk per Table 1 of a review
- Kind: Fermented (soured) milk
- What it is: A thick, sour, curdled milk, often compared to cottage cheese or plain yoghurt but sharper. Staple in pre-colonial times; now a common snack. Sold commercially as well as made at home.
- How it is made: Traditional: unpasteurised cow's milk fermented 1–5 days in a smoked calabash or hide sack; the watery whey (umlaza) is drained off, leaving thick amasi. Commercial (per Wikipedia): milk fortified with skim milk powder and gelatine, pasteurised 72 °C for 15 s, cooled to 30 °C, inoculated with Lactococcus lactis and a related subspecies, incubated 30 °C for 16–24 h. Commercial samples bought in Durban were labelled full-cream or recombined milk with MAAS cultures (Lactococcus lactis subsp. lactis and cremoris, Leuconostoc mesenteroides subsp. cremoris); only one of three was labelled pasteurised; temperature and time not reported in the study.
- Where / who: Zulu, Xhosa and Ndebele food culture; also Zimbabwe and Lesotho. Usually eaten over maize porridge (pap), in clay pots, or drunk. South African Indian cuisine uses it like curd.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amasi — the traditional method, commercial method, peoples, names; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8701341/fullTextXML (Maleke et al., Scientific African 2021, doi 10.1016/j.sciaf.2021.e00795 abstract) — commercial amasi from Durban, labels, cultures, acetoin-dominated aroma; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC10678836/fullTextXML — amasi listed as South African product made with cow milk, yeast plus LAB mix, "produced commercially"; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6691171/fullTextXML — yeast species in amasi (Gadaga et al., 2000)
- Unsure: Wikipedia says "mafi" is the Sesotho name for amasi, but the cuisine article and a microbiology review treat mafi as a separate product (see Mafi).

## Mafi (Sesotho; sometimes called amasi)
- Animal(s): Milk (animal not stated)
- Kind: Fermented milk
- What it is: Fermented milk eaten with pap or on its own.
- How it is made: Not described in the sources opened; a review lists its starter organisms (Leuconostoc mesenteroides subsp. dextranicum, Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. lactis, Lactococcus lactis subsp. lactis, Lactobacillus plantarum).

## Sethemi
- Animal(s): Milk (animal not given in sources)
- Kind: Spontaneously fermented milk
- What it is: South African spontaneously fermented milk, traditionally made in gourds and clay pots.
- How it is made: Fermented by lactobacilli, lactococci and yeasts (Debaryomyces hansenii, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Cryptococcus curvatus). Container type affects yeast and LAB growth (Kebede et al. 2007, doi 10.1016/j.foodres.2006.07.012, not opened; abstract known from search only). Time and temperature not given in opened sources.
- Where / who: South Africa, traditional; language group not stated.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC4944601/fullTextXML — "spontaneously fermented milk, traditionally made in gourds and clay pots"; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC10678836/fullTextXML — microbes in sethemi (South Africa dairy table)
- Unsure: Milk type and fermentation time not in opened sources.

## Umvubo
- Animal(s): Milk (cow assumed; not stated)
- Kind: Fermented milk dish (sour milk with dry maize porridge)
- What it is: Sour milk mixed with dry pap (maize porridge). Eaten by Zulu, Xhosa and Sotho speakers.
- How it is made: Sour milk is mixed with dry pap (the cuisine article) or with umphokoqo (samp, per the Xhosa cuisine article). No temperatures or times given.
- Where / who: Xhosa and Zulu households; a Sotho list in the cuisine article also names it.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_African_cuisine — "sour milk mixed with dry pap"; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xhosa_cuisine — "sour milk mixed with umphokoqo", commonly eaten by the Xhosa
- Unsure: Sources disagree on the grain (dry pap vs umphokoqo/samp).

## Boeber
- Animal(s): Cow milk (assumed; sweet milk drink)
- Kind: Sweet milk drink / pudding
- What it is: A Cape Malay sweet milk drink, sometimes called a pudding.
- How it is made: Vermicelli and sago cooked in sweetened milk, flavoured with cardamom, stick cinnamon and rose water (method not described in source).
- Where / who: Cape Malay community. Traditionally served on the 15th night of Ramadan.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeber — ingredients, serving occasion, community
- Unsure: Served hot or cold not stated.

## Melktert (milk tart)
- Animal(s): Cow milk
- Kind: Baked milk custard tart / dessert
- What it is: South African dessert with a sweet pastry base and milk custard filling.
- How it is made: Custard of milk, flour, sugar and eggs, dusted with cinnamon (sometimes milk steeped with a cinnamon stick). Some bake the custard in the crust, others add a separately made custard just before serving. No baking temperature or time given.
- Where / who: Afrikaans-speaking and national; "milk tart" in Afrikaans.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melktert — ingredients, preparation variants, origin

## Melkkos
- Animal(s): Cow milk
- Kind: Milk porridge / milk dish
- What it is: A traditional Afrikaans farm food, like a melktert without the crust; served warm on cold evenings or as a light morning meal or supper.
- How it is made: Milk, flour, sugar, eggs and cinnamon; some recipes use pasta or sago for the crumbs. No step-by-step method given.
- Where / who: Afrikaans farm food; South Africa.
- Sources: https://af.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melkkos — ingredients, serving; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_African_cuisine — served as supper or sometimes lunch, not usually a dessert

## Donor breast milk (Milk Matters, Cape Town)
- Animal(s): Human
- Kind: Human donor milk (milk bank service)
- What it is: A Cape Town-based programme that collects and distributes donor breast milk.
- How it is made: Milk Matters pasteurises donor milk by the Holder method: heated to 62.5 °C for 30 min, then cooled to 4 °C over 60 min (a 120-min cycle). A donor milk bank in general collects, pasteurises, stores and tests donor milk before distributing it.
- Where / who: Cape Town, South Africa; for infants.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milk_bank — "South Africa: Milk Matters, a Cape Town-based program that collects and distributes breast milk"; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6124008/fullTextXML — Milk Matters is a member of the Human Milk Banking Association of South Africa and supplies donor milk to a Cape Town neonatal unit; it uses Holder pasteurisation (62.5 °C for 30 min, cooled to 4 °C over 60 min); https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11168366/fullTextXML — 70 active donor milk banks in South Africa (SABR, 2023, as cited), and a Limpopo study of a donor milk bank
- Unsure: The Milk Matters website gave a certificate error and its Wikipedia article returned 404. The processing method comes from one study of a Cape Town neonatal unit, not from a description of Milk Matters' whole operation.

## Commercial yoghurt (yogurt, sold in shops)
- Animal(s): Cow milk assumed (not stated in source)
- Kind: Fermented milk (yoghurt)
- What it is: Shop-bought yoghurt, used by caregivers in a Gauteng qualitative study; the households that bought it did not make yoghurt at home.
- How it is made: Made on a larger scale than home fermentation, using selected strains of lactic acid bacteria as starter cultures. Home fermentation usually adds no specific bacteria and sometimes uses a portion of an old ferment. No temperatures or times given for commercial yoghurt.
- Where / who: Sold in South African shops; bought by the caregivers in the study.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC4944601/fullTextXML — caregivers buy commercial yogurt and milk and do not make yoghurt at home; commercial yogurt and milk are made on a larger scale with selected LAB starter cultures (general statement citing a fermentation review)
- Unsure: Qualitative study, so no brand, fat level or processing temperatures. The starter-culture statement is a general claim, not a South African measurement.
