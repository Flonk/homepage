## Kiselo mlyako (кисело мляко) — Bulgarian yoghurt
- Animal(s): cow most often; buffalo, goat or sheep also allowed (Bulgarian standard BDS milk list, per bg.wikipedia)
- Kind: fermented milk (yoghurt)
- What it is: the national yoghurt, made with the two bacteria Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus and Streptococcus thermophilus, which work together and do not ferment milk alone. Bulgarian standard BDS requires Bulgarian-isolated cultures for product labelled "Кисело мляко по БДС"
- How it is made: warm milk to 30–45 °C and hold for about 4–12 h; higher temperature is faster but risks a lumpy texture or whey separation; cool to stop fermentation (left warm for days it keeps souring)
- Where / who: home and industrial, across Bulgaria. Its bacteria were first isolated by Stamen Grigorov in 1905 from homemade Bulgarian yoghurt or soured milk from his village
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgarian_cuisine — listed as Bulgaria's yoghurt, eaten in many dishes; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogurt — starter cultures, 30–45 °C for 4–12 h, 1905 isolation by Grigorov, Mechnikov's promotion; https://bg.wikipedia.org/wiki/Кисело_мляко — BDS milk types, cultures, 30–45 °C and 4–12 h, origin theories; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12785569 (Eastern European fermented foods review) — Bulgarian yoghurt produced in a way similar to ayran, with a historical antiageing reputation; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13287551 (traditional fermented dairy products review) — table row for yoghurt in Bulgaria, Greece and Turkey: cow milk fermented with L. delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus and S. thermophilus
- Unsure: the origin of yoghurt is uncertain (sources offer several theories, including a Thracian and a Proto-Bulgarian one); the Bulgarian-standard details come from the bg.wikipedia article and were not checked against the BDS text itself

## Goat yoghurt (Bulgarian White Dairy Goat milk)
- Animal(s): goat (Bulgarian White Dairy Goat breed)
- Kind: fermented milk (yoghurt)
- What it is: a goat-milk yoghurt used in a Trakia University pilot trial in type 2 diabetes patients, with added Aronia melanocarpa juice
- How it is made: the trial used commercial starter Laktina 17 (Bankya, Sofia) with L. delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus and S. thermophilus; no temperatures or times are given in the trial summary I read
- Where / who: Bulgarian dairy research at Trakia University, Stara Zagora; the goat yoghurt is a study product, not confirmed as a market product
- Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12472687 — the trial product, goat breed and starter
- Unsure: the source describes the product as used in a study; it does not say the product is sold commercially

## Ayryan (айрян, айран) — ayran
- Animal(s): cow (factory uses skimmed cow milk, 0.1% fat)
- Kind: drink (yoghurt drink)
- What it is: a cold, salted yoghurt drink; popular across the Balkans and in restaurants in Bulgaria
- How it is made: home version: yoghurt mixed 1:1 with water, salt to taste, optional black pepper, mint, lovage or basil; richer version uses yoghurt above 4% fat or yoghurt mixed with cream and lemon juice. Factory version: skim cow milk pasteurised, cooled to 42–43 °C, starter added, coagulated, then homogenised to a liquid; served cold
- Where / who: Bulgaria and the Balkans; eaten at breakfast or lunch with pastries (en.wikipedia); sold ready-made in shops
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayran — yoghurt, chilled or iced water, carbonated sometimes, served in Bulgarian restaurants, ayryan spelling; https://bg.wikipedia.org/wiki/Айрян — home and factory method, 42–43 °C, herbs, serving cold; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgarian_cuisine — listed as a fermented beverage
- Unsure: the bg.wikipedia article does not mention carbonation, which the English article mentions

## Katak (катък) — also qatiq, katyk, katuk, krotmach, krukmach, kurtmach, katmach
- Animal(s): sheep (ewes), taken from the thickest milk at the end of lactation (August)
- Kind: fermented sour milk; salted, matured
- What it is: a salty, sour, traditional sheep-milk product, described in sources as a curd or yoghurt-like product. The name is Turkish-derived per bg.wikipedia. Studied as a source of lactic acid bacteria
- How it is made: per bg.wikipedia, milk is heated in a water bath, kept in a dark cool place, stirred over several hours or days, and salted with no rennet and no added starter; a thicker product comes from simmering the milk on low heat for hours before souring. Traditionally matured in a clay pot kept at a steady temperature, ideally in a well, for over six months; once opened it is eaten directly and keeps for months. The 2023 study (Lukovit area, NW Bulgaria) reports four homemade samples from a farm on Balkan Mountain, made from whole sheep milk at the end of lactation with only salt and no starter, then stored at 10 °C for two months
- Where / who: southern Bulgaria (bg.wikipedia) and the Lukovit area of NW Bulgaria (2023 paper); a dairy salad made with katak is served as a meze with rakia
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgarian_cuisine — katak in the dairy list; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogurt — katuk made by the Bulgars from the 7th century, with starter of fermented sheep milk, still made in rural areas at the end of the milking season; https://bg.wikipedia.org/wiki/Катък — method, sheep milk, clay pot, names; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10745348 — homemade samples from Lukovit area, sheep milk, salt only, stored 10 °C for 2 months; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13512861 — 2026 study of L. plantarum L3 isolated from traditional Bulgarian katak; calls katak a traditional Bulgarian fermented dairy product with extended shelf life; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13515080 — sheep milk review; notes studies on traditional Bulgarian sheep milk products with high CLA content
- Unsure: en.wikipedia "Katyk" (redirects to Qatiq) calls katak a spread with a mayonnaise-like consistency and flags the claim as needing a citation; bg.wikipedia says modern "катък" can mean a yoghurt and sheep-cheese mix, which lacks the traditional qualities; katak is a borderline curd, with a cheese-like side

## Kaymak (каймак)
- Animal(s): buffalo, cow, sheep or goat (per en.wikipedia)
- Kind: cream (cream-skin product)
- What it is: a creamy, clotted-cream-like product with about 60% milk fat, pale yellow, sweet-sour; called a national meal by Bulgarians along with Bosnians, Montenegrins, Serbs and Albanians (en.wikipedia)
- How it is made (general method, not Bulgaria-specific): raw milk heated slowly and simmered on very low heat for about 2 hours; cream skimmed from the surface and chilled, where it mildly ferments for hours or days; in the Balkans it may be salted and aged up to a year
- Where / who: Balkans and wider Turkic-influenced region; Bulgaria listed as a region (en.wikipedia)
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaymak — description, milks, method, Bulgaria listed as a region and national meal; https://bg.wikipedia.org/wiki/Каймак — produced across the Balkans, sweet-sour taste, the word also used for the cream on espresso and cappuccino in Bulgaria
- Unsure: no Bulgarian production detail found; the method above is the general Balkan method from en.wikipedia; the bg.wikipedia page is flagged as needing sources

## Tarator (таратор) — cold yoghurt soup
- Animal(s): cow (yoghurt)
- Kind: dish (cold yoghurt soup; dish where milk is the main ingredient)
- What it is: a traditional cold dairy soup and a popular summer dish in Bulgaria, usually an appetiser; some drink it with mastika or rakia
- How it is made: cucumber, garlic, dill and oil mixed first; yoghurt and cold water added, stirred, salted, finished with ground walnuts; served chilled, sometimes with ice; eaten with a spoon. The modern yoghurt form dates from the mid-20th century; earlier "tarator" meant a wider group of cold soups and sauces, often vinegar- or walnut-based
- Where / who: Bulgaria; regional variants not described
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarator — yoghurt, cucumber, garlic, oil, salt, dill, walnuts; "In Bulgaria, tarator is a traditional cold soup and a popular summer dish"; https://bg.wikipedia.org/wiki/Таратор — method, mid-20th-century modern form, serving customs; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogurt — tarator-like milk salad in Bulgaria
- Unsure: none major; a Turkish cacık and a Greek tzatziki are related foreign dishes, not Bulgarian entries

## Human breast milk and colostrum (Bulgarian mothers, research samples)
- Animal(s): human
- Kind: raw human milk (not a food product on sale)
- What it is: breast milk and colostrum of Bulgarian mothers, sampled for their bacteria; not a donor-milk or retail product
- How it is made: not a processed product; the first study took breast milk and infant faeces from 14 mother–infant pairs over the first month of life; the second is a pilot metagenomic comparison of colostrum microbiome by delivery mode
- Where / who: Bulgaria; the author affiliations are Sofia research institutes
- Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11433867 — cultivable lactobacilli from breast milk and infant faeces of 14 Bulgarian mother–infant pairs; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12843876 — colostrum microbiome in Bulgarian mothers by delivery mode, pilot study
