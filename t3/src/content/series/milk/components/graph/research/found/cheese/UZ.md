## Qurut (Qurt / Kurut / Qurut; kashk in Persian-Turkic usage)
- Animal(s): sheep, goat or cow (Russian Wikipedia); the Uzbek form is mainly made from fermented milk with no animal named
- Kind: dried curd (dried fermented milk balls, salted)
- What it is: A dried, salty, sour curd sold in balls or chunks. The Uzbek name is qurut; the Central Atlas lists it as a dense, sour-milk dairy product. Wikipedia's Kashk article shows balls of it at Chorsu Bazaar, Tashkent.
- How it is made: Katyk (soured milk) is hung in a cloth bag for several days so the whey drains, leaving a curd (suzma). Salt, and sometimes red pepper, is added. The curd is rolled into walnut-sized balls and sun-dried for several days under gauze until firm. Boiled variant: katyk boiled 2–3 hours, strained, usually unsalted, dried into balls or kept as a paste for soup. Modern versions (Kashk article): sour yogurt blended, boiled, strained, fermented several days in a warm oven, dried, salted. Balls range about 0.5–5 cm across. A dried kurut study describes a 3-day drying of salted rolls at 28 °C after fermentation.
- Texture / ripening: Hard, dry and salty, stores for months (Russian Wikipedia says up to about a month in dry cloth bags; the dried kurut study and Kashk article describe longer keeping, several years in the Kurut study's account).
- Where / who: Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan, Kazakhstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan and China (Kurut study). Used as a snack and as a seasoning for soup (qurutob in Tajikistan). Farm/household; no protected status found.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashk — Uzbek qurut forms, Tashkent photo, modern oven method, sun-drying over weeks; https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/Курут — milk types, katyk-to-suzma method, ball sizes, Uzbekistan named only in a photo caption; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12608619/fullTextXML — Central Asian atlas: Qurt/Kurut/Qurut in Uzbek, Tajik and Kazakh, dairy product from fermented milk, dense texture; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC13066772/fullTextXML — dried kurut, names and milk species (sheep, goat, cow, mare, camel) and process
- Unsure: The Uzbek-specific recipe is not confirmed. The sources describe Kazakh and Kyrgyz methods, and the Wikipedia Uzbek-cuisine page does not name qurut at all. Whether Uzbek qurut is always made from sheep or goat milk is not established.

## Suzma (Chakka / Süzma / Süzbe)
- Animal(s): cow (most common, per Uzbek Wikipedia on qatiq); goat and camel milk also used in Uzbekistan for qatiq, and so for suzma
- Kind: fresh, strained (soft cheese-like curd)
- What it is: A thick, strained soft curd made from qatiq (local fermented milk). Also called chakka in English Wikipedia. Wikipedia's Strained yogurt article says chak(k)a or süzme is eaten across Uzbek and other Central Asian and Iranian-Turkic cuisines.
- How it is made: Qatiq (boiled milk, cooled to about 30–35 °C, cultured, left 4–5 hours) is strained in cloth bags until it reaches a soft-cheese consistency. Further drying yields qurut (see above). Uzbek Wikipedia also uses suzma as the base for ayran and chalop.
- Texture / ripening: Soft, thick and spreadable; not aged.
- Where / who: Uzbekistan and wider Central Asia; household and rural use. No protected status.
- Sources: https://uz.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qatiq — qatiq made from cow, goat and camel milk in Uzbekistan; qatiq strained in cloth bags to make suzma; uses; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzma — Uzbek and Central Asian chak(k)a/süzme, made by draining qatiq, further drying yields qurut; https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/Узбекская_кухня — suzma as sour curd mass from katyk; base of ayran; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalap — chalap made from qatiq or suzma
- Unsure: Chakka is the name used in English Wikipedia and the Indian sense of strained yogurt is different; only the Central Asian sense is recorded here. Whether Uzbek suzma is always from cow milk is not confirmed.

## Tvorog (cottage cheese; Uzbek "tvorog")
- Animal(s): cow (default); the source does not name another animal for Uzbek production
- Kind: fresh curd (cottage/pot cheese)
- What it is: A soft white fresh curd made by fermenting milk and draining the whey. The Central Atlas gives Tvorog as the Uzbek (and Kazakh, Kyrgyz, Tajik) name for a solid, white dairy product made by fermenting milk and removing the whey.
- How it is made: Traditionally, fermented milk is hung in bags to drain. Industrial method (en Wikipedia): standardised, pasteurised milk warmed to 28–30 °C, ferment and pepsin added, about eight hours, curd cut, pressed, cooled, packed.
- Texture / ripening: Soft, fresh, not ripened.
- Where / who: Uzbekistan and across Central Asia, both household and factory (industrial). No protected status.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12608619/fullTextXML — Table 2 entry Tvorog (Kaz, Uzb, Kir, Tgk); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tvorog — how it is made, traditional bag draining, industrial method; Wikipedia Kurut note lists "dry cottage cheese in Uzbekistan" as the regional comparison
- Unsure: Its status as a traditional Uzbek cheese versus a general dairy product is not settled by the sources.

## Brynza (Uzbek industrial brined cheese)
- Animal(s): not stated in the Uzbek source; the English Brynza article describes sheep-milk versions as the classic style (Slovak, Polish), with goat and cow also used
- Kind: brined (salty, white, crumbly or soft)
- What it is: A brined white cheese. Uzbek Wikipedia lists brinza as a salty cheese that matures in brine, and says Uzbek dairies have set up production of soft cheese and brinza.
- How it is made: Not described for Uzbekistan. English Brynza article: a salty finish and white, tangy, crumbly, slightly moist texture; the brine step is not described there.
- Texture / ripening: White, tangy, crumbly, slightly moist (English Brynza article, Balkan/Eastern European type).
- Where / who: Uzbek dairy factories (industrial). No protected status found.
- Sources: https://uz.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pishloq — brinza as a salty cheese matured in brine; Uzbek dairy production of soft cheese and brinza; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brynza — texture, sheep-milk origin, no Uzbek variant
- Unsure: Whether Uzbek brinza follows the Balkan sheep-milk method or is made from cow milk is not confirmed. The Uzbek source gives no milk type.
