## Kajmak (kajmak; kaymak, kajmaku in other languages)
- Animal(s): Cow (bs.wiki and en.wiki specify cow's milk); en.wiki Kajmak also lists water buffalo, sheep and goat milk in the general article.
- Kind: Fermented milk-fat product (clotted-cream type)
- What it is: A soft, creamy dairy product with a mildly sour taste, about 60% milk fat, made by fermenting the fat that separates from cooked milk. Bosnian wiki describes it as an aged and young form; young is milder, aged is sharper and yellowish.
- How it is made: Raw milk is slowly boiled, then simmered about two hours over very low heat (no temperature given); heat is turned off and the cream skimmed; the cream is chilled for several hours or days while it mildly ferments. It may be salted and aged up to a year. Skorup is a version matured in dried animal-skin sacks.
- Where / who: Balkans, named as a particularly Bosnian dish on bs.wiki; en.wiki says Bosnians, Bulgarians, Montenegrins, Serbs and Albanians consider it a national meal. Mostly home-made, with growing commercial production. Served fresh as a breakfast or condiment, with ćevapi, and with desserts in the East.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kajmak — production steps, cow milk, aged/young types, Bosnia named as a kajmak region; https://bs.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kajmak — Bosnian-language description of kajmak as fermented milk fat, cow's milk, "naročito u Bosni"; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaymak — Balkan method and national-meal statement; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skorup — skorup as sack-matured kajmak; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:32846575%20AND%20SRC:MED&format=json&resultType=core — Food Research International 2020 record (DOI 10.1016/j.foodres.2020.109494) lists kajmak from Bosnia and Herzegovina households among samples
- Unsure: The Bosnian Wikipedia and en.wiki do not describe Bosnian regional or pastoral variants or specific Bosnian names beyond "kajmak". Skorup is linked to the Balkans generally, not to Bosnia specifically.

## Mlado maslo / maslo (butter; puter, maslac, posvojenica)
- Animal(s): Cow most commonly; sheep, goat and buffalo milk also used (bs.wiki Puter)
- Kind: Butter, and a clarified butter form
- What it is: Fresh churned butter is called "mlado maslo" in Bosnia and Herzegovina. "Maslo" refers to the clarified, melted-and-set form, a granular, anhydrous milk fat with a strong flavour, stored cool and dark (traditionally in a cellar).
- How it is made: Fat is separated from fresh or fermented kajmak, or skimmed from milk. Traditionally beaten in a wooden vessel called a "stap" to make mlado maslo. Clarifying by melting and solidifying gives maslo. General method from the article: cream heated to 85–95 °C for 10–30 (time unit not given), cooled until fat crystallises, churned until fat grains form, then rinsed with cold water and kneaded. Cultured butter uses sour cream fermented with lactic acid bacteria.
- Where / who: Bosnian tradition, home production described; the article gives no pastoral or regional detail.
- Sources: https://bs.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puter — Bosnian names mlado maslo, maslo, stap churn, kajmak as starting material, heat treatment and churning steps
- Unsure: The 85–95 °C and "10–30" time figures are general dairy practice in the article, not a Bosnian source; the time unit is missing.

## Pavlaka (cream; sour cream; kisela pavlaka, slatka pavlaka)
- Animal(s): Cow milk (not specified in the sources; general article)
- Kind: Milk cream / sour cream
- What it is: A "vrsta mliječne kreme" (a kind of milk cream). Contains up to 30% fat. Sweet pavlaka (skimmed before souring, 24% fat) is used for whipping, ice cream, parfaits and sauces; sour pavlaka (10–20% fat) is skimmed when acidity has developed.
- How it is made: Cream forms on standing milk; skimming it from the surface produces pavlaka; lactic acid bacteria sour the milk and the cream is skimmed from the top. Sour type depends on how long the milk stands and how sour it gets. No temperatures given in the Bosnian source.
- Where / who: Widely used in Bosnian cooking; added to dishes alongside kajmak and milk; used in Kljukuša, a traditional dough dish with white garlic.
- Sources: https://bs.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pavlaka — Bosnian-language description of sweet and sour pavlaka, fat contents and method; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosnian_cuisine — pavlaka named in Bosnian dishes, Kljukuša made with pavlaka or sour milk
- Unsure: The Bosnian article is flagged as weakly sourced; no regional Bosnian production notes.

## Jogurt (yoghurt)
- Animal(s): Cow, sheep (Balkan history); not further specified for Bosnia
- Kind: Fermented milk
- What it is: Yoghurt, named in the Bosnian dairy list. Balkan yoghurt production dates back to the 7th century per en.wiki Yogurt.
- How it is made: Milk is heated, then Lactobacillus bulgaricus and Streptococcus thermophilus are added; fermented at 42–45 °C for 2–4 hours; then cooled to slow souring. Final product about 0.7% lactic acid (bs.wiki Jogurt, general method). Katuk, a Bulgar-era yoghurt, was made from fermented sheep's milk or cheese starter mixed with water, still made in rural Balkan areas at the end of the sheep-milking season.
- Where / who: Balkan and Bosnian dairy; named as "jogurt" (Bosnian) in the Bosnian dairy list. No Bosnia-specific pastoral detail found.
- Sources: https://bs.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jogurt — Bosnian method with temperatures and times; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogurt — Balkan history, katuk, Bosnian title link; https://bs.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mliječni_proizvodi — jogurt named in Bosnian dairy list; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:32846575%20AND%20SRC:MED&format=json&resultType=core — yoghurt among products sampled in Bosnia and Herzegovina households
- Unsure: The method on bs.wiki is general, not Bosnian-specific.

## Kefir
- Animal(s): Cow, sheep or goat milk (general)
- Kind: Fermented milk drink
- What it is: Fermented drink made with kefir grains, from the Caucasus (North Caucasian origin of the name). Listed as a non-alcoholic beverage in the Bosnian cuisine article.
- How it is made: Kefir grains (2–5% of milk) are added and fermented 12–24 hours (en.wiki Kefir) or 1–2 days at 10–25 °C (en.wiki, general). Grains are strained out and reused. Industrial kefir uses cultures instead of grains.
- Where / who: Bosnian cuisine article lists kefir as a beverage; neither opened Wikipedia article mentions Bosnia.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosnian_cuisine — kefir listed under beverages; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kefir — method and origin; https://bs.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kefir — Bosnian-language article, general origin and method
- Unsure: Whether kefir is made in Bosnia commercially or at home is not confirmed. The Bosnian kefir article does not describe Bosnian practice.

## Sutlijaš (sutlija; rice and milk pudding)
- Animal(s): Cow milk
- Kind: Milk dessert (rice pudding)
- What it is: A sweet rice-and-milk dessert typical of the Balkans and Turkey; the name comes from Turkish süt (milk). Listed as a Bosnian dish in the Bosnian cuisine article.
- How it is made: Rice is cooked in water until it releases its starch; water is drained; milk is added and cooked until thick; sugar and optional vanilla are added. Traditional method about 40 minutes; instant versions use hot milk and brief stirring.
- Where / who: Home and general Balkan preparation; Bosnian cuisine article cites a Radio Sarajevo piece calling it an iftar dessert (cited by en.wiki Sutlijaš, not opened).
- Sources: https://bs.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sutlijaš — Bosnian-language method, milk, rice, sugar, vanilla, origin; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sutlijaš — Balkan dessert, Turkish name, method, Bosnian dish listing; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosnian_cuisine — sutlijaš listed as Bosnian sweet rice dish
- Unsure: The sutlijaš en.wiki page gives a general preparation; the Bosnian-specific steps are not confirmed.
