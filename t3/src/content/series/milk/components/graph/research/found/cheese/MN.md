## Byaslag (бяслаг; byaslag, byasulag, byaslag)
- Animal(s): Yak or cow most often in the sources; goat, sheep and mare's milk also named for the cheese. Sources disagree.
- Kind: Pressed fresh cheese, air-dried until hard (not aged).
- What it is: A firm, mild, slightly sweet and salty cheese eaten as a snack. Dried so it keeps while herders move.
- How it is made: Boil the milk. Add kefir (more common) or rennet and let it curdle. Strain the curds through cloth and squeeze out the liquid. Press between two boards with a heavy weight. Dry outdoors on rooftops. Eaten dry, often soaked in warm tea. One source (irci) describes it as made from skimmed milk after the cream is removed; another (yogurtathome) uses whole milk.
- Texture / ripening: Very dry and hard when dried; not aged. Some sources note a lumpy, sour curd before drying.
- Where / who: Widespread across Mongolia; least regional variation of the dried cheeses per a secondary source. No protected status found.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byaslag — Mongolian byaslag made from cow or yak milk, lumpy curd, sour; https://www.yogurtathome.com/single-post/2019/10/03/fermented-milk-products-from-all-over-the-world-byaslag-mongolia — steps (kefir or rennet, straining, pressing, rooftop drying), yak preferred, texture; https://mn.wikipedia.org/wiki/Бяслаг — made from cow, sheep or goat milk with a curdling enzyme; https://www.irci.jp/rdb/item/13427 — soft cheese from fermented raw or cooked milk, skimmed milk; https://www.au-plovdiv.bg/docs/Razvitie_AS/DSc/2022/Culuunbat/Abstracts.pdf — lactic acid bacteria isolated from byasulag samples.
- Unsure: The milk (yak, cow, goat, sheep) and whether the cream is removed both differ across sources. The Wikipedia "byaslag" page also has a Chinese (Inner Mongolian) entry; the Mongolian entry is the one counted here.

## Aaruul (ааруул; aaruul, aarul)
- Animal(s): Cow, yak, goat and camel make curd that can be dried; horse milk does not form a curd. Camel curd needed 20 hours at 43 °C and stayed thin; cow, yak and goat formed thick curd in 4 hours.
- Kind: Dried curd (hard, air- and sun-dried).
- What it is: The main Mongolian dried curd; a hard, sour, snack-type product stored for months without refrigeration. Usually eaten plain or sweetened, sometimes with dried berries.
- How it is made: Pasteurise whole milk at 85 °C for 30 minutes, cool to 42 °C and inoculate with a starter (a thermophilic yoghurt culture in the trial). Incubate at 43 °C for about 4 hours. Pour the curd into cheesecloth and drain the whey. Cut the curd into small pieces and dry completely in the sun (on a roof). Stored at 3 °C in the trial. The traditional method is to make a yoghurt (tarag) first and then dry the curd. In an alternative method, yoghurt is boiled 10–20 minutes, drained in a bag, pressed for over 10 hours, sliced thin and sun-dried (selenatravel, not fully checked). Camel-milk aaruul is made without removing fat and sometimes with wild plants added (Ishii and Samejima, 2006).
- Texture / ripening: Very hard, about six times harder than a peanut per one source. Camel aaruul is harder and lower in water than cow aaruul (Ishii and Samejima). Iwate analysis: moisture 13.3–20.3 g, protein 41.5–55.6 g per 100 g; harder and drier than Parmesan.
- Where / who: Nationwide, a staple of nomadic herders; eaten by children as a snack. Protected status: none found.
- Sources: https://stud.epsilon.slu.se/8197/1/daginder_e_150701.pdf — full aaruul preparation (pasteurise 85 °C, starter, 43 °C, cheesecloth, sun-dried, 3 °C), milks tested, horse milk no curd, camel 20 h; https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/647244c3e17b74d2224f42e3 — camel-milk aaruul made without fat removal, with some wild plants, drier and harder; https://iwate-pu.repo.nii.ac.jp/records/1932 — composition and shape comparison with Parmesan and Nepal churpi; https://www.discovermongolia.mn/about-mongolia/culture-art-history/mongolian-traditional-food-and-beverage — dried curd storable for months; https://www.au-plovdiv.bg/docs/Razvitie_AS/DSc/2022/Culuunbat/Abstracts.pdf — goat and sheep milk suited to aaruul; LAB isolated from aaruul.
- Unsure: The traditional method (yoghurt first, or fresh curd pressed) differs by source; the thesis trials used whole milk, while a secondary source describes a skimmed-milk base for the related aarts.

## Eezgii (eezgii, eezgiy; Cyrillic spelling not found in sources)
- Animal(s): Sheep and yak most often (irci); the first spring milk is said to be richest. Other milks not named.
- Kind: Roasted dried curd (sweet-tasting, dry).
- What it is: A dried, roasted curd eaten as a snack. Sweet and high in protein.
- How it is made: Bring milk to a boil, stirring; add kefir (~1/2 cup per 2 quarts); stir until curds separate from yellow whey. Strain out curds and keep the whey. Stir the curds over low heat until the excess moisture is gone and they look crumbly and slightly yellow. Roast in a pan at about 150 °C (300 °F) for about 3 hours, stirring every half hour. This is a home recipe; it does not name a cutting step.
- Texture / ripening: Dry, crumbly, sweet. Iwate analysis: moisture 7.8 g and protein 35.9 g per 100 g; carbohydrate and calcium higher than aaruul.
- Where / who: Nationwide; traditional curd dish. Protected status: none found.
- Sources: https://passtheflamingo.com/tag/cheese/ — home recipe (kefir curdling, dry, roast 300 °F for 3 h); adapted from a Mongolian food site; the "first milk of spring" claim is from an unnamed book; https://iwate-pu.repo.nii.ac.jp/records/1932 — eezgii composition, dried and high in protein; https://www.irci.jp/rdb/item/13427 — eezgii dried cheese from sheep and yak milk, made by fermenting raw and cooked milk; https://www.au-plovdiv.bg/docs/Razvitie_AS/DSc/2022/Culuunbat/Abstracts.pdf — eezgii compared with "eedem"; no lactic acid bacteria found in eezgii.
- Unsure: The recipe is a home method, not a documented industrial one. The sweetness is stated in the discovermongolia page; it is not in the recipe.

## Aarts (aarts; Cyrillic spelling not found in sources)
- Animal(s): Not named; made from yoghurt or from sour milk (animal unspecified).
- Kind: Fresh, semi-solid curd (cheese-like curd, drained and pressed).
- What it is: A semi-solid curd drained from yoghurt. Kept cool or frozen and eaten, or boiled into a hot drink; also the base for khuruud.
- How it is made: Heat yoghurt; drain through a cloth sack; squeeze the curd, wrap it and press it under a heavy stone until the liquid is gone. Cut into pieces about the size of a bread slice for drying (see khuruud).
- Texture / ripening: Semi-solid, moist when fresh.
- Where / who: Nationwide, herders' food. Protected status: none found.
- Sources: https://yogurtathome.com/single-post/2019/10/17/fermented-milk-products-from-all-over-the-world-khuruud-mongolia — aarts made from heated yoghurt drained in a sack and pressed under a stone; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongolian_cuisine — aarts listed as a curd from skimmed milk, slightly fermented.
- Unsure: Whether aarts is a fresh cheese or simply a curd (no fixed category in sources). It is included as a fresh curd because it is pressed and shaped.

## Khuruud (хурууд; khuruud)
- Animal(s): Not named; made from aarts (see above).
- Kind: Hard, dried curd cheese (air- and sun-dried).
- What it is: A very hard dried cheese, eaten after soaking in tea or water. Stores indefinitely; historically a food for soldiers on campaign.
- How it is made: Aarts is cut into bread-slice-sized pieces and dried in the wind and sun on the roofs of tents until fully hard through to the core.
- Texture / ripening: Very hard; "cannot be eaten as it is".
- Where / who: Nationwide, herders' food. Protected status: none found.
- Sources: https://yogurtathome.com/single-post/2019/10/17/fermented-milk-products-from-all-over-the-world-khuruud-mongolia — khuruud as very hard cheese made from aarts; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aaruul — Mongolian spellings хурууд (khuruud) and ааруул (aaruul) listed in the kashk article.
- Unsure: Sources differ on whether khuruud and aaruul are the same product or two forms of one dried curd. Here they are kept as separate entries; see Unconfirmed leads.
