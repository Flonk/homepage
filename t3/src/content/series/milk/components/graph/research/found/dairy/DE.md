## Dickmilch (Setzmilch, Stockmilch; Sauermilch in common use)
- Animal(s): cow (raw or pasteurised, homogenised)
- Kind: fermented milk (thickened sour milk)
- What it is: milk thickened by lactic acid, either from natural fermentation of raw milk or from added lactic bacteria on pasteurised milk. The base for quark and sour-milk cheese.
- How it is made: industrial versions use mesophilic Lactococcus lactis at about 22–28 °C; the milk is held at about 25–28 °C for 15–20 hours until it sets. Rennet can speed up setting.
- Where / who: Germany-wide. Northern Germany eats it with sugar and crumbled rye bread or zwieback; southern Hesse and the Hanover area serve it chilled in midsummer with cinnamon and sugar; southern Hesse and the Southern Palatinate eat it with fried potatoes in summer. Austria and Bavaria use sour milk in soups such as Stosuppe and Herbstmilchsuppe. Lower in lactose than unfermented milk.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dickmilch — definition, culture, temperatures, regional uses; https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sauermilch — disambiguation, confirms the term means Dickmilch
- Unsure: Sauermilch is also used as a name for buttermilch mixed with skim milk (Trinksauermilch), see Buttermilch.

## Buttermilch (buttermilk, incl. Trinkbuttermilch and Buttermilchpulver)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: drink / by-product of butter making; cultured variant
- What it is: the liquid left after churning butter from cream, usually under 1% fat. Also sold cultured (soured with lactic bacteria), mixed with skim milk as Sauermilch or Trinksauermilch, whipped, flavoured with fruit, or dried as powder.
- How it is made: cream from raw milk is separated by centrifuge, then churned; the liquid is the buttermilk. Sweet buttermilch can be soured by adding lactic bacteria. Standard buttermilch may have water or skim milk added; "reine Buttermilch" may not.
- Where / who: Germany, mostly industrial. Traditional farm buttermilch is less common. Austria's rule that buttermilch come from sour cream does not apply in Germany.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buttermilch — production, types, nutrition; no culinary uses or brands given
- Unsure: the article gives no culinary uses for German buttermilch, so use in dishes is not confirmed here.

## Joghurt (Naturjoghurt, Joghurt mild, Sahnejoghurt, Fruchtjoghurt, Trinkjoghurt)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: fermented milk, sold plain, flavoured, or as drinking yoghurt
- What it is: milk thickened by lactic acid bacteria. Legal tiers under the Milcherzeugnisverordnung: skim up to 0.5% fat; low-fat 1.5–1.8%; Joghurt at least 3.5%; Sahnejoghurt at least 10%; Greek-style strained up to about 10%. Fruit yoghurt is about 80% of sales.
- How it is made: traditional cultures are Lactobacillus bulgaricus and Streptococcus thermophilus at 42–45 °C; "Joghurt mild" uses other lactobacilli (e.g. L. acidophilus) at 22–30 °C and has been the norm since the early 2000s. Trinkjoghurt stays runny and is stirred smooth; stichfester yoghurt ripens in the cup. Home method: heat milk to 40–50 °C, add about two spoonfuls per litre, keep warm at least six hours.
- Where / who: Germany-wide, industrial. Fruit flavouring labels depend on fruit content (6%, 3.5%, under 3.5%).
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joghurt — legal definitions, cultures, temperatures, product types, home method
- Unsure: none

## Kefir (incl. Kefir mild, Sahnekefir)
- Animal(s): cow, goat, sheep (home); cow (industrial)
- Kind: fermented drink
- What it is: fermented milk drink. Industrial "Kefir mild" uses a defined mix of bacteria and yeasts for consistent taste. Sahnekefir is a cream variant with fruit added. Starter is kefir grains (Kefirknollen) or powdered starter (Kefirferment).
- How it is made: at home, milk is fermented 1–2 days with grains at about 10–25 °C, then the grains are strained out. Industrial product follows German dairy rules.
- Where / who: Germany; powdered starter sold in Reformhäuser and pharmacies.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kefir — names, home and industrial method, sale in Germany
- Unsure: the article does not describe production or sale in Austria or Switzerland.

## Schmand (also Schmant, Schmetten)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: soured cream (cultured cream)
- What it is: thick, spoonable soured cream with at least 20% fat.
- How it is made: cream fermented with mesophilic lactic bacteria such as Lactococcus lactis until it sours and thickens.
- Where / who: Germany, especially Thuringia, Hesse and southern Lower Saxony. Used to enrich soups and sauces, in desserts, and on Schmandkuchen (a yeast cake with fruit or quark, topped with Schmand, pudding or porridge). Also used on pizza with salmon. In some regions it means sweet coffee cream.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schmand — definition, production, German uses, regional names
- Unsure: none

## Sahne / Rahm (cream, Schlagsahne)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: cream
- What it is: the fat-rich phase of milk; Schlagsahne is the whipping cream.
- How it is made: cream rises when unhomogenised milk stands; today it is mostly separated by centrifuge, leaving skim milk at 0.03–0.06% fat, and the final fat level is set by blending cream and skim back together. Products sold as Sahne or Rahm must contain at least 10% fat (second source: de.wikipedia Rahm).
- Where / who: Germany-wide. Cream is served with hot chocolate, on Rote Grütze, in Sahnemeerrettich (horseradish cream), in sour cream forms for Frankfurter Grüne Soße, and with Ostfriesentee.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahne — only Rahm and Schlagsahne definitions; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_cuisine — uses of cream and whipped cream; https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rahm — creaming, centrifuge separation, 10% minimum fat

## Butter (Süßrahmbutter, Sauerrahmbutter, Mildgesäuerte, Salzbutter, Kräuterbutter, Landbutter, Maibutter, Halligbutter)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: butter
- What it is: churned milk fat. Butter must have at least 80% milk fat (max 16% water, 2% non-fat milk solids). Reduced-fat versions are labelled Dreiviertelfettbutter (about 60–62%) or Halbfettbutter (about 39–41%).
- How it is made: sweet-cream butter has no cultures (pH at least 6.4); sour-cream butter uses lactic bacteria (pH at most 5.1). Traditional churning: milk settled about two days, cream ripened, churned by hand at 7–8 °C for about 35 minutes. Industrial: cream pasteurised, ripened about 20 hours, then churned, worked and moulded. Over 20 litres of milk per kg.
- Where / who: Germany. Regional and traditional forms: Halligbutter (North Sea islands), Maibutter (spring pasture butter), Landbutter (farm butter, may be from raw milk under rules), Deutsche Markenbutter and Deutsche Molkereibutter (top quality grades). Historical separation methods such as Holstein, Gussand, Reimers.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butter — types, fat levels, churning temperatures and times, regional products
- Unsure: none

## Butterschmalz (clarified butter; Schmalzbutter; Geklärte Butter)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: butter fat / clarified butter
- What it is: almost pure milk fat, with water, milk protein and milk sugar removed. Solid at room temperature; smoke point about 205 °C.
- How it is made: at home, butter is kept barely liquid about 30 minutes without browning, foam and solids skimmed, clear fat poured off (about 700 g per kg of butter). Industrial: butter melted at 40–60 °C, centrifuged, heated to about 100 °C under vacuum, aerated with air or nitrogen.
- Where / who: Germany, for frying, roasting and baking. Geklärte Butter is a less pure form used for delicate sauces. Ghee is a related Indian/Pakistani product, not German.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butterschmalz — preparation, properties, German uses, related names
- Unsure: none

## Kondensmilch / Dosenmilch (evaporated and sweetened condensed milk)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: canned milk (evaporated or sweetened condensed)
- What it is: milk with part of its water removed, shelf-stable in cans, bottles, cartons or tubes. German law sets fat tiers: unsweetened full-fat at least 7.5%, coffee cream at least 15%, partly skimmed 1.0 to under 7.5%, skim up to 1%; sweetened full-fat at least 8.0%, partly skimmed 1.0 to under 8.0%, skim up to 1%.
- How it is made: heated 85–100 °C for 10–25 min; vacuum-thickened at 40–80 °C to about 60% water loss; homogenised; filled and sterilised again. Sweetened versions add up to 45% sugar after thickening.
- Where / who: Germany. Unsweetened is mainly used as coffee whitener and in sauces and desserts; sweetened is used as a spread or topping for fruit and pastries. Example brand given: Nestlé Milchmädchen (example only).
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kondensmilch — process, legal fat tiers, uses
- Unsure: none

## Trinkmilch (raw, Vorzugsmilch, pasteurised, ESL, UHT, sterilised; cow milk)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: drinking milk, by heat treatment
- What it is: legal drinking milk types. Rohmilch (raw) 3.5–5.0% fat, sold only from the farm; Vorzugsmilch (raw quality, filtered and packaged for retail) 3.5–4.0%; Vollmilch at least 3.5%; fettarme 1.5–2.7%; Magermilch up to 0.5%.
- How it is made: pasteurised usually 72–75 °C for 15–30 s, keeps about 10 days chilled; ESL 123–127 °C, about three weeks chilled; H-milk (UHT) at least 135 °C for a few seconds, months unopened at room temperature; sterilised in pack, up to a year unopened.
- Where / who: Germany-wide. Milk is a common breakfast drink. Flavoured milks (Kakao, Milchmixgetränke) are not covered by this source; the 2026 MilchPQV requires detailed labelling.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinkmilch — legal definitions, heat treatments, shelf life
- Unsure: flavoured milk products were not confirmed beyond the labelling rule.

## Molke-Getränke and Sig (whey drinks and sweet whey)
- Animal(s): cow (whey from rennet-set or lactic-set milk)
- Kind: drink / sweet / whey product
- What it is: whey used in soft drinks and as a sweet. Lattella is a whey soft drink labelled Molkenerzeugnis; Rivella is a whey-based drink mixed with fruit concentrate, spices and sugar. Sig is a sweet made by boiling sweet whey until it caramelises slightly; regionally popular.
- How it is made: sweet whey from rennet curd; sour whey from lactic fermentation. Sig: sweet whey stirred over high heat and reduced. Whey powder is used in bakeries and processing.
- Where / who: Germany and Switzerland (Swiss "Molkenkuren", whey cures, historically). A 2010 Düsseldorf court held whey-containing soft drinks were not deposit-free.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molke — whey drinks, Sig, powder, production basis
- Unsure: the Sig regional origin is not given in the source; Molkenbrot (whey bread) was not covered.

## Milchreis (rice pudding; Condéreis; Reisbrei)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: milk dish / dessert
- What it is: rice cooked in milk, served warm or cold, as a main meal or dessert. Also called "dicker Reis" or "Reisbrei".
- How it is made: round-grain rice simmered slowly in milk for about 40 minutes until creamy; oven-baked Condéreis adds vanilla, citrus zest, butter, sugar (about 3 parts sugar to 5 parts rice) and sometimes egg yolk. Instant versions are stirred into boiling milk.
- Where / who: Germany and France (Condéreis as a base for desserts such as Kaiserinreis and Malteser Reis). Served with fruit compote, applesauce, cinnamon and sugar.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milchreis — ingredients, preparation, German variants
- Unsure: none

## Speiseeis (ice cream: Cremeeis, Eiscreme, Rahmeis, Milcheis, Quarkeis, Spaghettieis, Eis am Stiel)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: frozen milk dessert
- What it is: German legal categories with minimum milk content: Cremeeis at least 50% milk and egg; Eiscreme at least 10% milk fat; Fruchteiscreme at least 8% milk fat; Rahmeis/Sahneeis at least 18% milk fat; Milcheis at least 70% milk; "-Eis" from a milk product such as Quarkeis at least 6.5% non-fat milk solids, with fermented milk over 35%.
- How it is made: custard and cream-based ice cream; cream and egg whipped separately for parfait. Spaghettieis is vanilla ice pressed through a press into strands, with strawberry sauce and white chocolate on whipped cream; the source does not classify it legally. Eis am Stiel was patented in the US in 1923 (no milk content stated).
- Where / who: Germany. Italian-run ice cream parlours spread in Germany from the mid-1850s; Spaghettieis originated in Germany.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speiseeis — legal milk-content thresholds, named German frozen desserts; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_cuisine — ice cream history and popularity
- Unsure: Spaghettieis and Eis am Stiel have no legal milk classification in the source.

## Ostfriesentee mit Sahne (East Frisian tea with cream and Kluntje)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: milk/cream tea drink
- What it is: strong black tea blend from East Frisia, served with cream and rock sugar (Kluntje).
- How it is made: a Kluntje goes into the cup, hot tea is poured over it, and cream is carefully added with a teaspoon to form a floating "Sahnewolke" (cream cloud), left unstirred.

## Ziegenmilch (goat milk)
- Animal(s): goat
- Kind: raw/fresh milk (drinking and processing)
- What it is: goat milk from dairy breeds such as Bunte Deutsche Edelziege, Weiße Deutsche Edelziege and Thüringer Waldziege. Most German goat milk goes to cheese; the article names no other German goat milk product.
- How it is made: milked by hand in small herds and by machine in larger ones. Yields: about 600 litres per year on average, 900–1,200 at the top.
- Where / who: Germany and Central Europe; sales rose in Bavaria and Switzerland in recent years.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ziegenmilch — breeds, yields, milking, sales trend

## Schafmilch (sheep milk)
- Animal(s): sheep
- Kind: raw milk, mostly processed into cheese
- What it is: milk from ewes; on average about 6.8% fat, 6.0% protein and 5.0% lactose, with more unsaturated fat, minerals and some vitamins than cow milk.
- How it is made: milked by hand or by machine (machine milking is used for Roquefort sheep). Most sheep milk is not sold fresh but turned into cheese; the article names Roquefort, Brocciu, Pecorino and Feta as examples.
- Where / who: Germany: sheep milk has little tradition in German-speaking countries, and production is small. The East Frisian Milchschaf was bred on the North Sea coast over 300 years ago, but its milk was rarely sold commercially. Today there are some full-time flocks and hundreds of hobby shepherds.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schafmilch — composition, German production, processing into cheese
- Unsure: the number of commercial German sheep dairies is not given.

## Stutenmilch (mare milk; with Kumys)
- Animal(s): horse (mare)
- Kind: raw milk; fermented drink (Kumys, Airag in Mongolia)
- What it is: milk of a mare in lactation. Lower in fat, protein and casein than cow milk, higher in lactose, and in some ways similar to human milk. Sold fresh, frozen or as freeze-dried powder.
- How it is made: mares are milked several times a day in commercial herds, with the foal allowed to suckle at times. For Kumys, lactic bacteria and yeasts turn part of the lactose into lactic acid, ethanol and carbon dioxide, giving a sour, slightly fizzy, mildly alcoholic drink. Its low casein makes rennet clotting hard, so it suits cheese less well than cow, sheep or goat milk.
- Where / who: Central Asia, Mongolia and parts of Eastern Europe. The article does not say that mare milk is produced or sold commercially in Germany.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stutenmilch — composition, milking, Kumys fermentation, uses
- Unsure: no German production or market is confirmed; Kumys is a Central Asian and Mongolian drink.

## Eselsmilch (donkey milk)
- Animal(s): donkey (female)
- Kind: raw or pasteurised milk, for drinking and infant feeding
- What it is: milk of female donkeys; low in fat and high in lactose, with a composition close to human milk. The whey is sweet-tasting.
- How it is made: the article describes pasteurised donkey milk being used as a natural hypoallergenic milk for infants with cow's-milk protein allergy, and says tolerance must be judged under medical supervision. Pule is a Serbian cheese made from donkey milk.
- Where / who: Germany: donkey milk was sold in German cities until about the late 19th century, when it was cheaper than cow milk. The article does not say it is produced in Germany now.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eselsmilch — composition, history in German cities, infant use, Pule
- Unsure: no current German production or retail is confirmed.

## Wasserbüffelmilch (water buffalo milk)
- Animal(s): water buffalo
- Kind: raw milk, high in fat
- What it is: buffalo milk has about 8% fat, twice the fat of cow milk, and a longer shelf life. Milk breeds (Murrah, Nili-Ravi, Mehsana, Italian Mediterranean) are rising in number.
- How it is made: the article gives no German method. Buffalo milk makes true buffalo mozzarella; most mozzarella is now made from cow milk.
- Where / who: Germany: about 1,800 buffalo in 2008 and 2,362 in 2010 (German Buffalo Association). One herd of about 290 on the Swabian Alb (2014), and a few breeders in Bavaria and Baden-Württemberg in 2018. Switzerland: milk processed into mozzarella at Marbach LU.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wasserbüffel — fat content, mozzarella, German herd counts and farms
- Unsure: no German buffalo milk product or processing is given; herd counts are from different years.

## Frauenmilch (donor human milk)
- Animal(s): human
- Kind: donated human milk, frozen or pasteurised
- What it is: milk given to a baby by a woman other than its mother, mainly preterm babies whose mothers do not have enough milk. Donor milk banks (Frauenmilchbanken) collect, test, store and distribute it.
- How it is made: donors are screened and questioned regularly about illness and medication. The milk is tested, stored in freezers (freeze-drying is rare), and fortified with nutrients, vitamins and trace elements before feeding. Pasteurisation has been used since 1919, when Marie-Elise Kayser set up a donor milk collection in Magdeburg and found it a simple way to keep the milk longer.
- Where / who: Germany: about 40 donor milk banks (March 2023) against 210 perinatal centres, most of the banks based at those centres. Founded in 2018, the Frauenmilchbank-Initiative aims for nationwide supply. In 1959 there were 26 banks in West Germany and over 60 in East Germany.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frauenmilchbank — definition, processing, fortification, German history and counts
- Unsure: none

## Säuglingsnahrung (infant formula)
- Animal(s): cow milk-based; soy-based formula also exists (from 1929 in the USA)
- Kind: manufactured infant formula, sold as powder
- What it is: in Germany, ready-made products are split into Pre-Nahrung (formerly adapted milk formula), whose only carbohydrate is lactose and which is close to breast milk, and 1-Nahrung (formerly partly adapted), which also contains sucrose. Preterm formulas have more carbohydrate and about 80 kcal/dl. Hydrolysed (HA) formulas have split protein for allergy prevention; heavily split therapy formulas taste bitter.
- How it is made: powder is mixed with water before feeding. The article does not describe the manufacturing process.
- Where / who: history: Justus von Liebig's infant food, first as a liquid soup in 1865 and then as a powder (Kindermehl), sold from 1866; Henri Nestlé added condensed milk and launched his version in 1868. Milupa entered in 1932 and Alete (1934) followed.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Säuglingsnahrung — German categories, preterm and HA formulas, history
- Unsure: where German formula is manufactured, and how, is not in the source.

## Milchmischerzeugnisse (flavoured milk and milk-mix drinks)
- Animal(s): cow (dairy base; whey-based versions too)
- Kind: dairy-based flavoured drinks and products
- What it is: a legal term for products made of at least 70% dairy products and at most 30% other foods. Examples: Kakaotrunk, Erdbeermilch, whey-based milk-mix drinks, fruit yoghurt, drinking yoghurt, fruit buttermilk and fruit kefir.
- How it is made: the article does not describe the process. Typical additions, in roughly descending order of volume, are sugar or sweeteners, fruit preparations or fresh fruit, binders, fibre, flavourings, caffeine, enzymes and vitamins. Ice cream and milk-based sauces are not counted as milk-mix products.
- Where / who: Germany (Milcherzeugnisverordnung, MilchErzV). Ingredient quantities must be declared under the QUID rule.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milchmischerzeugnis — legal definition, typical products, additives, labelling
- Unsure: the article does not separate flavoured milk made from raw milk from pasteurised.

## Milchpulver (milk powder: whole, skim and whey)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: dried milk product
- What it is: whole milk powder has about 26% fat, 25% protein and 38% lactose; skim milk powder about 36% protein, 52% lactose and about 4% residual water; whey powder (sweet or sour, from cheese making) about 11% protein and 70% lactose.
- How it is made: whole milk, about 87.5% water, is brought to about 3% water. It is first concentrated in evaporators, then dried by spray or roller drying. About 6–7 litres of milk make 1 kg of powder.
- Where / who: used in cheese, yoghurt, sweets and baked goods, especially chocolate, as a base for instant baby food, and for outdoor and disaster rations.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milchpulver — composition, drying methods, uses, history
- Unsure: EU intervention buying of skim milk powder is EU policy and is not written up here.

## Milchsuppe (milk soup)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: milk dish, sweet or savoury
- What it is: a dish that is now mostly sweet and often served to children; formerly it could be a savoury meal.
- How it is made: milk is boiled, sweetened or salted, and poured over pieces of white bread. Variations add flour, oats, dumplings, dough pieces, biscuits or fruit such as prunes. Polish zacierka: egg, sugar and flour are mixed into hot milk to form small dumplings, served warm or cold.
- Where / who: widespread. The article's historical example is Swiss: the Kappeler Milchsuppe of 1529, a peace meal for the First Kappel War. It names no specific German region.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milchsuppe — preparation, variations, history
- Unsure: German regional use is not in the article.

## Quarkkäulchen (quark fritters; Quarkkeulchen)
- Animal(s): cow (quark)
- Kind: fried quark-dough dish, sweet or savoury
- What it is: flat fried patties of quark dough, from Saxony (called Quarkkließle in the Vogtland).
- How it is made: dough of grated boiled potatoes, lean quark, eggs and flour. The potato-to-quark ratio varies from 3:1 to 1:1; the more potato, the lighter the dough. Seasoned with sugar, cinnamon and lemon zest or juice; raisins optional, sometimes soaked in brown rum. Shaped up to 1 cm thick and fried in hot fat until golden on both sides.
- Where / who: Saxony. Served with cinnamon sugar and compote or apple sauce, or with icing sugar and hot sour cherries. The savoury version uses chopped sausage or fried bacon, with paprika or pepper.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarkkäulchen — dough, frying, serving, savoury variant
- Unsure: none

## Herbstmilchsuppe (autumn sour-milk soup)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: sour-milk soup, eaten hot or cold
- What it is: a Bavarian peasant soup made from curdled milk.
- How it is made: autumn milk was collected in tubs and kept in the cellar until it soured and thickened. The fermented top layer was removed and replaced with fresh or curdled milk. The curdled milk was stirred with flour and poured into boiling salted water. Sweet milk, whey or buttermilk could be used, and cream was added by those who could afford it.
- Where / who: Bavaria; eaten as breakfast and evening meal, hot or cold. Also called Herbstsuppe or Rahmsuppn.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbstmilchsuppe — preparation, history, serving
- Unsure: none

## Stosuppe (sour-milk soup, Austria)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: sour-milk soup
- What it is: a simple Austrian soup of sour milk, sour cream, water and flour; known in Bavaria as Herbstmilchsuppe and in Poland as Polewka.
- How it is made: caraway is boiled in water; sour milk and sour cream are stirred smooth with flour, then whisked into the boiling water and seasoned with salt and pepper. Potatoes are optional; Erdäpfelschmarrn or Kartoffelsterz is often served with it.
- Where / who: Austria (Vienna, Lower Austria, Styria). The article says farmers ate it for breakfast until the 1930s, and it is a popular Lenten dish in Styria and Lower Austria.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stosuppe — recipe, history, regional use
