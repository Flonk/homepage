## Leche merengada (leche merengada; no other names found)
- Animal(s): cow (milk)
- Kind: drink / frozen milk drink
- What it is: A Spanish drink of milk and egg white, sweetened and spiced, served partly frozen.
- How it is made: Mix milk with egg white, sugar (or sweetener), cinnamon sticks and powder, optional lemon peel and vanilla, and a small pinch of salt, while the milk is still warm. Beat in a blender (once done by hand), then freeze until partly set with a snow-like texture. No temperatures or times given.
- Where / who: Described as a very typical drink of Spanish cuisine; the article files it under Madrid gastronomy. Some cooks add a few drops of coffee. Served cold as a dessert, snack or restorative drink.
- Sources: https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leche_merengada — origin, ingredients, method and serving.

## Leche frita (leche frita; "fried milk"; Catalan "llet de Pasqua", Basque "pella")
- Animal(s): cow (milk)
- Kind: sweet / milk dessert
- What it is: A set custard of milk and flour, cut into pieces, fried, and glazed with sugar and cinnamon.
- How it is made: Simmer milk with sugar, cinnamon sticks and sometimes lemon zest. Mix flour, sugar and egg yolks, stir in the sweetened milk, then cook over medium heat until a firm dough forms. Shape into rounds, rectangles or diamonds, coat in beaten egg, dust with flour and fry. Glaze with sugar and dust with cinnamon before serving. No temperatures given.
- Where / who: Northern Spain; origin uncertain, considered to be from the province of Palencia. Listed in the Extremadura dessert list on the Spanish cuisine page.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leche_frita — ingredients, origin, method; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_cuisine — listed as an Extremaduran dessert.

## Crema catalana (crema catalana; "crema cremada", "Crema de Sant Josep")
- Animal(s): cow (milk, egg yolks)
- Kind: milk custard dessert
- What it is: A milk custard flavoured with cinnamon and lemon zest, topped with a layer of caramelised sugar, served at room temperature.
- How it is made: Milk, egg yolks (or whole egg) and sugar; some modern versions add cornflour. The page gives no temperatures or cooking times and says it cannot be frozen successfully.
- Where / who: Catalonia. Early custards appear in the 14th- and 16th-century Catalan cookbooks, the burnt top is first documented in 1770, and the name was first used in Friar Juan de Altamiras's 1745 cookbook. Served on Saint Joseph's Day under the name Crema de Sant Josep.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crema_catalana — base, origin, method (no temperatures).

## Cuajada (cuajada; traditionally sheep, now often cow)
- Animal(s): sheep (traditional); cow (now common commercially)
- Kind: fresh curd dessert (set milk)
- What it is: A soft set curd dessert, eaten sweet, with sugar or honey and walnuts, raisins or fruit on top.
- How it is made: Raw milk coagulated with rennet (cuajo), traditionally in a clay or ceramic vessel called a kaiku. No temperatures or times given.
- Where / who: Originates in Navarre and is typical of northern Spain (Basque Country, Navarre, Castilla y León, La Rioja); its use has spread across the Iberian Peninsula. The name refers to the snowflake-like curds that set in winter. Home and commercial production.
- Sources: https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuajada — milk, method, vessel, origin, serving; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_dairy_products — listed as Spanish, with the regions named.
- Unsure: Some sources treat it as a cheese-like curd; this entry follows the dessert description.

## Natillas (natillas)
- Animal(s): cow (milk, egg yolks)
- Kind: milk custard / pourable dessert
- What it is: A thin, pourable custard, richer than flan, with cinnamon and no caramel. Often topped with a María biscuit.
- How it is made: Gently heat milk with sugar, vanilla and cinnamon; slowly stir in eggs (often just yolks) and other ingredients. The result is a thin pouring custard rather than a set one. No temperatures or times given.
- Where / who: Spain; the Spanish term is a family name for custards and sweet desserts. Home cooking and commercial (sold as ready-made desserts).
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natillas — ingredients, method, description of the Spanish version.

## Arroz con leche (arroz con leche; Asturian "arroz con lleche", Galician "arroz con leite", Basque "arroz-esne")
- Animal(s): cow (milk)
- Kind: milk pudding / rice dessert
- What it is: A rice pudding of milk, rice and sugar, cooked slowly over low heat, flavoured with orange peel in Spain.
- How it is made: Milk, rice and sugar cooked slowly on low heat; flavoured with orange zest. No temperatures or times given. In Asturias it is usually topped with sugar caramelised by a hot iron (called requemau).
- Where / who: Widely eaten across Spain; a particular tradition in Asturias. Home and commercial.
- Sources: https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arroz_con_leche — base, flavouring, Asturian top; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_cuisine — rice pudding, Asturias.

## Quesada pasiega (quesada pasiega)
- Animal(s): cow (milk, butter)
- Kind: baked milk-and-butter cake / dessert
- What it is: A lightly sweet, pudding-like cake from the Valles Pasiegos, made from curdled milk, butter and sugar; served warm or cold.
- How it is made: Mix sugar and butter, add the curdled milk, lemon and cinnamon, beat in eggs, add flour and knead. Pour into cake tins and bake for one hour at 180 °C until golden.
- Where / who: Valles Pasiegos, Cantabria; traditional home and bakery production.
- Sources: https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quesada_pasiega — ingredients (curdled milk, butter, sugar), method, baking time and temperature.
- Unsure: The article lists "cuajo de la leche" (curdled milk) as the milk ingredient, not a cheese; kept here as a dairy-based dessert.

## Torrijas, milk version (torrijas con leche; torreja, picatostes in Asturias)
- Animal(s): cow (milk)
- Kind: milk-soaked fried bread (sweet)
- What it is: Stale bread soaked in warm milk, sugar and cinnamon, coated in egg and fried, then dusted with sugar and cinnamon.
- How it is made: Warm milk with sugar and cinnamon without boiling; soak bread slices for about half an hour; coat in flour and egg; fry in hot oil; dust with sugar and cinnamon. Served warm or cool. Wine and syrup versions also exist.
- Where / who: Traditionally eaten in Spain during Lent and Holy Week; known under other names in several regions.
- Sources: https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torrija — milk method, Lent tradition, regional names.
- Unsure: Milk is one of three soaking options (milk, wine, syrup); this entry covers the milk version only, and bread rather than milk is the main ingredient.

## Chocolate a la taza (chocolate a la taza; chocolate espeso)
- Animal(s): cow (milk, when made with milk)
- Kind: thick hot chocolate drink
- What it is: A thick hot chocolate drink made from a chocolate tablet with flour (rice or corn starch) added for body.
- How it is made: The chocolate is melted in milk or water; the text gives no steps or temperatures.
- Where / who: Spain; mainly in winter, served with churros or roscón de Reyes; a standard breakfast in the 19th century.
- Sources: https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chocolate_a_la_taza — milk or water, starch, serving and tradition.

## Yogur (yogur; pasteurised and live)
- Animal(s): cow (milk); regional goat, sheep and donkey yoghurts reported in a search snippet only (see leads)
- Kind: fermented milk
- What it is: Yoghurt made by fermenting milk with live cultures.
- How it is made: The article does not give Spanish method details; it notes that live yoghurt is not heated after fermentation, while pasteurised yoghurt is heated and keeps for months without refrigeration.
- Where / who: Spanish producers disagreed on whether "yogur" should cover pasteurised yoghurt; Danone was started in Barcelona in 1919 by Isaac Carasso and named after his son (the name later became Dannon in the USA). Industrial and home (yoghurt makers) production.
- Sources: https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogur — Barcelona origin, live vs pasteurised yoghurt debate, home yoghurt makers.

## Mantequilla (mantequilla; butter; Asturian artisanal butter)
- Animal(s): cow (milk, cream; animal not stated in the source for the Asturian butter)
- Kind: butter
- What it is: Butter made by churning cream, which separates the fat from buttermilk.
- How it is made: Churning cream until the fat clumps and separates from buttermilk (suero de mantequilla); hand-churned butter is worked with wooden paddles (called "manos escocesas") to press out water. No temperatures given.
- Where / who: Local production in Galicia, Asturias and Soria, and imported butter elsewhere, per the Spanish cuisine article; an artisanal Asturian butter with engraved moulds is pictured in the Spanish article. Spain's butter scarcity noted by visitors. Lard (manteca de cerdo) was the traditional fat in southern and eastern Spain, later replaced by olive oil; this is not a dairy product.
- Sources: https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantequilla — churning method, Asturian butter caption, lard note; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_cuisine — butter scarcity and local production areas.
- Unsure: The Soria butter name appears only as a link on the Spanish article, with no description.
