## Karnemelk (karnemelk; botermelk informally)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: fermented milk drink (sour, low-fat)
- What it is: A sour, low-fat, drinkable dairy product. Traditionally the liquid left after churning soured cream into butter. Drunk year-round in the Netherlands, most in summer, though its popularity has declined.
- How it is made: Traditional: soured cream churned until the fat clumps into butter, the sour liquid kept. Modern: skimmed milk cultured with lactic acid bacteria. Biodynamic karnemelk is still made the traditional way, and some farm versions survive.
- Where / who: Netherlands; used in karnemelkse pap/gortepap, hangop and kruudmoes.
- Sources: https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karnemelk — production, uses and names; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_cuisine — buttermilk as a regional cuisine ingredient; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fermented_milk_products — karnemelk listed under Netherlands.

## Hangop
- Animal(s): cow (from yoghurt or karnemelk)
- Kind: strained yoghurt, dessert
- What it is: A dessert dairy product, a thick, quark-like yoghurt or buttermilk.
- How it is made: Liquid poured into a clean tea towel hung over a bowl or tied at the corners; left to drain overnight. No temperatures given. Increasingly made industrially.
- Where / who: Netherlands, eaten as a dessert. Sometimes confused with Jan in de zak, which differs in recipe and texture.
- Sources: https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hangop — method, dessert use and confusion with Jan in de zak; https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoghurt — listed as a main Dutch yoghurt style.

## Vla (vla; blanke vla, chocoladevla, hopjesvla and others)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: dairy dessert / custard (vla)
- What it is: A thick Dutch dairy dessert from fresh cow's milk with thickeners. Legally at least 50% cow's milk, at least 2.6% milk fat, and starch must be a characteristic ingredient. Variants include vanilla, blanke (plain), chocolate, hopjes (coffee caramel), caramel, fruit flavours, chipolata, bitterkoekje, oranjevla, wintervla and dubbelvla (two flavours in one carton).
- How it is made: Traditionally milk heated with custard or with eggs, cornstarch (maïzena), vanilla and sugar. Store-bought versions usually use other thickeners. No temperatures or times given.
- Where / who: Originally Netherlands (and Suriname). Word likely from Limburgish vlaai, the dairy filling of the vlaai in the west became called vla. Campina introduced it in Belgium in 2013 and in Germany in 2006 (en wiki says 2010 for Wallonia/Germany, the two pages disagree). Also sold in Indonesia.
- Sources: https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vla — ingredients, legal definition, history, variants, brands; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vla — origin, Campina spread to Wallonia and Germany, Indonesia; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_cuisine — vla in vlaai context.
- Unsure: The two Wikipedia pages give different years for the introduction in Germany and Belgium (2006/2013 vs 2010). Left as the nl page's figures.

## Chocomel
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: flavoured milk (chocolate milk)
- What it is: A trademarked chocolate milk, sold mainly in the Netherlands. "Chocomel" is often used in everyday speech for chocolate milk in general.
- How it is made: Partly skimmed milk, sugar, about 1.7% cocoa, carrageenan stabiliser, guar gum thickener.
- Where / who: Industrial. Made by FrieslandCampina (Aalter and Bornem, Belgium). Belgium's version was Cécémel until 2026, when the Chocomel name was adopted there.
- Sources: https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chocomel — ingredients, manufacturer, history (introduced 1932 by Martien Breij, Baarn); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_cuisine — chocomel as popular Koek-en-zopie drink.

## Anijsmelk
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: hot milk drink with aniseed
- What it is: Warm milk traditionally boiled with aniseed, typically Dutch and especially in the northern provinces, usually drunk in winter. Sugar optional.
- How it is made: Traditionally crushed aniseed simmered in milk. Modern: a ready-made anise powder or tablet dissolved in warm milk. No temperatures given.
- Where / who: Traditional, northern Netherlands. Still sold as ready-made powder sachets (De Ruijter) and tablets. Anise sugar cubes are no longer sold. Folk belief that it promotes sleep.
- Sources: https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anijsmelk — ingredients, preparation, region, products; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_cuisine — "traditional Dutch drink", now hardly drunk.

## Kruudmoes (kruutmoes; groenmoes; melkmoes)
- Animal(s): cow (buttermilk)
- Kind: savoury porridge
- What it is: An old hearty porridge for agricultural workers in Gelderland and Overijssel, with buttermilk, barley groats, bacon, smoked sausage, raisins and lots of fresh herbs (chervil, parsley, celery, fennel, dill, spearmint, sorrel).
- How it is made: Not described on the page. Can be eaten warm, lukewarm or cold.
- Where / who: Gelderland and Overijssel. Near Kampen and Genemuiden called groenmoes; on the former Zuiderzee and western Veluwe called melkmoes. Recipes vary by area.
- Sources: https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kruudmoes — ingredients, regions, serving; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_cuisine — described as a traditional buttermilk food with barley, bacon and herbs.
- Unsure: The page does not clearly say whether it is still commonly eaten today.

## Koffie verkeerd
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: milk coffee drink
- What it is: Coffee with a large amount of ordinary milk. The name ("wrong coffee") distinguishes it from coffee with a splash of milk. In Belgium, lait russe.
- How it is made: Originally at most half filter coffee and at least half warm milk. Modern cafés often use espresso or caffè lungo and frothed milk, which makes it nearly identical to a caffè latte.
- Where / who: Netherlands; the page does not say whether it originated there.
- Sources: https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koffie_verkeerd — ingredients, proportions, modern versions.

## Rijstebrij (rijstepap; dikke rijst)
- Animal(s): cow (sweetened milk)
- Kind: dish: milk-based rice pudding (sweet)
- What it is: An old sweet dish of rice cooked in sweetened milk. Optional vanilla, saffron or cinnamon; often sprinkled with candy sugar; raw egg yolks or butter for a richer version; raisins optional.
- How it is made: Rice cooked in sweetened milk for about one hour until soft; can be pre-cooked in water with salt first. No temperatures given.
- Where / who: Traditional. Called dikke rijst in the east of the Netherlands and rijstepap in Flanders and the south. Shown in Breughel's painting De boerenbruiloft.
- Sources: https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rijstebrij — ingredients, method, regional names, painting.

## Gortepap (karnemelkse gortepap)
- Animal(s): cow (buttermilk)
- Kind: dish: buttermilk porridge (dessert/dairy)
- What it is: A Dutch porridge of barley groats and buttermilk, served with sugar or syrup (stroop). Classed as a dairy product and dessert.
- How it is made: Groats soaked about 12 hours, simmered in water about an hour, buttermilk stirred in at the end.
- Where / who: Dutch. The first porridge Dutch dairy factories sold, in a milk churn labelled karnemelkse pap.
- Sources: https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gortepap — ingredients, method, history; https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karnemelkse_pap — disambiguation: the term covers this dish and karnemelkse bloempap.
- Unsure: The page does not say whether it is still commonly eaten.

## Yoghurt (roeryoghurt; standyoghurt; Griekse yoghurt)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: fermented milk
- What it is: Dutch yoghurt in its main styles: roeryoghurt (stirred, the most widely produced type in the Netherlands), standyoghurt (set in its pot), and Griekse yoghurt (strained, thicker, a milder form of hangop).
- How it is made: Milk pasteurised at 80–90 °C, cooled to 30–45 °C, inoculated with Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus and Streptococcus thermophilus. Standyoghurt: about 45 °C for 4–6 h in the pot. Roeryoghurt: 31–32 °C for 10–16 h, then stirred. Fat: full at least 3%, half-fat 1.5–1.8%, skim under 0.5%. Milk powder, casein, whey powder, gelatin or starch may be added.
- Where / who: Industrial and retail, Netherlands.
- Sources: https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoghurt — method, cultures, styles, fat rules, thickeners.

## Kwark
- Animal(s): cow (also goat, sheep)
- Kind: fresh curd (dairy dessert)
- What it is: A soft fresh curd. Historically a very fresh cheese, but the Dutch article classifies it as a dairy product (zuivelproduct) and dessert under dairy rules, and it is mostly eaten as a dessert, plain or with fruit.
- How it is made: Traditional: milk soured with lactic bacteria and rennet, about 24 h at room temperature, curd cut and drained in cloth to about a third, then stirred smooth. In the Netherlands most kwark uses yoghurt cultures and added milk ingredients, which is faster and cheaper. Full-fat kwark has cream added to reach fat rules.
- Where / who: Netherlands, industrial and home. Casein from kwark was once a binder in fresco paints.
- Sources: https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kwark — what it is, method, Dutch production, classification.
- Unsure: Borderline with cheese. Included as a dairy product because the Dutch article classes it as zuivelproduct. The cheese researcher may also list it.

## Roomboter (butter)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: butter
- What it is: Churned cream; in the Netherlands the name boter is legally protected and requires at least 80% milk fat, so margarine cannot be called boter.
- How it is made: Cream concentrated to roughly 35–40% fat by centrifuge (historically skimming, afromen). Older methods soured the cream first. Churned until the fat clumps; buttermilk (karnemelk) drained off. Kneaded to spread residual moisture, lactic bacteria added for aroma. Industrial centrifugal separators in Dutch roomboterfabrieken from about 1880.
- Where / who: Netherlands, farm and industrial. Historical cooperative dairies (zuivelcoöperaties) and butter auctions (botermijn), e.g. Maastricht 1895.
- Sources: https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roomboter — method, legal minimum, history, variants.

## Zure room (sour cream)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: soured cream
- What it is: Cream soured with lactic bacteria. About 10% milk fat in the Netherlands. Used in cold dishes, unlike the richer crème fraîche (35% or more) used warm.
- How it is made: Lactic bacteria added and left to ferment, which lets it keep longer than regular cream.
- Where / who: Netherlands, retail. Similar product smetana in Central and Eastern Europe.
- Sources: https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zure_room — fat content, method, uses.

## Slagroom (whipped cream)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: cream
- What it is: Cream of 30–40% fat. The name slagroom is legally reserved in the Netherlands for cow's milk cream with at least 30% fat. Tops coffee, hot chocolate, ice cream, pastries, strawberries and advocaat.
- How it is made: Sweetened before whipping (60–80 g fine sugar per litre); whipped well chilled until just before it turns yellow; can add skim milk powder or stabiliser.
- Where / who: Netherlands and Flanders, retail; sold in aerosol cans (nitrous oxide propellant).
- Sources: https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slagroom — fat rule, whipping method, uses.

## Gecondenseerde melk (sweetened condensed milk; evaporated milk)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: condensed milk (sweetened)
- What it is: Cow's milk with part of the water removed and sugar added, which preserves it. Popular in the Middle East; diluted for coffee.
- How it is made: Milk evaporated in a seven-stage evaporator; sugar added for flavour and preservation.
- Where / who: Industrial. Dutch maker: Coöperatieve Condensfabriek Friesland (CCF), now part of FrieslandCampina.
- Sources: https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gecondenseerde_melk — method, uses, CCF, Gail Borden 1856 patent.

## Melkpoeder (milk powder)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: dried milk
- What it is: Milk with water removed to a dry powder; base for solid block milk and condensed milk.
- How it is made: About 87% water removed by vacuum evaporation to about 50% dry matter, then spray-dried in a tall tower with hot air (droplets fall 8–30 s); cyclone separates powder, may be rewetted for granule size, sealed airtight. Drum or freeze drying also used.
- Where / who: Industrial. No Dutch producer named on the page.
- Sources: https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melkpoeder — method, uses, trade-offs.

## Roomijs (ice cream; melkijs variant)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: frozen milk dessert (ice cream)
- What it is: Consumer ice cream; roomijs is a protected term requiring a minimum milk fat (8% until 2009, now 5%). Melkijs has about 2.5% fat. Eggs can be added but then the product cannot be called roomijs. Classic dessert dame blanche (vanilla ice cream with warm chocolate sauce).
- How it is made: Cream, milk and sugar base; vanilla milk boiled with vanilla pods; churned while freezing; ripened about 12 h in the freezer; served at about -20 °C.
- Where / who: Industrial and ice cream van (ijsco) tradition. Brands on the page include Cornetto, Calippo, Magnum, Liuk, Raket, Schatkist.
- Sources: https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roomijs — types, legal minimum, method, brands and traditions.

## Wei (whey)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: by-product (whey)
- What it is: The liquid left after curdling milk with rennet in cheese making, greenish-yellow and slightly sour. Used in drinks (e.g. Rivella), as a food ingredient, in baby food, and for protein supplements.
- How it is made: A by-product of cheese making, split into retentate (protein) and permeate (mostly animal feed).
- Where / who: Industrial, Netherlands and elsewhere. Named product Molkosan (remedy promoted for digestion, no supporting research) is listed on the page.
- Sources: https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wei_(zuivel) — definition, composition, uses, products.

## Kefir (drink made in the Netherlands)
- Animal(s): cow (also goat, sheep)
- Kind: fermented milk drink
- What it is: Fermented milk drink. Traditional kefir uses grains; factory "mild" kefir uses a bacteria and yeast blend and little alcohol.
- How it is made: Traditional grains in milk at about 10–25 °C for 2–4 days; alcohol about 0.2–2%. Factory kefir: no grains, bacteria and yeast blend, about 3–4% lactose.
- Where / who: Sold in the Netherlands. The page does not say it is made there. A Keuringsdienst van Waarde broadcast reportedly found nearly all factory kefir sold in the Netherlands had no yeast and no kefir culture. The name kefir is not protected.
- Sources: https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kefir — process, Dutch factory-kefir finding and name not protected.
- Unsure: Whether real kefir is made in the Netherlands is not confirmed by the source; the Dutch mention is about factory products sold there.

## Melkunie (Melkunie Holland; Campina Melkunie)

## Karnemelkse bloempap (karnemelkse pap; bloempap; zoepenbrij in the north)
- Animal(s): cow (buttermilk)
- Kind: porridge (dish)
- What it is: An old Dutch porridge of buttermilk and flour, eaten warm with stroop (syrup) or brown or white sugar. In the north of the Netherlands it is called zoepenbrij.
- How it is made: Flour is heated in boiling buttermilk, with a little salt added to counter blandness; the flour must be heated through or it stays raw.
- Where / who: Netherlands, traditional. Related to gortepap and kruudmoes.
- Sources: https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karnemelkse_bloempap — method, serving, northern name zoepenbrij.

## Rauwe melk (raw milk)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: raw (unpasteurised) fresh milk
- What it is: Milk as it leaves the cow, the starting material for the dairy industry. The Dutch dairy herd is TB-free, so raw milk is allowed for sale. Farms may sell it, with a clear label that it must be boiled before use.
- How it is made: Not processed. Unhomogenised milk lets the cream rise quickly. Fat content varies with breed, individual cow, feed and season; raw cow milk is generally richer than standardised milk.
- Where / who: Netherlands, sold at dairy farms. The national food authority NVWA advises against drinking raw milk after several cases of illness in the early 21st century, and the Belgian FAVV does the same. NVWA wants rules to stop farm visitors drinking raw milk from cups.
- Sources: https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melk_(drank) — raw milk section: TB-free herd, sale rules, NVWA and FAVV advice, fat variation; standardisation in 1940 (Warenwetbesluit Zuivel).
- Unsure: The same article covers pasteurised and UHT milk only in passing (UHT sterilisation is mentioned), so no separate entry for those.

## Pudding (dairy dessert)
- Animal(s): cow (milk-based)
- Kind: dessert
- What it is: A usually sweet dessert made with milk or thickened with a starch product (such as maizena), with eggs, custard, custard powder or gelatine as optional setting agents. Usually made in a pudding mould.
- How it is made: Warm puddings are set with starch, sometimes with eggs; flavourings (for example almonds) are steeped in the warm milk. Cold puddings are set with agar or gelatine and finished with fruit purée, chocolate paste or whipped or custard cream (banketbakkersroom).
- Where / who: General article; not specific to the Netherlands. Named varieties include Christmas pudding, chipolata pudding and griesmeel pudding.
- Sources: https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pudding — warm and cold types, binding agents, varieties.
- Unsure: Not Dutch-specific; included because the page covers milk desserts in general. Dutch vla is covered above.
