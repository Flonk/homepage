## Brunost (brunost; mysost; "brown cheese")
- Animal(s): whey and milk/cream from goat, cow, or a mix (Norwegian and en Wikipedia); "geitemyse" for ekte geitost (no.wiki Geitost)
- Kind: whey-based brown product (caramelised whey / "mysost" family)
- What it is: the general name for Norway's sweet brown whey product. The en Wikipedia article calls it "not a true cheese". FAO and WHO do not count it as cheese (no.wiki Geitost).
- How it is made: milk, cream and/or whey are boiled slowly for several hours until most of the water evaporates; heat caramelises the milk sugars (brown colour, sweet taste). No temperatures are given. Historically Anne Hov (1863) added cream to whey and boiled it in an iron pot until under 80% fluid; later goat's milk was added. Boiling whey alone for a shorter time gives prim.
- Texture / ripening: firm but softer than Gouda, does not crumble; ready to eat once packed and refrigerated.
- Where / who: Norway; industrial dairies (Tine, Synnøve Finden) and small farm dairies since the 2000s. Low-fat versions have more whey.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brunost — production, history, names; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_cuisine — brunost/gammalost description; https://no.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geitost — brunost is made by evaporating whey, FAO/WHO classification; https://no.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myse — brunost made from goat or cow whey or a mix, boiled down; https://no.wikipedia.org/wiki/Litago — TINE children's brand that includes brunost (brand, not a cheese type)
- Unsure: "cheese" status is disputed (the sources say it is not a true cheese). The Tine Litago brunost (named on the TINE page) is a brand, not a separate type.

## Gudbrandsdalsost (also brunost or geitost in Norway; gjetost in US)
- Animal(s): cow and goat milk, cream, whey (variant G35 uses goat's milk; no.wiki says also made from pasteurised cow and goat milk)
- Kind: brunost (whey-based, caramelised)
- What it is: the most popular brunost in Norway, roughly half of brunost sales (no.wiki).
- How it is made: as for brunost: a mixture of whey, cream and milk boiled for hours until the water evaporates, caramelised. Cream was first added in 1863 to make the cheese better and easier to cut; Ole Steberg of Ringebu later added goat's milk; first industrial steam cooker at Tretten, 1908 (no.wiki).
- Texture / ripening: sweet, full, caramel-like taste; more piquant than the milder mysost (no.wiki).
- Where / who: from Sør-Fron in Gudbrandsdalen (Anne Hov, Solbråsætra); TINE sells it as "Gudbrandsdalsost G35", Synnøve Finden as "Gudbrand"; made in Lom, Skjåk, Elnesvågen, Byrkjelo, Storsteinnes; the article says production is to move to Ørsta. Also called gjetost in the US.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gudbrandsdalsost — milk mix, Anne Hov, Rusthågå farm, export to Christiania; https://no.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gudbrandsdalsost — milk, process, origin, producers; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brunost — variety naming
- Unsure: the en and no Wikipedia sources disagree on the exact goat/cow proportions; no.wiki says the process in the article applies to brunost in general.

## Fløtemysost (fløte mysost; cream whey cheese)
- Animal(s): cow's milk and cream (no goat's milk)
- Kind: brunost (whey-based, brown, mild)
- What it is: a traditional Norwegian brown whey cheese, the milder brunost made without goat's milk. About 30% of brunost sold is fløtemysost.
- How it is made: cream is added to whey and boiled down (en Wikipedia Brunost, "Historical method"); the page on fløtemysost itself does not describe the boiling.
- Where / who: Norway; Anne Hov is credited with making the first brunost, which was fløtemysost (no.wiki).
- Sources: https://no.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fløtemysost — cow's milk, cream, mild, 30% of sales; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brunost — "fløtemysost is made without goat's milk"
- Unsure: none

## Geitost (geitost, gjetost, ekte geitost)
- Animal(s): goat's milk; "ekte geitost" from goat's milk and whey only (no cow milk). Mixed goat/cow variants also called geitost.
- Kind: brunost (whey-based, brown); the pale version ("geitost av ostemasse") is a curd cheese
- What it is: the goat-milk brunost. "Ekte geitost" (true goat cheese) is the strict version. The brown type is Norway's national symbol (no.wiki).
- How it is made: brown geitost is made by evaporating whey (caramelised lactose). Ekte geitost is made from goat's milk whey, pasteurised goat's milk and goat's cream (no.wiki). Brunost in general is boiled for hours; no times given.
- Texture / ripening: sharper and tangier than cow's-milk brunost; firm, softer than Gouda, does not crumble.
- Where / who: Norway; industrial dairies since early 1900s; small farm dairies since 2000s, often raw milk; example from Veitastrond, Sogn og Fjordane (2008) (no.wiki).
- Sources: https://no.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geitost — goat milk, process, variants, producers; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gjetost — names, goat-milk types
- Unsure: the no.wiki article says the term "ekte geitost" is controversial in the craft sector.

## Prim (pålegg; "prim")
- Animal(s): not stated (made from whey mixture; cream or milk not stated)

## Gammelost (gammalost, gamalost; "old cheese")
- Animal(s): cow's milk (skimmed)
- Kind: sour-milk cheese, mould-ripened (blue-green/brown mould)
- What it is: a traditional, very pungent Norwegian cheese, over-matured. It is high in ACE-inhibiting peptides and is studied for blood-pressure effects.
- How it is made: skimmed cow's milk is soured with a lactic starter, then gently heated; curds are separated from the whey and pressed into forms. The mould is either from the wooden walls of a dedicated form or rubbed on by hand (traditional method). Ripening takes about 4–5 weeks, from the outside inward. Result is firm, moist, coarse, often granular, brownish-yellow. No salt is used (per the trial paper). Production is mainly in Hardanger and Sogn; commercial production centred at TINE's Vik facility.
- Texture / ripening: firm, moist, coarse, granular; ripening outside inward; bitter taste and crumbly texture (trial paper)
- Where / who: Hardanger and Sogn (Norway); TINE, Vik; rarely exported
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gammelost — milk, mould method, ripening, texture, regions, TINE Vik; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC4976624/fullTextXML — trial of Gamalost: skimmed milk, no salt, <1% fat, ~50% protein, ACE peptides, intake in Norway; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_cuisine — described as over-matured, pungent; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.3168/jds.2012-5993&format=json&resultType=core — Qureshi et al. 2013 abstract: Gamalost ACE-inhibitory activity after digestion, peptides mainly from β-casein
- Unsure: the en Wikipedia page spells it "Gammalost" in places. The production account the trial cites (Qureshi et al. 2012) could not be opened (Springer redirect to login).

## Pultost (pultost; "soft sour-milk cheese")
- Animal(s): not stated (sour skimmed milk)
- Kind: acid-set (sour-milk) cheese; soft and mature; spreadable and grainy variants
- What it is: a traditional Norwegian sour-milk cheese flavoured with caraway seeds.
- How it is made: made from skimmed milk that has been soured in a way similar to cultured buttermilk; acid-set. Heating and curd handling not described. Preserved with salt. Byproducts of prim production were formerly used.
- Texture / ripening: two variants: the spreadable one is softer and stronger; the other is grainy.
- Where / who: Norway; Tine's Nybergsund dairy in Trysil municipality; Synnøve Finden processes the curd further.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pultost — skimmed sour milk, caraway, salt, variants, Nybergsund; https://no.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ost — acid-set, traditional sour-milk cheese; https://no.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prim_(pålegg) — byproduct of prim used for pultost
- Unsure: ripening not described in the sources.

## Jarlsberg (Jarlsberg cheese)
- Animal(s): cow's milk
- Kind: pressed, semi-hard, propionic-eyed cheese (Swiss-type); mild, large regular holes
- What it is: Norway's best-known export cheese, developed in Norway in 1956 by Ole Martin Ystgaard at the Dairy Institute of the Agricultural University of Norway, based partly on 1850s recipes and Swiss 1830s recipes.
- How it is made: holes ("eyes") come from Propionibacterium freudenreichii, added back under a trade-secret culture formula. The page gives no rennet, cutting, cooking, brining or temperature steps.
- Texture / ripening: aged at least 3 months, some versions 9, 12 or 15 months. Usually 10 kg wheels (about 330 mm across, 95–105 mm high), yellow wax rind, semi-firm yellow interior.
- Where / who: Norway; trademark owned by Tine SA (formula is a trade secret). Also made in Ireland (Dairygold) under licence and in Ohio, USA (Alpine Cheese Co.) from frozen Norwegian culture. No PDO status stated.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jarlsberg_cheese — culture, ageing, form, history, trademark, licence production; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_cheeses#Norway — cow's milk, mild, large holes
- Unsure: the 2016 Int Dairy J paper (abstract opened, DOI 10.1016/j.idairyj.2015.10.005) studies a Norwegian semi-hard washed-curd cheese with a propionic adjunct from two commercial dairies; the abstract does not name the cheese, so it is not counted here as Jarlsberg.

## Norzola
- Animal(s): not stated
- Kind: blue-mould cheese (blåmuggost)
- What it is: a Norwegian blue cheese, milder and smoother than Gorgonzola, with fat at 60% of dry matter; developed after Norway voted against joining the EC in 1972.
- How it is made: blue-green marbling is less pronounced than in Normanna; ripening not described. From the 1980s wrapped in gold foil and vacuum-packed in 175 g pieces.
- Where / who: TINE; launched 1974 by Norske Meieriers Salgssentral; developed at Snertingdal, early production at Sparbu, from 1987 at Selbu.
- Sources: https://no.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norzola — blue cheese, TINE, history, fat content; https://no.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ost — blue-mould cheese in Norwegian specialties
- Unsure: milk type not stated.

## Skjørost (skyr; surmjølkost)
- Animal(s): skimmed cow's milk ("skumma mjølk")
- Kind: fresh, acid-set (sour-milk) cheese
- What it is: a traditional Norwegian sour-milk cheese; associated with the Røros area, served with tjukkmjølk, rømme, sometimes a little sugar, with cured meats (spekemat).
- How it is made: milk is soured about 17–18 hours, then gently heated until it "cracks" (sprekker); the curd is lifted out at the right time; separated by souring alone, by kjernemjølk, or by pure lactic cultures. Pressing and salting not described.
- Where / who: Røros area, Norway; Røros Meieri's organic product page is the only source (no producer named).
- Sources: https://no.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skjørost — souring, heating, separation, origin, Røros; https://no.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ost — skjørost as a fresh and acid-set cheese
- Unsure: no pressing or salting step confirmed.

## Kvarg
- Animal(s): cow's milk (pasteurised skim milk; cream may be added)
- Kind: fresh, acid-set cheese (quark-type)
- What it is: a Norwegian fresh cheese, similar to kesam; the en Wikipedia article is about German quark and lists Norwegian and Swedish "kvarg" as cognates.
- How it is made: milk is soured with lactic cultures (modern dairies often add some rennet); curd is drained (traditionally in cloth; industrially by centrifuge). Heating is not mentioned.
- Texture / fat: soft, white, unaged, usually unsalted; drier and more sour than kesam; ~0.2% fat in basic quark (German law figures).
- Where / who: Norway; no.wiki lists kvarg among fresh cheeses.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kvarg (redirects to quark) — process, fat content, cognates (via redirect from Kvarg); https://no.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ost — kvarg as acid-set fresh cheese
- Unsure: the en article covers German quark; Norwegian kvarg details come from Norwegian pages only.
