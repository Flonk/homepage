## Cajeta (cajeta de Celaya; goat-milk dulce de leche)
- Animal(s): goat (cajeta); cow for generic dulce de leche
- Kind: sweet / milk caramel
- What it is: A thick, caramel-coloured milk-and-sugar confection. In Mexico and some Central American countries, dulce de leche made with goat's milk is called cajeta. Cajeta de Celaya is named as a well-known Mexican version.
- How it is made: Simmer milk and sugar, stirring almost constantly until the sugar dissolves; baking soda may be added. Keep stirring for about 1.5–2 hours until thick and golden-brown; roughly a sixth of the original volume remains. Colour and flavour come from caramelisation and the Maillard reaction. Vanilla is optional. Pressure-cooker variant uses sweetened condensed milk for 20–45 minutes depending on the desired firmness (source gives no temperatures).
- Where / who: Mexico (named Mexican confection); Celaya is named as the famous centre. Home and industrial.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cajeta — describes goat-milk cajeta, Celaya, and the stovetop/pressure-cooker methods; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dulce_de_leche — confirms cajeta as the Mexican/Central American name for goat-milk dulce de leche, a photo of Mexican production in a copper vessel, and cajeta de Celaya as a Mexican dessert; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cajeta_de_Celaya — goat's milk the usual base; in Celaya a confection of half goat's and half cow's milk became known as cajeta
- Unsure: The Mexican-specific method is not separately sourced; the method given is the general dulce de leche process.

## Rompope (Mexican eggnog-style milk drink)
- Animal(s): cow (milk; the source does not name the animal)
- Kind: drink (alcoholic milk and egg drink)
- What it is: A yellow, emulsified drink of milk, egg yolk, sugar and vanilla, with rum. Cinnamon, nuts or strawberry are sometimes added. The Spanish word is said to derive from "rompón", and it is compared with eggnog.
- How it is made: The source gives no steps, temperatures or times. It notes only that egg yolks give the yellow colour.
- Where / who: Traditionally associated with the convents of Puebla, said to date from the 17th century (Convento de Santa Clara). Now made commercially across Mexico and also at home.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rompope — ingredients (eggs, milk, vanilla, rum, sugar), convent origin in Puebla, commercial and home production; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_cuisine — rompope named, with Puebla convent origin
- Unsure: The Mexican-cuisine page says the convent origin is "believed"; the rompope article presents it as tradition. Cream is not named in the rompope article.

## Chongos zamoranos (chongos zamoranos)
- Animal(s): cow's milk (the source says "milk" only)
- Kind: milk dessert; a milk curd
- What it is: A Mexican dessert of curdled milk, giving a soft, cheese-like curd in a sweet brown milky syrup; its origin is attributed to colonial-era convents in Zamora, Michoacán.
- How it is made: Milk curdled with rennet tablets, then cooked with sugar and cinnamon. Times and temperatures are not given in the source.
- Where / who: Zamora, Michoacán; convent origin attributed.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chongos_zamoranos — dessert of milk curdled with rennet tablets, with milk, sugar and cinnamon, in a sweet brown syrup; origin attributed to Zamora convents
- Unsure: Animal not named in the source; the convent origin is "attributed", not proven.

## Champurrado (chocolate atole, made with milk or water)
- Animal(s): cow's milk where milk is used (animal not named)
- Kind: warm, thick drink (chocolate atole)
- What it is: A warm, thick chocolate drink made from masa or corn flour, served with churros in the morning and at Day of the Dead and Las Posadas with tamales.
- How it is made: Masa, masa harina or corn flour with piloncillo, water or milk, and chocolate, sometimes cinnamon, aniseed or vanilla; whipped with a wooden molinillo or a blender until frothy.
- Where / who: Mexico; made at home and sold.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Champurrado — chocolate atole with "water or milk", ingredients, molinillo whisking, service with churros and tamales
- Unsure: Milk is one option in the recipe; how common milk is in Mexican home or commercial versions is not stated.
