## Matsoni (მაწონი; მაწვნი in genitive; Armenian: մածուն)
- Animal(s): cow (mostly), water buffalo, goat, sheep, or a mix
- Kind: fermented milk (yoghurt-like, lactic acid)
- What it is: traditional Georgian sour milk product. Armenian origin is claimed by Armenia as well; Georgian matsoni has been a protected geographical indication since 24 January 2012.
- How it is made: warm milk (about ½ litre) is inoculated with one level teaspoon of matsoni, first mixed with a little milk, then combined in a jar and kept warm for several hours. Each batch uses part of the previous one as starter (unbroken chain). Starter bacteria are local lactic strains; no fermentation temperature given.
- Where / who: across Georgia, traditional jar method; whether home or industrial is not stated on the page. Commercial history: sold in New England from 1929 (Colombosian creamery, Andover, MA), which is not Georgian.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matsoni — origin, PGI date, culture, Armenia/Caspian yoghurt comparison; https://ka.wikipedia.org/wiki/მაწონი — starter ratio, method, animals, name variants; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_cuisine — matsoni in Georgian dishes; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fermented_milk_products — lists matsoni under Georgia; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12785569/fullTextXML — matzoon/matsuni described as a traditional Armenian and Georgian fermented dairy product made from milk and a starter (second pass).
- Unsure: Georgian and Armenian claims of origin conflict; the 2012 PGI is per English Wikipedia, citing Sakpatenti (the national IP centre); no temperature or time for the culture.

## Nadughi (ნადუღი)
- Animal(s): not named (made from whey)
- Kind: soft curd-like dairy product from whey albumin
- What it is: a smooth, milky-white, soft curd with a cream tint and a taste typical of albumin. Eaten salted, or with mint. Described as a long-established national dairy product.
- How it is made: whey is normalised by fat, heated and acidified, boiled, and the curd pressed out; then cooled, packed, chilled, stored. No temperatures or times given.
- Where / who: traditionally western Georgia (Adjara/West); producer type not stated.
- Sources: https://ka.wikipedia.org/wiki/ნადუღი — description and method; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_cuisine — nadughi as ingredient in Adjaran sinori.
- Unsure: animal not stated; method is from a Georgian encyclopedic entry with a "source needed" flag on the method.

## Kaimaghi (ყაიმაღი; Adjaran კაიმაღი)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: milk skin / clotted-cream type product (Kaymak family)
- What it is: thick, creamy product, about 60% milk fat, similar to clotted cream; eaten at breakfast or as dessert in the Caucasus and beyond. In Adjara it is the local version.
- How it is made: raw milk is simmered slowly for about two hours on very low heat, cooled, and the cream skimmed and chilled for several hours or days while it mildly ferments. No temperature given. A separate Adjaran method leaves milk two days to let cream rise, then the cream is churned for butter (Georgian Wikipedia, Achara section).
- Where / who: made in homes in the mountain municipalities of Keda, Shuakhevi and Khulo (Adjara); eaten with cheese or bread; rarely in restaurants.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaymak — Georgian name and Adjara details; https://ka.wikipedia.org/wiki/ქართული_სამზარეულო — Achara method.
- Unsure: the Georgian-language page for the product returned 404, so only the English article and the cuisine article were read.

## Arazhani (არაჟანი)
- Animal(s): not stated
- Kind: cultured cream (lactic-fermented cream), crème-fraîche type
- What it is: lactic-fermented cream, similar to crème fraîche but less thick and less sour; unlike Western crème fraîche it does not curdle in hot dishes, per the Georgian article. Highest grade 36% fat, first grade 30%.
- How it is made: pasteurised cream cooled to 18–20 °C, inoculated with a pure lactic culture; left 1–2 days at 2–6 °C after thickening. Alternative method: fresh butter and skim-milk emulsion.
- Where / who: the Georgian Wikipedia page describes the product but ties it to Central and Eastern Europe, not Georgia, and names no producers.
- Sources: https://ka.wikipedia.org/wiki/არაჟანი — description and method.
- Unsure: the page does not say arazhani is made or eaten in Georgia; kept as an entry because the Georgian article exists, but Georgian provenance is unconfirmed.

## Butter (კარაქი)
- Animal(s): mainly cow; also goat, buffalo, sheep
- Kind: butter
- What it is: water-in-fat emulsion from milk cream; 60–80% fat; about 20–25 L milk per kg (historically a symbol of prosperity).
- How it is made: traditional churning in a wooden barrel, the sadghvebi (სადღვები), hung from ceiling ropes and rocked until butter separates from do (buttermilk). Home production described; industrial method not given.
- Where / who: Georgia-wide, households.
- Sources: https://ka.wikipedia.org/wiki/კარაქი — definition, animals, sadghvebi churn, buttermilk do; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_cuisine — butter on adjaruli khachapuri.
- Unsure: the Georgian article's sources are the Codex and national standard.

## Ghee / clarified butter (ერბო)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: clarified butter (ghee / butter oil)
- What it is: pure milk fat freed from plasma; about 98% fat; white or pale yellow, soft.
- How it is made: butter is boiled for a long time; white sediment forms; liquid is strained through a sieve, reboiled, salted to taste, poured hot into a container to cool. Traditionally made from butter that has turned. No temperatures or times given.
- Where / who: Georgia-wide; home vs industrial not described.
- Sources: https://ka.wikipedia.org/wiki/ერბო — composition, method, Georgian source cited (Georgian Soviet Encyclopedia, 1979); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_cuisine — ghee in borano, khavitsi, Kotori khachapuri.
- Unsure: the Georgian page's method carries a "source needed" flag.

## Kurut (ყურუთი)
- Animal(s): not stated (milk, cream and whey based)
- Kind: dried dense loaf made from curd and cream by-products (not cheese per the Georgian article; its cheese status is unclear)
- What it is: dense, oblong, dried loaf of nadughi, corn flour, milk and kaimaghi (cream), in Adjarian cuisine. Also a dried product from boiled cream after butter removal (Achara), eaten with corn bread instead of cheese.
- How it is made: nadughi dried and drained, soaked in hot whey, bagged and pressed for several days; mixed with hot kakia, kneaded with corn flour rubbed with butter; shaped into loaves and sun-dried in the mountains; stored in a gvarda. No salt amount or drying time given.
- Where / who: Adjara; mountain households.
- Sources: https://ka.wikipedia.org/wiki/ყურუთი — description, method, Adjara placement, Ethnological Dictionary of the Georgian People (Batumi, 2018); https://ka.wikipedia.org/wiki/ქართული_სამზარეულო — Achara dried cream product.
- Unsure: whether it is cheese, dried curd or whey product is not stated.

## Khacho (ხაჭო; curd / cottage-type curd)
- Animal(s): not stated
- Kind: fermented curd (cottage-cheese-type); dried curd balls also used (khachoerbo is listed as cheese on English Wikipedia and is excluded)
- What it is: dairy product obtained by lactic acid fermentation; sold in semi-fat, fat-free and full-fat forms; used in khachapuri (kotori) and vareniki and khinkali fillings.
- How it is made: lactic fermentation; no further steps given on the Georgian page.
- Where / who: Georgia-wide; industrial and home.

## Kefir (კეფირი)
- Animal(s): any mammal's milk; cow, goat, sheep named
- Kind: fermented milk drink (kefir grains)
- What it is: fermented drink with kefir grains; attributed to the Karachay and Balkar peoples and the Elbrus area; widespread in the post-Soviet space.
- How it is made: traditionally 1–3 nights in goatskin bags hung by the door; modern: kefir grains in milk for 12–24 h in the dark, then strained with stainless steel tubes. Not temperature given.
- Where / who: the Georgian sources do not say kefir is a Georgian national product or made in Georgia; Georgian Wikipedia mentions a Tbilisi doctor in 1867 describing its benefits.
- Sources: https://ka.wikipedia.org/wiki/კეფირი — origin, method, sources; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kefir — Caucasus origin and North Caucasus usage; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12731747/fullTextXML — traditional account that kefir grains came from the Georgian Caucasus and spread from the Georgian mountains (second pass; a legend-type narrative, not a production source).
- Unsure: no Georgia-specific production source found; the Caucasus link is North Caucasus, not Georgia. The second source places the origin narrative in Georgia but does not say kefir is made there now.
