## Bryndza (bryndza; Slovak and Polish; also "Liptauer" in Austria)
- Animal(s): sheep (Slovak bryndza must contain at least 50% sheep milk; sheep-only is also made); some versions include cow milk; goat and cow are listed in the Wikipedia infobox
- Kind: fresh-to-matured white cheese, crumbly to soft and spreadable (modern spreadable form dates to the late 18th century)
- What it is: a white, crumbly, slightly moist sheep cheese with a tangy, strong flavour and a noticeable butyric-acid taste; central to Slovak cooking, especially bryndzové halušky (the national dish) and bryndzové pirohy.
- How it is made: the Wikipedia page does not give coagulant, salting, ripening time or temperature. The ewe-whey paper (PubMed 40153355) says it is made from raw ewes' milk by small and medium producers and that the previous batch's whey is used as a natural starter; it isolated lactobacilli and lactococci from that whey.
- Texture / ripening: crumbly, slightly moist, matured (Wikipedia)
- Where / who: Central and Eastern Europe, especially Slovakia and Poland; the name is borrowed from Romanian, probably via migrating Vlachs; recorded in Slovakia in 1470 and in Poland's Podhale in 1527; popularised in the Habsburg monarchy by entrepreneurs from Stará Turá; in Austria called Liptauer. Protected status: Slovak bryndza is a PGI, registered 16 July 2008; Bryndza Podhalańska (Poland) is a PDO, registered 11 June 2007.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryndza — description, milk, history, PGI/PDO registration dates, Liptauer name; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slovak_cuisine — bryndza used in bryndzové halušky, pirohy and soup; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40153355/ (via Europe PMC/NCBI efetch, PMID 40153355) — ewes' whey and natural starters in raw ewes' milk bryndza from 8 producers; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%28slovak%20OR%20slovakia%29%20AND%20cheese — listing that surfaced the paper; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8005922/fullTextXML — second pass: the same bryndza paper (Open Life Sci 2021, DeGruyter DOI 10.1515/biol-2021-0038) opened; microbiota of sheep bryndza, no making steps
- Unsure: the Wikipedia infobox says sheep, goat and cow milk, while the text says Slovak bryndza must be at least 50% sheep. Both are recorded here; the making steps are not confirmed from a source.

## Oštiepok (oštiepok; Slovak; cognate oscypek in Polish Goral areas)
- Animal(s): sheep, cow, or mixed milk (sources differ on the mix; the Wikipedia page says sheep, the cookslovak page says sheep or cow)
- Kind: stretched-curd, semi-hard, pliable hot-worked cheese; smoked or unsmoked
- What it is: a Slovak smoked or unsmoked sheep or cow cheese made in mountain shepherd huts (salashe), shaped in hand-carved wooden moulds; the Wikipedia page lists it under stretched-curd cheeses.
- How it is made: worked at 80–90 °C, then pulled into threads and plaited, rolled into small drums, or pressed in carved wooden moulds (cookslovak; which method applies to which cheese is not stated). Salting is typical and the taste varies with place and season. The Wikipedia page does not give temperatures or times. Second pass (PMC13328557, Sci Rep 2026, Slovak University of Agriculture in Nitra): in shepherd-hut (salaš) production the steps are curdling, shaping, salting, drying and cold smoking; industrial production adds pasteurisation, starter culture, curd stretching, pressing, acidification, steaming, shaping, salting, drying and smoking. Slovenský oštiepok (EU PGI, Regulation 943/2008) is made from raw sheep, cow or mixed milk and has at least 48% dry matter and 38% fat in dry matter.
- Texture / ripening: semi-hard, salty; the Polish Goral oscypek is similar but differs in ingredient ratios and process (Wikipedia)
- Where / who: mountainous Slovakia; still made the traditional way at Slovak salashes and small mountain farms (cookslovak; Zázrivá Salash named as a place to see it made). Protected status: Oštiepok is a protected trade name in the EU (Wikipedia).
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O%C5%A1tiepok — milk types, smoked/unsmoked, stretched-curd category, EU protected trade name, Goral oscypek comparison; https://cookslovak.com/2016/06/21/traditional-slovak-cheeses/ — hot-working at 80–90 °C, pulling and plaiting, moulds, mountain salashe, salt taste; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC13328557/fullTextXML — second pass: artisanal and industrial process steps, PGI rules, milk types, dry-matter and fat minimums
- Unsure: the milk (sheep vs cow vs mixed) differs between sources; the Wikipedia page does not give the making steps.

## Parenica (parenica; Slovak)
- Animal(s): originally unpasteurised sheep milk (1800s); modern versions use cow milk or mixtures
- Kind: semi-firm, non-ripening, semi-fat, steamed and usually smoked; a non-smoked version is also made
- What it is: a Slovak steamed cheese shaped in strips and woven into snail-like spirals; typical rolls about 100 g.
- How it is made: steamed (which darkens its cream-yellow colour), then shaped in strips and woven into spirals; usually smoked. Coagulant and salting are not stated in the source.
- Texture / ripening: semi-firm, non-ripening (Wikipedia)
- Where / who: Slovakia; no specific region named. Protected status: "Slovenská parenica" is a protected trade name under the EU PGI scheme (Wikipedia). The Spectator article on the EU protection (2008) could not be opened (redirected to a blocked host), so the 2008 date rests on the search snippet only.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parenica — milk, steamed, semi-firm, spiral shaping, roll weight, EU protected trade name; https://cookslovak.com/2016/06/21/traditional-slovak-cheeses/ — listed alongside korbáčik and oštiepok as still made at salashes
- Unsure: the 2008 EU protection date is not confirmed from an opened page.

## Korbáčik / korbáčiky (korbáčiky, korbáčik; Slovak; "cheese strings")
- Animal(s): sheep or cow (cookslovak)
- Kind: pliable, hot-worked, stretched-curd cheese
- What it is: a Slovak cheese pulled into long threads and plaited; sold today as a snack for children (Liptov brand steamed version named in search results).
- How it is made: worked at 80–90 °C, pulled into long threads and plaited (cookslovak). Salting and ripening are not stated.
- Texture / ripening: pliable, salty (cookslovak)
- Where / who: mountainous Slovakia, made at salashe and small mountain farms (cookslovak)
- Sources: https://cookslovak.com/2016/06/21/traditional-slovak-cheeses/ — hot-working, plaiting, mountain production
- Unsure: the Wikipedia and cookslovak pages treat the three cheeses together; the step details may apply to all three rather than to korbáčik alone.

## Ewe lump cheese (ovčí hrudkový syr; Slovak "ewe lump cheese", traditional specialty-guaranteed)
- Animal(s): sheep (ewe's milk)
- Kind: fresh/drained lump cheese (soft, unripened or briefly drained)
- What it is: a traditional Slovak sheep lump cheese made by many small producers across Slovakia; the 2025 J Dairy Sci study frames it as a traditional specialty-guaranteed cheese for which national specifications are being developed.
- How it is made: the abstract reports composition (casein 15.33–23.07%, fat 18.31–31.08%, dry matter 35.52–55.15%, ash 1.76–3.80%) but not the production steps. A 2023 Foods paper (PMC10743011) says a private-seller ewe cheese was allowed to drain at 18 °C and then cut into portions; sheep lump cheese is stored vacuum-sealed at 4 °C for 15 days in that study.
- Texture / ripening: soft, lump; not described beyond the composition figures
- Where / who: sold by private sellers and small producers across Slovakia (e.g., Turčianske Teplice sample); sheep's milk collected in Slovakia is small, about 100 tonnes a year per the 2024 Foods paper (PMC11677667)
- Sources: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39778803/ (PMID 39778803, J Dairy Sci 2025) — composition ranges of traditional Slovak ewe lump cheese from producers across Slovakia, described as traditional specialty-guaranteed cheese; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC10743011/fullTextXML — ewe cheese from Turčianske Teplice, drained at 18 °C, cut into 25 g portions, 4 °C storage; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11677667/fullTextXML — sheep's-milk soft rennet-curd cheese with wild garlic, Slovak University of Agriculture, collected ewe milk about 100 t/year
- Unsure: the production steps (coagulant, cutting, salting) are not confirmed in an opened source.

## Tvaroh (tvaroh; Slovak "quark / farmer's cheese")
- Animal(s): cow milk (sk.wikipedia: made from skimmed cow's milk)
- Kind: fresh curd cheese (quark-like)
- What it is: a fresh curd cheese used in many traditional Slovak dishes, including tvarohové rezance (cottage-cheese noodles), štedrák (Christmas cake layers) and zlaté halušky (semolina dumplings).
- How it is made: second pass, Slovak Wikipedia "Tvaroh": all unripened cheeses made from skimmed cow's milk; a dairy product of creamy taste, firmer consistency and usually white colour. No steps or temperatures are given (https://sk.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tvaroh)
- Texture / ripening: fresh, not ripened; it is brown only if mixed with cinnamon or honey (cookslovak comment, which is an unverified reader remark)
- Where / who: Slovakia-wide household use
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slovak_cuisine — tvaroh (quark/farmer's cheese) used in široké rezance s tvarohom, štedrák and zlaté halušky; https://cookslovak.com/2016/06/21/traditional-slovak-cheeses/ — tvaroh mentioned in a reader comment only
- Unsure: the sk.wikipedia article is tagged as lacking references and gives no steps; the cookslovak mention is a reader comment.

## Liptauer (liptauer; Šmirkás in Slovak; spicy cheese spread)
- Animal(s): sheep milk cheese (bryndza makes up about a third of traditional recipes); goat cheese, quark or cottage cheese also used
- Kind: spiced cheese spread (a spread, not a cheese proper)
- What it is: a spicy cheese spread from Slovak, Austrian and Hungarian cuisine, listed in Wikipedia's Slovak cheeses category.
- How it is made: soft cheese (bryndza, quark, cottage or goat cheese) mixed with sour cream, butter or margarine, finely chopped onion, chives and paprika, sometimes beer; the Slovak name Šmirkás comes from German Schmierkäse (spread cheese). Households keep their own recipes.
- Where / who: Slovakia (as Šmirkás), Austria, Hungary, Serbia and Slovenia; ready-made versions come in small tinfoil packs.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liptauer — cheese spread from Slovak, Austrian and Hungarian cuisine; ingredients; Šmirkás name; traditional bryndza share; household recipes; Slovak cheeses category.
- Unsure: a spread, so it may fall outside cheese proper; the Wikipedia article's regional and recipe sections carry citation-needed tags.
