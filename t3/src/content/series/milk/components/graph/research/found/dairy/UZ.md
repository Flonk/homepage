## Qatiq (qatiq; Latin "qatiq", Cyrillic not shown in source)
- Animal(s): cow most commonly; goat and camel also used in Uzbekistan (per uz.wikipedia)
- Kind: fermented milk (thick yoghurt-like)
- What it is: A thick fermented milk, more solid than ayran, from the Turkic countries and Bulgaria. Its base for several other products (suzma, ayron, sariyog, chalop).
- How it is made: Boil the milk, cool to 30–35 °C, add about one tablespoon of qatiq or smetana per litre as starter, cover and keep warm; ready after 4–5 hours (uz.wikipedia). En.wikipedia gives 6–10 hours in a warm place, and adds red beet or cherry for colour in some versions. Keeps 2–3 days in a cool place. Ivitilgan qatiq is made from boiled milk.
- Where / who: Turkic peoples of Central Asia, home-made; widely eaten in Uzbekistan
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qatiq — qatiq as a Turkic-countries product, 6–10 h method, links to suzma and chalop; https://uz.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qatiq — cow, goat and camel milk in Uzbekistan, 30–35 °C method, 4–5 h, starter ratio; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uzbek_cuisine — qatiq named among Uzbek dishes
- Unsure: The English and Uzbek articles give different times (6–10 h versus 4–5 h); both are kept.

## Ayron (ayran; Cyrillic Айран in other-language interwiki)
- Animal(s): not stated (made from qatiq, so cow, goat or camel)
- Kind: drink (yoghurt-based, salted or plain, sometimes carbonated)
- What it is: A drink made from qatiq. It is the liquid left after the fat is churned out of qatiq.
- How it is made: Churn qatiq in a kuv (also called guppi) to separate out the fat; keep the liquid that remains as ayron. Strain part of it through a cloth bag to get suzma. No times given.
- Where / who: Uzbekistan; the article also mentions its Central Asian form (yoghurt, water, salt, sometimes mint or carbonation). Home-made.
- Sources: https://uz.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayron — ayron as the liquid after churning qatiq, churning method, suzma and qurt steps, the name chalob in some regions; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayran — Central Asian ayran as yoghurt mixed with chilled water and salt, not Uzbek-specific
- Unsure: The Uzbek article and the English article disagree on the base (qatiq versus yoghurt). Ayron, the Uzbek drink, may overlap with the Central Asian ayran named in other sources.

## Chalop (chalap, chalob; Kyrgyz chalap, Kazakh shalap)
- Animal(s): not stated (qatiq, suzma or yoghurt base)
- Kind: drink or cold soup (fermented milk with salt and vegetables)
- What it is: A chilled dairy drink made from qatiq or suzma, eaten as a light dish or beverage. In Uzbek preparations vegetables are added, which makes it look like a cold soup.
- How it is made: Mixed with salt and, in modern versions, carbonated water. The English article gives no step-by-step method. The vegetable detail is marked as needing citation there.
- Where / who: Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan and Kazakhstan; linked to rural and nomadic life
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalap — drink of Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan and Kazakhstan, qatiq or suzma base, vegetables in Uzbek versions; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qatiq — "chalop is a soup made from qatiq in Uzbekistan"
- Unsure: The vegetable detail is marked as needing a citation in the source.

## Suzma (chak(k)a, süzme; Uzbek Latin "suzma")
- Animal(s): not stated (made from qatiq)
- Kind: fermented milk (strained yoghurt, soft cheese-like)
- What it is: Qatiq drained through a cloth bag, leaving a thick, spreadable product. Eaten across Uzbek, Tajik, Tatar, Afghan and other Central Asian cuisines.
- How it is made: Strain qatiq in a bag. The English article gives no timings.
- Where / who: Uzbekistan and Central Asia; home-made
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzma — obtained by draining qatiq, eaten in Uzbek cuisine; https://uz.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qatiq — suzma as thick qatiq strained through a bag; https://uz.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayron — suzma from strained ayron, chakki name
- Unsure: Relationship to chakka (strained yoghurt, Central Asian and Iranian) is stated but not shown for Uzbek practice.

## Qurut (qurt; Uzbek and Tajik qurut, Kyrgyz kurut, Kazakh qurt)
- Animal(s): not stated (made from qatiq, suzma or yoghurt; goat milk for some forms elsewhere)
- Kind: dried fermented milk (dried curd or sour-milk product, not a cheese in the sources)
- What it is: A dried sour-milk product, made by drying strained yoghurt further into balls, strips or chunks. It is dissolved in water for dishes such as qurutob in Tajikistan. In Uzbekistan it is sold as balls at markets (Chorsu Bazaar, Tashkent).
- How it is made: Strain yoghurt or qatiq, shape, dry in the sun over weeks (traditional). Modern: blend, boil, strain, ferment in a warm oven for several days, then salt. The Uzbek qurt article is about butterfly larvae and is not relevant.
- Where / who: Uzbekistan and Central Asia; home-made and sold in markets
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qurut — dried strained yoghurt, traditional and modern method, Uzbek name "qurt", Chorsu Bazaar photo; https://uz.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayron — qurt made from suzma; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aaruul — qurut treated as a form of kashk, Uzbek cuisine link
- Unsure: Some sources would class this as a dried cheese; included here as a dried curd product. Cheese-category overlap is for the cheese researcher.

## Sariyog (sariyogʻ; also maska yogʻ) — butter
- Animal(s): not stated (made from qatiq, so cow, goat or camel)
- Kind: butter
- What it is: Butter from qatiq, by churning qatiq.
- How it is made: Churn qatiq in a kuv (guppi) to separate out the fat; the fat is the butter and the remaining liquid is ayron. No temperatures given.
- Where / who: Uzbekistan; home-made
- Sources: https://uz.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qatiq — sariyogʻ (maska yogʻ) made by churning qatiq; https://uz.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayron — churning qatiq in a kuv to separate fat
- Unsure: The source names butter only briefly; no detail on yield or ageing.

## Qaymoq (qaymoq; cream)
- Animal(s): not stated in the source
- Kind: cream
- What it is: Cream, traditionally skimmed from milk. Today made at 10%, 20% or 35% fat, pasteurised and sold in jars or cartons. Most of it is turned into butter, sour cream and ice cream.
- How it is made: Before centrifugal separators, milk stood 10–12 hours at low temperature in clay bowls ("special vessels, such as clay bowls used in Uzbekistan") and the cream rose. Industrial: pasteurised, chilled rapidly to 4–6 °C, packed.
- Where / who: Uzbekistan and Central Asia; home (clay bowl) and industrial
- Sources: https://uz.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qaymoq — traditional settling in clay bowls in Uzbekistan, fat levels, industrial processing, composition; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaymak — Uzbek name qaymoq, Central Asia origin
- Unsure: The Uzbek article gives "qaymoq" in Latin only. It is probably the same food as balqaymaq (clotted cream); see that entry.

## Balqaymaq (balqaymaq; Kazakh balqaimaq, Kyrgyz balkaymak)
- Animal(s): not stated in the source (cow or buffalo milk in the wider Turkic-world product)
- Kind: clotted cream (dairy product rich and creamy)
- What it is: Described in the atlas paper as "a dairy product similar to clotted cream, rich and creamy in texture", with the Uzbek name Balqaymaq.
- How it is made: Not described in the source. Kaymak elsewhere: heat raw milk slowly for about two hours, remove the cream that forms, chill for hours or days (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaymak).
- Where / who: Central Asia and Turkic peoples; Uzbek name in the atlas
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12608619/fullTextXML — Balqaymaq (Uzb, Tgk, Tuk), clotted cream description; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaymak — clotted-cream method, Central Asia origin and Uzbek name
- Unsure: Likely the same product as qaymoq; kept as a separate entry because the sources name them differently and the Uzbek method is unclear.

## Qimiz (qimiz; kumis)
- Animal(s): mare
- Kind: fermented drink (fizzy, mildly alcoholic)
- What it is: Mare's milk fermented with lactic acid bacteria and yeasts. Drunk by nomadic herding peoples; foams when poured.
- How it is made: Mix mare's milk with lactic acid bacteria and yeast in leather vessels called meshlar, in which it is stored and carried. Mares are milked about every three hours. Commercial versions in Central Asia may use cow milk with sugar.
- Where / who: Uzbekistan and Central Asia; nomadic and herding peoples; home-made
- Sources: https://uz.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qimiz — mare's milk in leather meshlar, milking every three hours, nomadic peoples; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kumis — Uzbek name qimiz in a footnote, fermentation details, Central Asian steppe peoples; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12608619/fullTextXML — Qimiz (Uzb, Tgk) as a fermented mare's milk drink
- Unsure: Sources differ on how much Uzbekistan itself is central to qimiz; the English article names no Uzbek people or regions.

## Qumron (Uzbek name for shubat, chal; camel milk)
- Animal(s): camel
- Kind: fermented drink (sour, slightly sparkling)
- What it is: Fermented camel milk, called shubat (Kazakh), chal (Turkmen, Uzbek, Kazakh), khoormog (Mongolian) or qumron (Uzbek). Described as sparkling and sour; resembles a drinkable yoghurt.
- How it is made: Sour camel milk in a skin bag or ceramic jar using previously soured milk as starter; fresh milk is added over three to four days; mature drink holds roughly one-third to one-fifth soured milk. Camel milk takes about 8 hours to sour at 30 °C, compared with 3 hours for cow milk.
- Where / who: Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, Kazakhstan; Turkic peoples (Uzbeks, Turkmen, Kazakhs) and Mongolic peoples
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12608619/fullTextXML — Qumron (Uzb) as the Uzbek name for the camel-milk fermented drink; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shubat — chal (shubat) as camel milk drink, Uzbekistan as one of three origins, method, Turkic peoples
- Unsure: The English article gives Uzbekistan as one origin among three; the Uzbek name qumron is from the atlas paper only.

## Tan (tan)
- Animal(s): cow or goat (per the atlas paper)
- Kind: fermented drink (salted, like ayran)
- What it is: A fermented dairy drink made with a starter culture of lactic acid bacteria, dairy yeast, water and salt; named in Kazakh, Uzbek, Kyrgyz, Tajik and Turkmen.
- How it is made: Cow or goat milk, starter culture, water and salt; no timings given in the source.
- Where / who: Uzbekistan and Central Asia

## Shirchoy (shirchay, shurchay; salty milk tea)
- Animal(s): cow (milk or cream version; animal not stated, milk presumed cow)
- Kind: milk tea drink (hot, salted, with butter or cream)
- What it is: A hot breakfast drink made from tea and milk, with salt. Served in cups on the breakfast table; traditional for festive occasions.
- How it is made: Milk version: boil about 0.5 l water, add 2–3 tsp dry tea, add 2.5 l milk and a little salt, boil 8–10 min. Cream version: boil 1.5–2 l water with tea, add 1 kg cream and salt, boil 3–4 min. Source describes the cream version as the richer form.
- Where / who: Uzbekistan; home-made
- Sources: https://uz.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shirchoy — ingredients, two versions, method, breakfast use; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uzbek_cuisine — shirchoy as a salt milk tea with butter, served on festive occasions
- Unsure: The Uzbek Cyrillic name is not confirmed on the page; the Russian interwiki gives Шурчай. The atlas lists "Sutli choy" (sweet milk tea) separately; see leads.

## Muzqaymoq (muzqaymoq; ice cream)
- Animal(s): cow (milk and cream)
- Kind: frozen milk dessert
- What it is: Ice cream. The atlas lists Uzbek name muzqaymoq. The Uzbek article gives a frozen sweet made from eggs, cream or fruit.
- How it is made: Cream-based version: whisk eggs or yolks with sugar, add warm milk, vanillin, freeze. Ratio for 1 kg batch: about 600 g milk, 75 g cream, 250 g sugar, 3 eggs. Fruit version: fruit puree with syrup and citric acid. Industrial stabilisers listed.
- Where / who: Uzbekistan; industrial and home
- Sources: https://uz.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muzqaymoq — cream and fruit versions, recipe quantities, industrial ingredients; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12608619/fullTextXML — Ice Cream, Muzqaymoq (Uzb)
- Unsure: The Uzbek article does not describe a traditional Uzbek form; it may be a generic industrial ice cream.

## Durda (durda; Uzbek Latin, Cyrillic not shown)
- Animal(s): not stated (made from sariyog', the butter; cow butter is the usual base)
- Kind: butter and qatiq dish (snack spread, eaten with bread)
- What it is: A traditional Uzbek dish of butter and qatiq. The top forms golden lumps and a brown layer forms below. Sour, pleasant, eaten with bread and sometimes added to dishes. The Uzbek article gives "durda" first as the residue left when butter is melted.
- How it is made: Melt butter over low heat, add qatiq and salt, and boil. Ready when the top forms golden lumps and a brown sediment forms underneath.
- Where / who: Uzbekistan; home-made
- Sources: https://uz.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durda — definition, butter and qatiq method, salt, use with bread
- Unsure: Dish sense from the Uzbek article only; no commercial product found.

## Katyk (katyk; Uzbek katiq, Russian katyk)
- Animal(s): cow or goat (fresh milk)
- Kind: fermented milk (soured milk, drinkable)
- What it is: Fresh cow or goat milk fermented with lactic acid bacteria, with a distinct taste, smell and consistency. It is the base for suzma, qurut and katykli soup in Uzbek cooking.
- How it is made: Fermented with lactic acid bacteria; the cuisine page gives no temperature or time (see qatiq for the Uzbek home method).
- Where / who: Uzbekistan; home-made and sold
- Sources: https://www.orexca.com/uzbekistan/cuisine/dairy-products.htm — katyk from fresh cow or goat milk, lactic bacteria, katykli soup
- Unsure: Source is a cuisine page; katyk and qatiq are treated here as close relatives, not confirmed as the same product.

## Pishirilgan sut (baked milk; cooked milk)
- Animal(s): cow (unskimmed milk, about 6% fat)
- Kind: heat-treated milk (yellowish, keeps longer, does not sour)
- What it is: Milk that is heated for a long time, giving a yellowish colour and longer keeping than raw milk. Used to make red qatiq and in confectionery.
- How it is made: Unskimmed milk is boiled, then held warm at 80–85 °C for 4 hours, or at 90–95 °C for 2–3 hours.
- Where / who: Uzbekistan; home and dairy
- Sources: https://uz.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pishirilgan_sut — definition, 6% fat, times and temperatures, red qatiq and confectionery use
- Unsure: No Uzbek production volume or producer named in the source.

## Atsidofilin (atsidofilin; acidophilin qatiq)
- Animal(s): cow (pasteurised or boiled milk base)
- Kind: fermented milk (a type of qatiq)
- What it is: A qatiq made with acidophilus bacteria. Sweeter than plain qatiq; 3.2% fat. Given as diet food in medicine and widely used in veterinary practice for calves. Acidophilus milk and paste are also made industrially.
- How it is made: Pasteurised or boiled milk is leavened with acidophilus organisms. Lactic acid, streptococci, kefir fungus or vanillin are sometimes added, with sugar.
- Where / who: Described on Uzbek Wikipedia; no Uzbek producer named
- Sources: https://uz.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atsidofilin — definition, 3.2% fat, method, diet and calf use
- Unsure: The article is generic and cites Russian sources; Uzbek production not confirmed.

## Quyultirilgan sut (condensed milk; evaporated and sweetened)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: condensed milk (partly dried milk, sometimes sweetened)
- What it is: Cow milk from which part of the water is removed, with other processing. In Russia it is usually sweetened with sugar. Used in tea and coffee, as a cream or sauce, and in breads, biscuits, ice cream and sweets.
- How it is made: Water is partly removed from cow milk, and sugar is added in the sweetened version. The article gives no step-by-step method.
- Where / who: Uzbekistan; industrial
- Sources: https://uz.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quyultirilgan_sut — definition, sugar in the Russian product, uses in tea, sauce and confectionery, history (Borden patent 1856)
- Unsure: Uzbek producers and factories not named in the source.
