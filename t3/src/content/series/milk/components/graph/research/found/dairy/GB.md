## Clotted cream (Cornish clotted cream; Devon clotted cream)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: cream (heat-treated)
- What it is: thick, very high-fat cream served with scones and jam in a cream tea; a product of South West England.
- How it is made: traditionally, milk is left in shallow pans for several hours so the cream rises, heated over hot cinders or in a water bath, cooled slowly, and the clots skimmed off with a reamer. Modern float-cream method: double cream floats on milk in shallow trays, heated by steam or very hot water for up to an hour, cooled 12 hours or more, then separated. The UK counts the result as pasteurised for legal purposes. Minimum 55% butterfat.
- Where / who: Devon and Cornwall; industrial dairies and farms. Cornish clotted cream is a PDO (EU 1998, later UK law) and requires Cornish milk. Devon has no PDO for the cream itself; a 2010 campaign sought one for the "Devon cream tea".
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clotted_cream — method, PDO status, history (Tavistock Abbey early 14th century; "clouted cream" in 1658 recipe book); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cream_tea — cream tea use and Devon/Cornish dispute.
- Unsure: the Devon milk-origin rule is not stated in the source.

## Syllabub
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: dessert and drink (curdled cream or milk)
- What it is: sweetened cream or milk curdled with wine or cider, sometimes thick enough to eat with a spoon.
- How it is made: 18th-century recipe (Hannah Glasse) uses a quart of thick cream, half a pint of sack, citrus juice, lemon peel and sugar, whipped together and poured into glasses; the curd separates and rises.
- Where / who: Britain; known in England since at least 1537; popular 16th to 19th centuries.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syllabub — ingredients, method, history, English record.

## Posset
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: milk drink (historic) and cream dessert (modern)
- What it is: English milk curdled with wine or ale, a medieval and early-modern hot remedy; later a cold, set cream and lemon dessert similar to syllabub.
- How it is made: milk boiled and mixed with wine or ale to curdle, spiced with nutmeg and cinnamon; later versions use citrus, cream, sugar and eggs.
- Where / who: England; the word is in 15th-century English sources.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posset — method, ingredients, history, English origin.

## Cranachan
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: dessert
- What it is: traditional Scottish dessert of whipped cream, toasted oats soaked in whisky, honey and raspberries.
- How it is made: oatmeal toasted and soaked in whisky overnight; cream, whisky and honey combined and layered with raspberries and oats.
- Where / who: Scotland; earlier versions used crowdie cheese in place of or with the cream; now served year-round for special occasions.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cranachan — ingredients, method, origin, history with crowdie.

## Blaand (bland)
- Animal(s): cow (whey)
- Kind: fermented whey drink
- What it is: a fermented milk product made from whey, acidic with very low alcohol.
- How it is made: whey diluted with water and left to sour, or whey soured in large vats with some mixed with water before drinking.
- Where / who: farmers' home product in Scotland, Iceland, Faroes and Norway for centuries; once the most common daily drink there; never commercialised; production ended in the mid-20th century.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blaand — method, regions, end of production.
- Unsure: the page does not mention Orkney, Shetland or the Hebrides as production areas.

## Caudle
- Animal(s): cow (eggs and milk in some versions)
- Kind: alcoholic hot drink
- What it is: thick, sweet, usually alcoholic hot drink given to invalids and new mothers, from the Middle Ages to Victorian times.
- How it is made: medieval versions heat wine or ale with breadcrumbs or starch, sweeten and spice, thicken with egg yolks; later versions resemble oatmeal gruel or eggnog.
- Where / who: British cooking; custom largely died out in England around 1850.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caudle — ingredients, method, history.
- Unsure: milk is an ingredient only in some versions.

## Milk punch
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: alcoholic milk drink
- What it is: cold drink of milk, brandy or whisky, sugar and vanilla, topped with nutmeg.
- How it is made: shake spirit and sugar with ice, strain, add ice and milk; clarified versions curdle the milk and strain it off.
- Where / who: earliest record is in a 1688 travelogue of the Scottish isle of Iona; royal warrant for a bottling company in 1838; now common in New Orleans and the Deep South.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milk_punch — method, Scottish record, British popularity.
- Unsure: the source does not give an English origin; the Scottish record is the earliest known.

## English custard
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: sweet sauce or dessert
- What it is: milk or cream thickened with egg yolks, sugar and vanilla (cornflour sometimes added).
- How it is made: cooked in a bain-marie or gently in a pan, kept below about 80 °C; starts to set at about 70 °C; a 3–6 °C rise can curdle it.
- Where / who: Britain; custard tarts popular in the Middle Ages (The Forme of Cury); served with trifle, sponge puddings and as crème anglaise.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Custard — ingredients, temperatures, history.

## British rice pudding
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: baked or stovetop milk pudding
- What it is: pudding rice cooked slowly in milk with cream or butter and sugar, topped with nutmeg.
- How it is made: stovetop: simmer milk and rice until tender, stir in sugar and cream. Oven: low heat for a few hours until a thick crust forms; evaporated milk gives a richer result. Northern English variant uses butter, a pinch of salt, and rests about an hour before cooking so it can be sliced.
- Where / who: Britain; earliest recipes called whitepot, from the Tudor period.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rice_pudding — ingredients, methods, Northern variant, history.

## Condensed and evaporated milk (UK uses)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: canned milk (sweetened condensed or evaporated)
- What it is: milk concentrated by evaporation, with sugar added for condensed milk.
- How it is made: milk standardised, heated to 85–90 °C for several seconds, water evaporated, sugar added to a 9:11 ratio, then cooled and lactose crystallisation induced.
- Where / who: industrial; English engineer William Newton added sugar in 1835; a condenser unit in Buckinghamshire is mentioned in WWI; UK uses in tablet, banoffee pie, gypsy tart and fudge.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensed_milk — process, 1835 sugar addition, UK uses.
- Unsure: the source does not describe British producers of condensed milk.
