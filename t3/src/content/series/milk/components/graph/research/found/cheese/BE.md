## Herve (Herve cheese; Limburger; Fromage de Herve; Limburgse kaas, Hervekaas; Land van Herve)
- Animal(s): Cow (raw or pasteurised milk)
- Kind: Rind-washed soft cheese (washed-rind)
- What it is: A pungent, rind-washed cow's-milk cheese from the Herve area, east of Liège. Sold as a brick or cube. It is the cheese the English-language "Limburger" name points to, though the name is also used for the style made elsewhere.
- How it is made: Fresh milk curdled with a little rennet (a source describes rennet from an unfed calf). The whey drains off, the curd is lightly pressed and cut into squares, briefly dried, then brined. It is turned and washed daily during about 8–10 days of ripening in the dairy cellar, where red bacteria (Brevibacterium linens) develop the rind and the smell. It then finishes at room temperature under a glass cover. The French PDO page gives 4–5 weeks for mild ("doux") and 7–8 weeks for sharp ("piquant") versions, with the cheese turned several times; hand-salted or brine-soaked. Cutting and cooking details are not stated in the sources.
- Texture / ripening: Firm and crumbly when young, softening at the edges by about six weeks and spreadable at about three months. Young cheese is sweet; older cheese is spicy. Ripening times differ between sources (en Wikipedia infobox 3 weeks–2 months vs. text about 3 months; fr PDO page 4–8 weeks).
- Where / who: Made in the Herve country (Liège province, Wallonia). Protected: EU PDO "Fromage de Herve" (AOP in Wallonia since 1996); milk from the delimited zone (Vesdre, Ourthe, Meuse and borders). PDO page says at least 45% fat in dry matter; three producers, more than 500 t a year. Dutch sources say Herve was made in the Land van Herve from the 15th century and that the old Duchy of Limburg is the root of the name. The Limburger name and the style are also used in Germany, the Netherlands and the United States, but those are not Belgian.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herve_cheese — milk, rind wash, Herve as a PDO name; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limburger_cheese — Herve origin, ripening, texture; https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fromage_de_Herve — AOP status, zone, milk, salting, ripening weeks, producers; https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limburgse_kaas — rennet, drying, 8–10 day ripening, Land van Herve, Hervekaas naming; https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hervekaas — second source for the method: raw cow's milk, calf rennet, curd cut and pressed, brined, turned and washed daily for 8–10 days, red bacteria in the cellar; names the Herve producer Terre de fromages.
- Unsure: Ripening duration differs across sources. The Dutch page says the Dutch cheese-making tiling rule partly explains why it is barely made in southern Dutch Limburg. "Limburger" is the same cheese in Belgium but the same name is used for other countries' styles.

## Remoudou (r'moûdou)
- Animal(s): Cow (second milking, taken about 15 minutes after the usual one, per the source)
- Kind: Rind-washed soft cheese (Herve-area variety)
- What it is: A small Walloon cheese from the Land of Herve, sold in pieces of 200–500 g. Its name comes from the Walloon verb "rimoûd" (to re-milk).
- How it is made: Not described beyond the second milking. Washing with salt gives a strong taste; washing with milk keeps it mild.
- Texture / ripening: Not described in the source read.
- Where / who: Herve country, Belgium. Historically traded at the Leipzig and Frankfurt fairs; Herve merchants (called "haverlings" in Lorraine) sold it. A Dutch source describes "remoudou" as a richer, more complex Herve variety, used in Maastricht and the surrounding area. No protected status in the source read (the PDO covers the Herve name).
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remoudou — second milking, size, washing, history; https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limburgse_kaas — remoudou as a variety of Herve.
- Unsure: Whether Remoudou falls under the Fromage de Herve PDO or is a separate product is not clear from the sources.

## Brussels cheese (Brusselse kaas; fromage de Bruxelles; (h)ettekeis)
- Animal(s): Cow (skimmed cow's milk)
- Kind: Hard, washed-rind table cheese; dried-curd-like ripening on planks
- What it is: A traditional Brabant cheese named after the Brussels market. Smooth and sharp, with a strong, salty bite; nicknamed "stinkkaas". Traditionally eaten on bread at breakfast with strong coffee or Oude Geuze.
- How it is made: Skimmed cow's milk curdled without refrigeration; the curd is cut and rested 14–20 hours, then bagged so the whey drains for several days. The curd is kneaded, salted and shaped into balls of about 1 kg, then dried on wooden planks in an attic for several weeks until hard. Airborne bacteria form a greasy yellowish rind. Ripened 2–4 months with regular washing in salt water and turning in a humid, mild room; at the end the rind is brushed off and the cheese is bathed in brine and soaked in lukewarm water.
- Texture / ripening: Starts hard; washing softens it to a translucent yellowish look. Ripening 2–4 months (nl) or at least three months (en).
- Where / who: Traditionally made on dairy farms in Brabant around Brussels. Most commercial "Brusselse kaas" is now mass-produced in Herve under brand names; small traditional producers remain but are hard to find. No formal protection; a regional recognition from 2005 was withdrawn when the Zuun dairy closed in 2007.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brussels_cheese — milk, rounds or tubs, washing, local names; https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brusselse_kaas — skimmed milk, curd process, planks, ripening, producers, regional recognition.
- Unsure: The English and Dutch pages disagree on the ripening duration (three months minimum vs. 2–4 months), and the Dutch page says current commercial versions are not considered authentic.

## Passendale
- Animal(s): Cow (pasteurised)
- Kind: Semi-hard, pressed-curd, semi-cooked cheese (loaf-shaped)
- What it is: A Belgian semi-hard cheese, loaf-shaped, with a hard brown rind with white spots, golden interior with small holes, firm, moist and creamy, mild and slightly sweet. Often used in France as a stand-in for Tilsit.
- How it is made: Semi-cooked pressed curd; pasteurised milk. Further process steps are not given in the sources read. Classic's rind is brushed before packaging.
- Texture / ripening: Semi-hard, light and airy with small holes. Bel Age version is aged six months in the maker's cellars for a richer flavour; the standard types are not aged that long, and their duration is not stated.
- Where / who: Made in Passendale (near Zonnebeke, West Flanders). The Donck-Spruytte family founded it in 1932; the company later became part of Campina (1991), then Bongrain/Savencia (2007). It is the second-leading cheese brand in the Belgian market; production remains in Flanders. Varieties: Classic, Prelude, Bel Age, Fruitig, Caractère, Lightesse, Jong; Père Joseph has its own article. Commercial brand; no protected designation.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passendale_cheese — milk, shape, rind, versions, makers; https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passendale_(fromage) — semi-cooked, pressed curd, Tilsit substitute, Savencia; https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passendale_(kaas) — makers, varieties.
- Unsure: Fat content and ripening times are not given consistently.

## Maredsous
- Animal(s): Cow (pasteurised)
- Kind: Semi-hard washed-rind (brine-washed, orange rind)
- What it is: A loaf-shaped semi-hard cheese named after the Maredsous Abbey (Anhée, Namur province), with a firm orange rind and pungent aroma; sliceable, 45% fat per the English page.
- How it is made: Lightly pressed, then washed in brine by hand every two days, starting with the oldest cheeses; the wash water carries cellar flora to the younger ones. Ripened in the cellars at about 12 °C and 95% humidity. The English page gives about 22 days in the cellar.
- Texture / ripening: Firm, orange, pungent; about 22 days (en) per the English page.
- Where / who: Named after the Belgian abbey and ripened there since the early 1950s (fr page); the fr page says it is made mainly at Cléry-le-Petit in the Meuse department of France. Groupe Bel produced it; in 2017 Bel sold the Cléry-le-Petit factory and the Maredsous ripening cellar to Schreiber Foods. The abbey still makes some traditional sizes (390 g, 800 g, 2.5 kg) and a range of varieties (Tradition, Mi-Vieux, Fumé, Fondu, Frais, Light, Fagotin). No protected status mentioned.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maredsous_cheese — brine washing, 22-day maturing, abbey cellar, Groupe Bel, 45% fat; https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maredsous_(fromage) — production site in France, cellar conditions, Schreiber sale, abbey ripening since 1953.
- Unsure: Country of origin is listed as Belgium and France on different pages; the fr page says the cheese is made in France and ripened in Belgium. The English page says the abbey makes it (traditional sizes), while the fr page says only some products are still ripened on site.

## Nazareth
- Animal(s): Cow (pasteurised)
- Kind: Hard cheese (dark-rind)
- What it is: A hard Belgian cheese with a dark brown rind and a mild, subtle flavour, named after the village of Nazareth in East Flanders. Fat content 45%. Used in some quick-service restaurants as "kaas croketten".
- How it is made: Pasteurised cow's milk; no process steps given in the source read.
- Texture / ripening: Hard; ripening not given.
- Where / who: Now produced in West Flanders by Belgomilk (Milcobel group). No protected status; no abbey connection in the source read.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazareth_cheese — milk, rind, flavour, producer, fat content.
- Unsure: The source does not say whether the name ties to a monastery; the name is taken from the village.

## Chimay (Grand Cru; Chimay with beer; Grand Classic; Old Chimay)
- Animal(s): Cow (pasteurised milk per the Grand Cru); the page says the cows that supply the milk eat recycled solids from the beer mash
- Kind: Semi-hard pressed cheese (Grand Classic); beer-washed (rind soaked in Chimay beer)
- What it is: Cheeses made by the Trappist monastery at Scourmont Abbey (Chimay) since 1876. Four varieties offered as of 2010, including a beer-washed cheese and a hard, aged "Old Chimay".
- How it is made: Semi-hard pressed cheese; the rind of the beer cheese is soaked in Chimay beer. Process steps beyond that are not described in the sources read.
- Texture / ripening: Grand Cru matured six weeks; Old Chimay is a hard cheese matured at least six months.
- Where / who: Made by the Trappist monastery at Scourmont Abbey. No protected status in the source read.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chimay_Brewery — monastery cheese since 1876, beer mash fed to cows, beer-soaked rind, Grand Cru (6 weeks), Old Chimay (6 months), varieties.
- Unsure: Milk type is stated only for the Grand Cru. The Wikipedia pages do not describe the Chimay cheeses in detail.

## Maquée
- Animal(s): Cow (per the French Wikipedia infobox)
- Kind: Fresh cheese (fromage blanc)
- What it is: A fresh Walloon cheese, eaten salted or sweet; listed on French Wikipedia as a "fromage blanc".
- How it is made: Curds form (flocculate) while buttermilk or whey boils. The curds are drained in bags and crumbled before use. In the Pays de Herve it was ladled into a pyramid-shaped mould called a prihel; in La Gleize the buttermilk was heated slowly until it thickened, then strained through cloth.
- Where / who: Wallonia and Belgium (Pays de Herve, La Gleize), per French Wikipedia; listed on the French Wikipedia list of Belgian cheeses.
- Sources: https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fromage_belge — listed as fromage blanc; https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maquée — Walloon fresh cheese, cow's milk (infobox), flocculation on boiling buttermilk or whey, drained in bags, prihel mould, La Gleize method, salted or sweet.
- Unsure: Salting during production, ripening, protected status and current producers are not stated on the page.

## Floreffe (triple-cream)
- Animal(s): Cow (non-fermented whole milk)
- Kind: Triple-cream soft cheese, no rind
- What it is: A Belgian triple-cream cheese, traditionally 200 g. The article says it is close to Remoudou and Crâmeû, and that there is no connection to Floreffe Abbey despite the shared name.
- How it is made: Non-fermented whole milk, with crème fraîche added to bring the fat content to 75%. Curd handling, pressing and ripening are not described.
- Where / who: Belgium (region not stated on the page); no producers named.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floreffe_cheese — triple cream, whole milk, crème fraîche to 75% fat, no rind, 200 g, no abbey connection; listed in the Belgian cheeses navbox at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limburger_cheese.
- Unsure: The Wikipedia article cites no sources; producers and protected status not confirmed.
