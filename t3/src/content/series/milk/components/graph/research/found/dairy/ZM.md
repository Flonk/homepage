## Mabisi (Zambian fermented milk; "sour milk")
- Animal(s): cow (zebu cattle; raw cow milk)
- Kind: fermented milk, drink or thick yoghurt-like food
- What it is: traditional spontaneously fermented raw milk, the main Zambian traditional dairy product. Drunk on its own as a drink or as a thick yoghurt-like mix, or eaten with rice, sweet potato, pumpkin or leftover nsima. Often used in place of relish. Sometimes offered to visitors. Its aroma is buttery, caramel and fruity, from diacetyl, acetoin and esters; lactic acid bacteria (Lactococcus, Lactobacillus) dominate.
- How it is made: raw milk is poured into a container (calabash or gourd traditionally; now often plastic bucket, metal can or pot, or earthen pot) covered with a lid and left in a cool place at ambient temperature, about 22–33 °C, for about 24 h to 2 days or more. Ends when it thickens, tastes sour, separates whey, or gets lumpy. Whey may be drained off before stirring. Variants differ in steps: see the variant entries below. In the Moonga 2019 national survey, the typical fermentation was about 48 h. Fermentation is slower in cold months (June–July) and faster in heat; a cold waterbath is used in the hot months (Sept–Nov).
- Where / who: traditionally made in Southern, Western and Central provinces, where cattle numbers are high. The 2016 national sampling (168 samples, eight provinces) found it made and eaten widely in the Southern and Western provinces, and much less in Eastern Province. Mentioned by Ila, Tonga, Lozi (Barotse) and other groups. Home-made and sold by farmers and traders. Industrial/commercial production is not documented in these sources.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6417723/fullTextXML — national survey of production methods, container types, the 48 h fermentation, the seven method types and the cool-place lid practice; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11687773/fullTextXML — foodscape interviews in three provinces: uses, sharing with visitors, nsima and sweet potato mixes, Western/Southern/Eastern differences; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7406715/fullTextXML — 168 samples across eight provinces, ambient temperatures, containers and producers; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11795106/fullTextXML — aroma and microbes of four variants, ambient 22–33 °C; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1007/s11274-020-02957-5&format=json&resultType=core — field trial (abstract): buckets vs calabashes, back-slopping, no difference in diversity; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%22Traditional%20fermented%20milk%20product%20from%20Zambia%20shifts%22&format=json&resultType=core — SHIME gut model abstract: fed to gut model, shifts microbiota, heat-treated mabisi still had an effect.
- Unsure: "mabisi" is also used loosely for any sour milk. Its scientific status as a fully controlled product is not established; it is spontaneously fermented.

## Tonga mabisi
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: fermented milk
- What it is: the most common mabisi type, produced in all provinces and by all main ethnic groups interviewed. Named after the Tonga people, who are most associated with the method. Left to ferment to a stiff, sour product.
- How it is made: milked by hand into a bucket (plastic or metal; older practice was a gourd or earthen pot). Left covered in a cool place until sour, about a day, then fermented to a thick clot and stirred or sieved. Whey is partly drained. Ends when product is thick, sour, or lumpy. Variants: some producers ferment longer for a stiffer, more sour product, others stop earlier for a more watery, drinking-yoghurt product. Tonga mabisi had the most diverse bacterial community of the variants, dominated by Lactococcus.
- Where / who: all provinces; Tonga and many other groups; home and small-scale production.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6417723/fullTextXML — seven method types, tonga type process and whey/instant uses; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7406715/fullTextXML — tonga most popular and most diverse community; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11687773/fullTextXML — stiff/sour vs drinking-yoghurt preference; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%22Influence%20of%20fermentation%20temperature%20on%20microbial%20community%22&format=json&resultType=core — abstract (second pass): tonga mabisi fermented at 20–30 °C; 20 and 22 °C slower than 25 °C, 30 °C fastest but thin; all reached pH 4.2 within 48 h; temperature did not change tonga community, dominated by Lactococcus.
- Unsure: stop-point variants differ between producers; none measured here.

## Thick tonga mabisi
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: fermented milk (thicker, less whey)
- What it is: a thicker variant of tonga mabisi, found in all provinces except Northern. Ranked fourth in popularity in the national survey.
- How it is made: as tonga mabisi, but after fermentation a portion of the whey is drained off before stirring, so the product keeps a good thickness and homogeneity. High whey content is considered undesirable in the final product.
- Where / who: all provinces except Northern.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6417723/fullTextXML — thick tonga process and distribution across provinces.
- Unsure: none.

## Backslopping mabisi
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: fermented milk, cultured with a previous batch
- What it is: a type in which a portion of one batch is kept as starter for the next, repeated over many cycles. Ranked third in popularity in the national survey.
- How it is made: as tonga type up to stirring, with no whey removed; the mixing is vigorous and the product is less viscous. A proportion is removed for eating and some is left in the container as starter. Fresh raw milk is added and fermentation repeats. Cycles can go on for several months, especially in the rainy season when milk is plentiful and the container is kept full. The starter is "backslopped" from the first ripe product.
- Where / who: found across the country; traders in the 2020 sampling produced many barotse and backslopping samples.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6417723/fullTextXML — process and multi-month cycles; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11795106/fullTextXML — four-cycle study: volatiles decreased over cycles; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1007/s11274-020-02957-5&format=json&resultType=core — active vs passive vs no back-slopping: active back-slopping starts acidifying straight away.
- Unsure: the cycle count and its effect on flavour are not fixed; the field trial was done in one rural site.

## Illa mabisi and illa butter
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: fermented milk (illa mabisi), and butter (illa butter)
- What it is: illa mabisi is a churned version of backslopping. The butter is the product: butter granules formed by churning, used in cooking and as a skin lotion. Illa practice is mainly in Southern and Central provinces, among the Illa and Tonga people (Namwala, Choma, Monze and Itezi-tezi districts), less in Western province.
- How it is made: milk turns sour after about a day. The calabash is shaken back and forth for 5–20 minutes at a time, several times a day, on a mat or on the lap; the shaking forms butter granules that float up. They are skimmed with a wooden spoon into a small pot or cup. Mabisi is poured out, some left as starter; fresh raw milk added and gently stirred; fermentation continues for a few hours; by mid-morning the butter and mabisi are removed and raw milk added again. The butter is washed with water and stored in a cool place. It is used for porridge, vegetables, fish and meat, and as a lotion after brief heating and cooling.
- Where / who: Southern and Central provinces (Illa and Tonga, Namwala, Choma, Monze, Itezi-tezi districts); some in Western.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6417723/fullTextXML — illa process, churning, butter washing, cooking and lotion uses, districts; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11795106/fullTextXML — illa agitation with butter removal described as a distinct variant.
- Unsure: the source describes butter as a by-product of illa mabisi; it does not say whether butter is also made separately as its own product.

## Barotse mabisi
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: fermented milk
- What it is: named after Barotseland, the old name for the Western province, where it is mainly made. Second in popularity in the national survey, and most popular in Western province.
- How it is made: made in a calabash or plastic container with a small opening on top. Whey is removed through a hole in the lower side of the calabash, then fresh raw milk is added and it ferments again. Alternating whey removal and raw milk addition takes four to seven days to produce. Samples in the 2020 sampling and the 2024 study were mostly from traders, and had a lower pH.
- Where / who: Western province (Lozi / Barotse area); also found in other regions.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6417723/fullTextXML — barotse process, whey draining, popularity; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7406715/fullTextXML — 4–7 days, traders produced lower pH; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11795106/fullTextXML — barotse variant: removal and addition described; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%22Influence%20of%20fermentation%20temperature%20on%20microbial%20community%22&format=json&resultType=core — abstract (second pass): barotse mabisi fermented at 25 °C gave thick product dominated by Lactococcus; at 30 °C thicker, more acidic, Lactobacillus-dominated.
- Unsure: none.

## Creamy mabisi
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: fermented milk
- What it is: a type that keeps cream in the product. It is one of the seven national method types but found only in small proportion (less than 5 per cent of respondents).
- How it is made: the whey is drained off before stirring; the product is fermented for an additional day; the cream (kept from the top) is then added back and mixed through the product.
- Where / who: found in the national survey; rare.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6417723/fullTextXML — creamy type and cream added back (described as process VI).
- Unsure: the source notes the creamy type as a rare variant; the exact steps for this type are drawn from one passage in the paper and were not checked against another source.

## Cooked mabisi
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: fermented milk, heat-treated after sieving
- What it is: a type that is cooked after sieving. One of the seven national types, under 5 per cent of respondents.
- How it is made: produced as tonga type, except that after sieving the milk is heated in a pot until foam forms and it begins to boil; it is then removed from heat and allowed to cool slowly.
- Where / who: national survey; rare.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6417723/fullTextXML — cooked type steps.
- Unsure: rare; the proportion of producers using it is not given beyond the survey.

## Mabisi whey and instant mabisi
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: drink and cooking ingredient (whey from fermented milk)
- What it is: the whey drained from mabisi. Used in cooking porridge, given to children as a drink, and fed to dogs and pigs. Also used to make "instant mabisi".
- How it is made: whey is drained off the fermenting mabisi through a hole in the calabash or by pouring off before stirring. Instant mabisi: whey is added to fresh raw milk; because its pH is low, the milk coagulates at once.
- Where / who: home use across Zambia. In the rainy season (December–April) in Southern and Western provinces, whey is often simply discarded because milk is plentiful.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6417723/fullTextXML — whey uses and seasonal discarding.
- Unsure: "instant mabisi" is described in one paper only.

## Raw cow milk (fresh milk)
- Animal(s): cow (zebu cattle)
- Kind: drink / basic dairy (raw, unprocessed)
- What it is: fresh raw milk from cows, the base for all mabisi. Sold by smallholder dairy farmers; in some areas historically sold to Indian traders near villages.
- How it is made: milked by hand into a bucket (plastic or metal) or gourd; used directly for mabisi. Raw milk is also sold from smallholder farms in Northern Province (Mbala and Kasama).
- Where / who: smallholder cattle farms across the country; Northern, Southern, Western and Eastern provinces.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6417723/fullTextXML — milking into bucket; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12730005/fullTextXML — 93 raw milk samples from 56 smallholder farms in Mbala and Kasama (2025), for antibiotic residue testing; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11687773/fullTextXML — historic sale of fresh milk to Indian traders near villages; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1016/j.ijfoodmicro.2022.109737&format=json&resultType=core — abstract (second pass): S. aureus in 33–46% of raw milk from traditional and smallholder farms (Lusaka, Southern, Western); raw milk from collection centres, informal traders, market sellers and processors also often contaminated.
- Unsure: the Kasama paper is about residues; it does not describe consumer sale or any handling beyond collection.

## Industrially processed (heat-treated) milk
- Animal(s): cow (not stated in the source)
- Kind: packaged milk from processors (formal sector), heat-treated
- What it is: milk and dairy products sold through the formal channel after industrial heat treatment. A Zambian study sampled such products in Lusaka, Southern and Western provinces and found no S. aureus in them, in contrast to raw milk.
- How it is made: described only as "industrially processed (heat-treated)"; the source gives no pasteurisation, UHT or processing detail.
- Where / who: formal-sector processors and retail in Lusaka, Southern and Western provinces (sampling sites).
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1016/j.ijfoodmicro.2022.109737&format=json&resultType=core — abstract: heat-treated milk and dairy products sampled in the three provinces, no S. aureus isolated.
- Unsure: no brand, processor or product name is confirmed; the share of milk processed by dairy companies (under 50%) is in the mabisi source PMC6417723 and is not restated here.

## Mabisi with nsima or porridge
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: dish (mixed with staple)
- What it is: mabisi mixed with leftover nsima (maize meal porridge; sometimes millet, sorghum or cassava) and eaten as a main meal when no relish is available. Also eaten with rice, sweet potatoes and pumpkin.
- How it is made: mabisi stirred into cooked or leftover nsima.
- Where / who: widespread among mabisi consumers; described in Southern, Eastern and Western provinces.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11687773/fullTextXML — mixing with nsima, rice, sweet potato, pumpkin; used as a cheaper alternative to relish.
- Unsure: none.

## Goat mabisi
- Animal(s): goat
- Kind: fermented milk (rare)
- What it is: mabisi made from goat milk. Rare; in the foodscape study goat milk was not common in any of the three study areas. Some Tonga people milk goats; many Lozi respondents said it was not part of their practice.
- How it is made: not described in detail in the sources. Some respondents said goat milk gives more cream and would make very good mabisi.
- Where / who: Tonga-associated in the study; Eastern Province goats are more common than in Western.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11687773/fullTextXML — goat milk rarely used; some would make it if available in quantity.
- Unsure: the sources do not confirm any commercial or regular goat-milk mabisi; the claim rests on interview answers from one study of 86 participants.
