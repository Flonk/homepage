## Norse-era skyr (skyr; Old Norse skyr; other names: sour milk curd)
- Animal(s): Not specified in source (Norse settlers kept cattle, sheep and goats)
- Kind: fermented milk (curd-like)
- What it is: A sour milk product similar to quark, named in the 13th-century Konungs skuggsjá as part of Norse Greenlanders' diet.
- How it is made: Not described in the sources read.
- Where / who: Norse settlers (Eastern and Western Settlements), medieval period; not a current Greenlandic product in the sources read.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norse_Greenland — says farmers "lived primarily on meat, milk (skyr, a sour milk product similar to quark), butter and cheese" (quoting Konungs skuggsjá).
- Unsure: Historical only; no source found for production in modern Greenland.

## Norse-era butter (butter)
- Animal(s): Cattle, probably; animal not specified in source. Norse cattle kept at Brattahlíð and Garðar.
- Kind: butter (fat)
- What it is: Butter listed among the dairy foods of Norse farmers in Greenland.
- How it is made: Not described in the sources read.
- Where / who: Norse settlers, medieval period.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norse_Greenland — lists butter among Norse farmers' foods (Konungs skuggsjá); cattle kept in stables for 50 to 100 cows; cattle teeth at Western Settlement suggest milk production over meat.
- Unsure: Historical; not confirmed in modern Greenland.

## Norse-era cow and small-stock milk (fresh milk)
- Animal(s): Cow (Norse cattle); sheep and goats (later, replacing cattle on smaller farms)
- Kind: fresh milk
- What it is: Milk from cattle, later from sheep and goats, drunk and processed by Norse settlers.
- How it is made: Not described in the sources read.
- Where / who: Norse settlers, Eastern and Western Settlements.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norse_Greenland — milk named as a Norse diet staple, cattle "used more for milk production than for meat" at the Western Settlement; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norse_Greenland — cattle replaced by goats and sheep over time; https://www.sea.museum/article/what-the-vikings-really-ate — "In Greenland, on medium and small-sized farms, goats and sheep replaced cattle over time" and cattle kept on small sites, supporting Norse dairy importance.
- Unsure: Historical only.

## Local cow's and sheep's milk (fresh milk, modern)
- Animal(s): Cow; sheep
- Kind: fresh milk (for cheese making)
- What it is: A Nuuk restaurant reports using organic cow's and sheep's milk from Greenland to make its first Greenlandic cheese.
- How it is made: Not described; the cheese-making process is outside this entry (cheese is covered by another researcher).
- Where / who: Nuuk, a gourmet restaurant; not an industrial or farm-scale dairy in the sources read.
- Sources: https://www.four-magazine.com/?p=16660 — restaurant's first Greenlandic cheese from organic cow's or sheep's milk; sheep's milk cheese flavoured with thyme and parsley from Greenland.
- Unsure: Shows local milk is used at least once for cheese; the source does not confirm a regular milk supply, and the Michigan Public article says most dairy is imported. Fresh-milk sale, if any, is not confirmed. Second pass: the same page does not say the milk is Greenlandic, only that the cheese is; the milk origin is unconfirmed.

## Greenlandic coffee cream (whipped cream topping)
- Animal(s): Cow (cream from unspecified animal)
- Kind: cream (drink topping)
- What it is: Whipped cream is a standard topping for Greenlandic coffee, an after-dinner drink of hot coffee, whisky, Kahlúa, Grand Marnier and whipped cream, served in glasses and set alight.
- How it is made: Whipped cream, whipped and served on top of the hot coffee drink; no further steps given.
- Where / who: Greenland-wide, as a drink in cafes and households; a modern, partly imported-style drink.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenlandic_cuisine — beverages section names whipped cream in Greenlandic coffee; https://da.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grønlandsk_køkken — "flødeskum" (whipped cream) in Greenlandic coffee.
- Unsure: The cream is not shown to be made locally; imported cream is likely given that most dairy is imported.
