## Cultured buttermilk (buttermilk, US commercial)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: fermented milk drink / baking ingredient
- What it is: Pasteurised, homogenised milk thickened and soured by lactic bacteria; the commercial US product dates from the 1920s and was used in baking and as a drink.
- How it is made: Pasteurised homogenised milk is inoculated with Lactococcus lactis or Lactobacillus bulgaricus, with Leuconostoc mesenteroides subsp. cremoris added for butter flavour; the bacteria turn lactose into lactic acid and the casein precipitates. No times or temperatures given.
- Where / who: Industrial; US baking and beverage use. The page says it became a drink among Eastern European immigrants, but marks that claim as needing a citation. Peak US sales about 517 million kg in 1960; under half that by 2012.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buttermilk — US cultured buttermilk section, commercial process, sales figures
- Unsure: The traditional churning by-product buttermilk is documented on the same page but not as a US product; the page's immigrant-drink claim is unsourced.

## Eggnog
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: sweetened dairy drink with egg
- What it is: A chilled, sweetened drink of milk and/or cream, sugar and whipped eggs, often with spirits; tied to the US holiday season from Thanksgiving to Christmas.
- How it is made: Milk or cream, sugar, eggs, spirits, nutmeg or vanilla; can be cooked gently until it coats a spoon without boiling. Aged alcoholic versions are described as safer.
- Where / who: Colonial and US tradition; rum-based early, bourbon in the South. Named US historical variants in an 1878 bartender's guide: Baltimore egg nog, General Jackson egg nog, imperial egg nog, sherry cobbler egg nog. Commercial and home.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eggnog — history, base, regional and named variants
- Unsure: Pennsylvania Dutch version not covered by the page.

## Milkshake
- Animal(s): cow (dairy milk; plant milks possible)
- Kind: blended milk drink
- What it is: Milk, ice cream and flavouring blended together; US soda-fountain and fast-food staple.
- How it is made: Blended with milk, ice cream and syrup or fruit (commercial machines dispense premixed mix; home cooks use a blender).
- Where / who: US. First printed 1885 as a whiskey drink; non-alcoholic by 1900. Regional names: frappe (New England), cabinet (Rhode Island), thick shake, malt, concrete (Culver's), Frosty (Wendy's). Many chains avoid the word because of milk-fat legal rules.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milkshake — how made, history, names

## Frozen custard
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: frozen dessert with egg yolk
- What it is: A denser, slightly warmer frozen dessert than ice cream, made with egg yolks, cream and sweetener.
- How it is made: Egg yolk, cream, sweetener and flavour are frozen in a continuous freezer with blades scraping the barrel; overrun about 15–30%; served about −8 °C. US standard: at least 10% milkfat and 1.4% egg yolk solids (below that it is ice cream).
- Where / who: US. Commercial since Coney Island, New York, 1919; Ted Drewes, St. Louis, 1930 (the "concrete" is associated with it); Milwaukee, Wisconsin, is called the frozen custard capital.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frozen_custard — method, US standard, origins; https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/part-135/subpart-B — 21 CFR 135.110 ice cream and frozen custard standard (section title only)
- Unsure: none

## Frozen yogurt
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: frozen dairy dessert
- What it is: A frozen dessert made with yogurt cultures; tarter and lower in fat than ice cream in its tart form.
- How it is made: Milk products and stabilisers homogenised; culture added at 32 °C until set; cooled to 0–4 °C; sweeteners and flavours added; aerated and rapidly frozen.
- Where / who: US. Invented in New England in the 1970s; H. P. Hood's "Frogurt" in New York by 1972; soft-serve sweetened versions (TCBY, Tasti D-Lite) in the 1980s; tart Pinkberry (2005); self-serve shops (16 Handles, 2008).
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frozen_yogurt — history, method, forms
- Unsure: The 1972 Frogurt origin cites a 2025 Tasting Table piece as quoted on the page.

## Evaporated milk
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: canned unsweetened milk
- What it is: Shelf-stable canned milk with about 60% of its water removed.
- How it is made: Fresh homogenised milk has 60% of water evaporated, is chilled, stabilised, sterilised at 116–118 °C for 15 minutes and canned; it caramelises slightly. Federal rules require at least 6.5% milkfat and added vitamin D. Diluted 1 part to 1¼ water to approximate fresh milk.
- Where / who: US; 1920s–30s low-price availability; Christian Diehl Brewery "Jerzee" brand from 1922 (Prohibition). Carnation and PET named as brands.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evaporated_milk — method, US history and regulation; https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/part-131/subpart-B — 21 CFR 131.130 evaporated milk (title only)
- Unsure: none

## Sweetened condensed milk (Eagle Brand)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: sweetened concentrated milk
- What it is: Milk with about 60% water removed and sugar added, so it keeps without refrigeration.
- How it is made: Clarify and standardise milk; heat to 85–90 °C for several seconds; evaporate to about 60% of volume; add sugar to a sugar-to-evaporated-milk ratio of 9:11; cool and induce lactose crystallisation.
- Where / who: US; Gail Borden's Wassaic, New York factory with Jeremiah Milbank; Eagle Brand by 1858; bought in quantity as Union Army rations in the Civil War.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensed_milk — method and US history; https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/part-131/subpart-B — 21 CFR 131.120 (title only)
- Unsure: The page does not give US Hispanic uses; tres leches and dulce de leche use are in Latin America.

## Milk powder (dried whole milk and nonfat dry milk)
- Animal(s): cow (also buttermilk and whey powders)

- What it is: Dehydrated milk for long shelf life and transport.
- How it is made: Milk concentrated to about 50% solids in an evaporator, then sprayed into a hot chamber (spray drying), or dried on a heated drum; freeze drying preserves more nutrients. ## Sour cream (US)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: cultured cream
- What it is: Cream fermented with lactic acid bacteria to sour and thicken it.
- How it is made: Cream standardised; acidified with citric acid or sodium citrate; homogenised below 2 microns; pasteurised at 85 °C for 30 minutes; cooled to about 20 °C, inoculated at 1–2% culture, fermented about 18 hours. US rules: at least 18% milkfat before bulking agents, 14.4% in the finished product; total acidity at least 0.5%.
- Where / who: US; condiment, dips and sauces, and Tex-Mex topping; commercial and home.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sour_cream — method, US regulation, uses; https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/part-131/subpart-B — 21 CFR 131.160 sour cream and 131.162 acidified sour cream (titles only)
- Unsure: The page contains no US history or comparison with Mexican crema.

## Half-and-half
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: cream and milk blend
- What it is: A mix of milk and cream, used mainly in coffee; in the US it must contain 10.5–18% milkfat.
- How it is made: Pasteurised or ultra-pasteurised; may be homogenised; emulsifiers, stabilisers and flavourings may be added.
- Where / who: US; premixed product invented by William A. Boutwell of Boutwell Dairy, Lake Worth Beach, Florida, sold regionally 1927–1956. Regional uses: iced tea and lemonade (Arnold Palmer in the Northeast), sweet and unsweet iced tea in Atlanta and Houston, dirty soda.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half_and_half — definition, history, US regional uses
- Unsure: none

## Whipped cream (US forms)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: whipped cream
- What it is: Cream whipped to a light foam, sweetened and flavoured; sold fresh or in aerosol cans.
- How it is made: Cream of 30–36% butterfat whipped chilled to double its volume; aerosol versions use nitrous oxide (sometimes CO2); overwhipping makes butter. Whipped toppings made from oil are a separate US category, not dairy.
- Where / who: US; aerosol siphon patents in the 1930s (Charles Getz, Marshall Reinecke); Reddi-wip as a brand example; alcohol-infused whipped cream a 2010 US fad.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whipped_cream — method, aerosol forms, US history
- Unsure: no US legal definition of whipped cream on the page.

## Butter (sweet-cream and US "European-style" cultured)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: butter
- What it is: Fat churned from cream; sweet-cream butter dominates the US, cultured butter is marketed as "European-style".
- How it is made: Sweet-cream butter churned from fresh cream (about 6 hours); cultured butter from cream fermented for up to 72 hours (page's figure, marked as needing citation), then churned, washed, worked. US minimum 80% butterfat; European cultured butter 82%. Industrial shortcut NIZO method adds lactic acid and diacetyl.
- Where / who: US and UK sweet cream; Amish dairies make and sell cultured butter in the US.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultured_butter — method, US and European forms, Amish note
- Unsure: Culturing temperatures not given; the 72-hour figure is flagged on the page as needing citation.

## Greek yogurt (US strained yogurt)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: strained fermented milk
- What it is: Yogurt strained or concentrated to a thick texture; a large US category.
- How it is made: Strained by gravity historically; industrially concentrated by ultrafiltration before culture or centrifuged/membrane-filtered after; acid whey is a by-product.

## Kefir (US)
- Animal(s): cow, goat or sheep (typical)
- Kind: fermented milk drink
- What it is: Milk fermented with kefir grains; spread to the US by the early 21st century.

- Where / who: US; commercial and home.

## Infant formula (US milk-based)
- Animal(s): cow (milk-based; US goat-milk formula not available per Wikipedia goat article)
- Kind: powdered or liquid infant milk
- What it is: Milk-based formula that is the main infant feed in the US, mostly commercial.
- How it is made: Skim milk heated to 60 °C, fats and emulsifiers added, pasteurised at 85–94 °C for about 30 s, homogenised, heat-treated (retort, HTST or UHT); powder spray-dried. FDA sets nutrient rules.
- Where / who: US; Similac from the late 1920s (Alfred Bosworth); Enfamil 1959 (Mead Johnson); over 75% of US babies on formula by the early 1970s; WIC covers over half of formula; 2022 shortage.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infant_formula — process, FDA rules, US history
- Unsure: Spray-drying detail not given.
