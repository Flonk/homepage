- Kind: fresh/raw and heat-treated milk
- What it is: the basic dairy drink; the Estonian word covers both raw and processed milk
- How it is made: not described beyond general pasteurisation
- Where / who: industrial and home
- Sources: https://et.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piimatooted — lists piim as the basic product made from raw milk of cow, sheep, goat or camel

## Hapukoor (sour cream; koor)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: soured cream
- What it is: cream fermented with lactic acid bacteria; thicker with higher fat
- How it is made: no steps, temperatures or times given
- Where / who: industrial and home; used in salads, soups, baking and casseroles
- Sources: https://et.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hapukoor — Estonian fat content is 10–30%; uses listed; https://et.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piimatooted — koor (cream) is the fatty layer on milk or separated from it

## Või (butter)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: butter (churned from cream)
- What it is: the butter family, including natural, flavoured and reconstituted types
- How it is made: traditionally by working cream with a wooden spoon or shaking it in a jar or churn; 19th-century hand churns; modern: churn cream until fat clumps and separates from buttermilk (petipiim); no temperatures given
- Where / who: home and industrial. Natural butter (naturaalne või) is made only from pasteurised cream; flavoured butter (maitsevõi) with dill, garlic, parsley, chives, basil, thyme or mustard; reconstituted butter (taastatud või) from butter oil, skimmed-milk powder and water; whey butter (vadakuvõi) from whey cream or whey cream mixed with cream
- Sources: https://et.wikipedia.org/wiki/Või — types and method; https://et.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piimatooted — või is made by churning cream
- Unsure: none

## Pett (buttermilk; võipiim, petipiim)
- Animal(s): cow (milk by-product of butter production)
- Kind: buttermilk, sour or fermented
- What it is: the liquid left after churning butter; about 91–92% water
- How it is made (fermented version, as described for Estonia): add lactic starter to sour buttermilk (a little whey may be added first); homogenise; pasteurise at high temperature; cool; ferment about 14–16 hours until pH 4.5–4.7
- Where / who: industrial and home; drunk plain or mixed with berries, jam, vegetables or herbs; used in baking as a leavening acid
- Sources: https://et.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pett — composition, fermentation steps, uses
- Unsure: the article gives no animal species; "as described for Estonia" wording is from the article

## Hapupiim (sour milk)
- Animal(s): cow (milk in general in source)
- Kind: fermented/sour milk (buttermilk family)
- What it is: a fermented milk drink soured by lactic acid bacteria; pH about 4.0–5.0
- How it is made: either let raw milk sour at room temperature or inoculate with selected lactic cultures; milk used is whole, low-fat (about 1.5–1.8% fat) or fortified with skim powder; finished product 0.5–0.9% lactic acid; no incubation temperatures or times
- Where / who: traditional summer drink (haymaking); often whisked with honey; thicker versions eaten with a spoon. The traditional-use part of the source is about the Baltic peoples in general, with Latvian examples, not Estonia specifically
- Sources: https://et.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hapupiim — method and traditional use; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fermented_milk_products — Estonia row lists soured milk
- Unsure: Estonian-specific traditional details not confirmed from the source

## Kohupiim (curd; vaarapiim in South Estonian dialects)
- Animal(s): not stated on the source page
- Kind: fresh curd product from fermented milk, heated
- What it is: soft curd used in desserts and eaten salted or sweetened; high in calcium and phosphorus
- How it is made: milk is fermented, then gently heated to about 50–60 °C so the casein clumps; whey separates out; some regions drain the curd instead of heating; some add rennet to set it; no times given
- Where / who: industrial and home; most common in Central and Eastern Europe
- Sources: https://et.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kohupiim — method, names, uses; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estonian_cuisine — kohupiim named (borderline cheese)
- Unsure: borderline cheese (close to quark). The page calls it a dairy product rather than cheese but says a related product, kodujuust, forms a cheese-like curd. Kept as a dairy entry, not a cheese entry; the cheese researcher may also have it

## Jogurt (yoghurt)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: fermented milk, thick
- What it is: thick, usually flavoured fermented milk with more dry matter than other sour milks
- How it is made: whole milk, pure culture of Lactobacillus bulgaricus and Streptococcus thermophilus; common additives skimmed milk powder, sugar, fruit or berry syrups, honey; no fermentation temperatures or times
- Where / who: industrial. Estonian producers make unpasteurised yoghurts with live bacteria (chilled, +2 to +6 °C, at least 2–3 weeks) and post-pasteurised yoghurts (room temperature)
- Sources: https://et.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jogurt — culture, additives, storage, fat classes (low-fat 0.1–0.3%, milk 1.5–3.5%, cream 10%+); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estonian_cuisine — yoghurt usually sweetened with sugar or blueberry
- Unsure: none

## Keefir (kefir; kefiir)
- Animal(s): cow (cow, camel, goat or sheep milk in general source)
- Kind: fermented milk drink, 1–2% alcohol
- What it is: drink fermented by bacteria and yeasts; sour, slightly fizzy
- How it is made: milk pasteurised and cooled; starter culture (juuretis) added; ferments at a suitable temperature (no temperature or time given); bacteria make lactic acid; yeasts convert lactose to CO2 and a little alcohol
- Where / who: industrial and home; the Estonian page links it to hapupiim, kohupiim and kumõss but does not describe Estonian use
- Sources: https://et.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keefir — production and milk types; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fermented_milk_products — Estonia row lists kefir
- Unsure: Estonian use not confirmed

## Kumõss (fermented mare's milk)
- Animal(s): mare
- Kind: fermented milk drink, mildly alcoholic (1–3%)
- What it is: mare's milk fermented by lactic acid bacteria and yeast
- How it is made (general, from English Wikipedia Kumis): raw milk fermented for hours or days while stirred; modern control: 2–5 hours at about 27 °C, then cooler aging; traditionally in horse-hide containers; industrial versions often use cow milk with sugar or modified whey
- Where / who: Turkic and Mongolic peoples (Mongolia's airag); the Estonian page links it, but Estonian tradition is not confirmed
- Sources: https://et.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kumõss — drink, alcohol content, peoples; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kumis — method and temperatures; https://et.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keefir — links kumõss as a related Estonian dairy product
- Unsure: whether it is actually made or drunk in Estonia; no Estonian source describes production there. Listed in the Estonian cuisine page only as a link via kefir

## Jäätis (ice cream)
- Animal(s): cow (milk, cream)
- Kind: frozen milk dessert
- What it is: ice cream, usually from milk or cream, sugar and flavourings; the word also covers sorbet and fruit ice
- How it is made: not described on the source page
- Where / who: industrial. Industrial production began in 1934 when Evald Rooma founded the Eskimo company in Tartu. Output peaked at about 11,000 tonnes around 2010; about 8,700 tonnes in 2016, nearly half exported. Main producers named: Pureline, Revala, Tallinna Külmhoone, Ingman Jäätised, Balbiino
- Sources: https://et.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jäätis — history, production figures, producers

## Piimapulber (milk powder)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: dried milk
- What it is: milk dried to a powder; types cream powder (>=42% fat), whole milk powder (26–42%), reduced-fat (1.5–26%), skim (up to 1.5%)
- How it is made: spray drying (main method; milk pasteurised to about 50% solids, cream separated, then sprayed into a hot chamber); drum drying (about 40–45% solids, caramel flavour, used in chocolate)
- Where / who: industrial. Estonian producers named: Dairy Co-Operative E-Piim (Järva-Jaani), Revala LTD, AST Group LTD (Tallinn), Krimson AS (Maardus), Dessery AS (Türi), Kalev Paide Tootmine AS (Paide)
- Sources: https://et.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piimapulber — method, types, producers
- Unsure: none

## Ternespiim (colostrum; first milk after calving)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: first milk after calving
- What it is: milk a cow gives in the first 24–36 hours after calving; light yellow, buttermilk-like taste; more protein and immunoglobulins than normal milk, about half the lactose, nearly twice the dry matter
- How it is made: not a process; it is the milk taken straight after calving
- Where / who: mainly fed to calves; also used as human food, freeze-dried into powder, tablets and capsules sold as supplements
- Sources: https://et.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ternespiim — definition, composition, uses
- Unsure: health claims are presented in the article as folk medicine and are not verified

## Rjaženka (ryazhenka; rjaženka)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: baked, fermented milk; no gas (unlike kefir)
- What it is: highly pasteurised sour milk, creamy, golden-brown, mild yoghurt-like taste
- How it is made: milk baked in an oven for 8 hours or longer until golden brown, then fermented 3–6 hours by thermophilic streptococci and pure Lactobacillus cultures (Estonian article gives no temperature). The English article adds: simmered on low heat for at least eight hours; household versions start with sour cream; industrial cultures Streptococcus thermophilus and L. delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus; fermentation above about 40 °C
- Where / who: AS Rakvere Piim produces it and sells it under the Farmi brand
- Sources: https://et.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rjaženka — method, times, producer; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ryazhenka — simmering, starter, temperature (English article gives Ukraine, Russia and Belarus as origin, not Estonia)

## Vadak (whey)
- Animal(s): not stated (a by-product of cheese and kohupiim making)
- Kind: liquid by-product
- What it is: the liquid left after casein clots and is strained out; 8–10 litres of whey per kilogram of cheese; older Estonian name juustuvesi ("cheese water")
- How it is made: arises in cheese and kohupiim making
- Where / who: whey protein used in drinks and egg-white substitutes; whey powder in baking (cookies, candies, chocolate, ice cream, cakes); whey butter in confectionery; ricotta is made from mozzarella whey (per the article)
- Sources: https://et.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vadak — formation, yield, uses
- Unsure: no Estonian whey drink or producer is named
