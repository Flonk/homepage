## Skyr (skyr; skyr.is; ísskyr, other names)
- Animal(s): cow (today, cow's milk is the norm); sheep (historically)
- Kind: fermented milk (fresh, soured, rennet-set product)
- What it is: A thick, mild, high-protein sour dairy product, eaten cold with spoons, often with cream. It has been part of Icelandic food for centuries and is mentioned in medieval sagas, though no medieval description survives. Commonly classed as a fresh cheese, though marketed as a yoghurt.
- How it is made: Traditional: skimmed milk heated near boiling (English page gives 72–75 °C for 15–20 s), cooled to about 37 °C, mixed with a starter from a previous batch (þétti), rennet optional, left to set for about 5 hours, cooled to about 18 °C, drained through cloth or sieve so whey runs off. Modern: heated, cooled, microfiltered (örsíun) and canned before setting, leaving more whey protein. Many modern versions use yoghurt cultures instead of traditional skyr cultures. Historical quality terms: draflaskyr (coarse, set in overheated milk), kuldaskyr (thin, set in too-cold milk), ólekja (unfiltered, poor-quality skyr).
- Where / who: Iceland-wide; industrial (Mjólkursamsalan's Ísey skyr, cooperative skyr.is) and home. Traditional farm product.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skyr — history, production steps, Iceland and export; https://is.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skyr — traditional and industrial method, draflaskyr/kuldaskyr/ólekja; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icelandic_cuisine — skyr as common food, skyr.is, porridge with skyr; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9223127/fullTextXML — skyr is traditional Icelandic dairy, skimmed milk, whey removed by centrifugation or ultrafiltration; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12512351/fullTextXML — skyr as high-protein Icelandic yogurt made from skimmed milk
- Unsure: Wikipedia says skyr was first made from sheep's milk, while cow's milk is now common. Skyr.is is named as a cooperative brand, not a separate product.

## Smjör (butter; including sheep's-milk butter)
- Animal(s): cow (all modern Icelandic butter); sheep (historically, very common)
- Kind: butter
- What it is: Butter, historically made from soured cream and kept unsalted. It was once a form of currency: rents for land and livestock were paid in butter.
- How it is made: Churned from fresh or soured cream until the fat separates from the buttermilk; buttermilk drained; butter kneaded. Historically unsalted because salt was scarce; left to sour, and kept for decades. Salted butter lasted about 2 years, sour butter over 20 years (quoted from Björn Halldórsson's Arnbjörg). Summer butter from ewes milked in outdoor pens (kvíar) was yellower and softer than winter butter.
- Where / who: Iceland-wide; farmers, tenant farmers used it to pay landowners; a staple served with dried fish at every meal in early modern times.
- Sources: https://is.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smjör — production, sheep's-milk butter, salt scarcity, currency use, seasonal colour; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icelandic_cuisine — butter with dried fish at every meal, tenant farmers paying landowners, butter mashed into vegetables; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icelandic_sheep — sheep milk made into butter
- Unsure: The sources differ on whether butter was served with fish or only at mealtimes; the English page says every meal.

## Ábrystir (colostrum set custard)
- Animal(s): cow (colostrum); sheep historically
- Kind: sweet / dessert (set custard from colostrum)
- What it is: A traditional Icelandic dish, classed as spónamatur (food eaten with a spoon), made from colostrum. Surplus ewe colostrum was used in the sheep-milking era.
- How it is made: Colostrum mixed with regular milk (equal parts or more on day 1; less by day 2–3), heated and simmered gently for about 20 minutes until firm, cooked in a water bath traditionally to prevent scorching. Name may come from bresta ("to burst") when overcooked. Served hot, warm or cold with cinnamon sugar, fruit syrup or a sweet sauce.
- Where / who: Iceland-wide; home; cow and ewe.
- Sources: https://is.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ábrystir — preparation, dilution by day, name, accompaniments; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icelandic_sheep — ewe colostrum set custard ábrystir; https://is.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broddmjólk — cow colostrum used for ábrystir
- Unsure: The Icelandic article is unreferenced and gives little history. Compared to the Faroese ketilostur and British beestings, but that is from the same article.

## Sheep's milk (ewe milk)
- Animal(s): sheep
- Kind: raw/fresh milk
- What it is: Sheep were Iceland's main dairy animals for centuries. Milk was drunk fresh, or made into butter, cheese and skyr.
- How it is made: Ewes were milked for several weeks after lambs were separated from them at night from about two weeks old. Typical yield about 1 L/day, peaks near 3 L/day. Regular milking declined sharply early in the 20th century and had largely stopped by the 1940s.
- Where / who: Iceland-wide; farms; historical.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icelandic_sheep — sheep as main dairy animal, milking method, yields, decline; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icelandic_cuisine — sheep used for milk
- Unsure: Dates and yields come from a single source.
