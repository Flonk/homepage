## Kajmak (skorup) (кајмак; скорупа/скоруп in Montenegro; kaymak)
- Animal(s): mainly cow; also sheep, goat, buffalo; in Montenegro mixed cow and sheep milk is most common
- Kind: fermented/cultured cream (fat layer skimmed from boiled milk)
- What it is: a creamy, sour, buttery product made from the fat that forms on boiled and cooled milk; sits between cheese and butter. In Montenegro the name skorup is common and it is a national meal.
- How it is made: boil milk and stir about 15 minutes; pour into shallow wide vessels; cool about 2 h near 80 °C then slowly for about 12 h (may rest 24 h or longer, especially in winter); skim the crust at about 10–15 °C (above 20 °C the fat stays in the milk); drain and salt each layer separately; store at about 15–18 °C for 15–20 days, then keep cool. Mature skorup in Montenegro ripens at least about 2 months and keeps up to a year. Production typically July–September in Montenegro, when fat content is higher and nights are cooler. Traditionally shallow wooden bowls; now enamelled pots.
- Where / who: skorup mainly around Durmitor and Sinjajevina (Plužine, Žabljak, Šavnik, Mojkovac, Kolašin, Nikšić); also kept by katun herders. Also made across Serbia, North Macedonia, Bosnia (en. Wikipedia lists Montenegro among kaymak countries). Home and small-scale production, with some industrial.
- Sources: https://sr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Кајмак — Montenegrin skorup production areas, method, temperatures, times; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaymak — general method, milk types, Montenegro in list of countries; https://sr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Црногорска_кухиња — skorup (kajmak) listed among Montenegrin specialties; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montenegrin_cuisine — kaymak as Montenegrin dairy and in cicvara, popara and kačamak
- Unsure: the en. Wikipedia Kaymak article says kajmak is a Montenegrin national meal; the sr. Wikipedia kajmak article gives the exact Montenegrin areas. The en. article gives no Montenegro-specific method, so the method above is the sr. article's, applied to the whole Balkan kaymak tradition.

## Cicvara (цицвара)
- Animal(s): none directly; made with kajmak (cow/sheep milk fat)
- Kind: dish made from kajmak (dairy-based cornmeal dish)
- What it is: a cornmeal dish in which kajmak is the main fat and flavour; served hot, sometimes with honey.
- How it is made: put 200 g ripe kajmak in 500 ml hot water and bring to a boil over low heat; slowly add 200 g corn flour while stirring constantly with a wooden spoon until it thickens; keep the melted kajmak floating on top. Served in plates and eaten with a spoon, with honey, potatoes or bread.
- Where / who: attributed to Durmitor in Montenegro, where it was a Christmas dish; also in western and south-western Serbia, among Serbs in Republika Srpska and eastern Herzegovina. Often served with smoked pork prosciutto as a sign of honour in Montenegro.
- Sources: https://sr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Цицвара — ingredients, method, Durmitor origin, Montenegrin serving; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montenegrin_cuisine — cicvara as a Montenegrin dish with kajmak
- Unsure: this is a kajmak dish, not a dairy product in its own right; it is listed here because kajmak is its core ingredient.

## Sour milk (kiselo mlijeko; kisjelo mlijeko; buttermilk)
- Animal(s): cow (mainly); sheep and goat where milked on katuns
- Kind: fermented/soured milk drink or soft milk product
- What it is: milk soured by bacteria (or occasionally by added acid); eaten on its own and with dishes.
- How it is made: traditionally fresh milk left in a warm place, often by the stove, to sour naturally over a day. The sr. Wikipedia article gives no temperatures or cultures. Commercial versions may be chemically acidified.
- Where / who: home-made and commercial. Paired with gibanica at breakfast in Montenegro, and traditionally eaten with popara and with kačamak. Montenegro is listed among countries where it is made at home or bought.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montenegrin_cuisine — soured milk paired with gibanica at breakfast; https://sr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Кисело_млеко — Montenegro among countries where it is made or bought; general method; https://sr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Попара — popara traditionally eaten with sour milk in parts of Montenegro
- Unsure: the sr. article names Montenegro only in a list, with no Montenegro-specific method.

## Yoghurt (jogurt; jogurt)
- Animal(s): cow (mainly)
- Kind: fermented milk (yoghurt); also drunk and used in cold dishes
- What it is: thick fermented milk, eaten plain and in cold dishes (e.g. cold yoghurt with kačamak or potatoes in Montenegro).
- How it is made (general, from sr. Wikipedia): heat milk to about 85 °C, cool to about 45 °C, add starter (Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus and Streptococcus thermophilus), hold at about 30–45 °C for 4–12 h, then refrigerate. Home method: warm milk to 40–42 °C, add about 1 dl yoghurt per 2 l milk, keep warm 2–3 h, then refrigerate.
- Where / who: home and industrial; part of everyday Montenegrin dishes (en. Wikipedia Montenegrin cuisine lists yoghurt; also "cold yoghurt" with kuvana krtola).
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montenegrin_cuisine — yoghurt listed as a dairy product and in dishes; https://sr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Јогурт — yoghurt method, temperatures, starter cultures; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balkan_cuisine — Montenegro among Balkan cuisines (general)
- Unsure: the Montenegro-specific yoghurt detail is only a dish mention; no Montenegrin yoghurt variety was found.

## Homemade sour cream (pavlaka)
- Animal(s): cow (mainly); sheep and goat where milked on katuns
- Kind: cream (fresh and sour)
- What it is: cream skimmed from the top of milk, sold sweet or sour; English Wikipedia lists "homemade sour cream" under Montenegrin dairy.
- How it is made: cream is skimmed from the surface of non-homogenised milk (sweet cream); souring method not described in the source.
- Where / who: homemade in Montenegro; also made industrially.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montenegrin_cuisine — homemade sour cream listed under Montenegrin dairy; https://sr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Павлака — how cream is skimmed, sweet vs sour, churning source

## Butter (maslac; масло; путер)
- Animal(s): cow (mainly); sheep and goat on katuns
- Kind: butter (churned from cream)
- What it is: butter from sweet or sour cream, a fat product also used in Montenegrin dishes; English Wikipedia lists butter under Montenegrin dairy and notes cicvara uses butter.
- How it is made: cream placed in a churn (historically wooden stick churns) and agitated until the fat clumps; the liquid left behind is buttermilk. Sour-cream butter smells of diacetyl; sweet-cream butter tastes of cream. Stored up to 14 days at or below +5 °C.
- Where / who: home-churned (historically) and industrial; in Montenegro used in cicvara and other dishes.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montenegrin_cuisine — butter listed under Montenegrin dairy; https://sr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Маслац — churning method, cream types, storage, local names
- Unsure: the churning method is general, not Montenegro-specific.

## Sheep milk and katun summer dairy (katun; katunski sir/kajmak)
- Animal(s): sheep (especially); goat; cow where kept
- Kind: pastoral milk and dairy (milk on summer pastures, source of kajmak, butter and sour milk)
- What it is: katuns are summer mountain pastures where livestock are milked, mainly sheep, and where dairy is made. The products are grouped as bijeli mrs ("white food") and include kajmak, butter, sour milk and cheese.
- How it is made: milked on the katun and processed there into kajmak, butter and sour milk; the katun season is the summer move (izdig) from lowland to mountain pasture.
- Where / who: pastoral herders across Montenegro, with named katuns on Sinjajevina, near Durmitor and Biogradska Gora, Katunska nahija around Lovćen, and near Pljevlja and Reževići (Budva).
- Sources: https://sr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Катун — sheep milking on katuns, dairy products (kajmak, butter, sour milk), Montenegrin katun examples and terms
- Unsure: the source is a general katun article; it names Montenegrin katuns as examples, not a Montenegrin-specific recipe. Sheep milk as a standalone drink was not found.
