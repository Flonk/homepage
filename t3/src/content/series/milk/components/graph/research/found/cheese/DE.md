## Harzer Käse (Harzer Roller; Harzer; Harzer Käse)
- Animal(s): Cow (Kuhmilch), made from sour-milk quark
- Kind: sour-milk cheese (Sauermilchkäse), smear-ripened, low-fat
- What it is: A small, strong-smelling sour-milk cheese with a yellow-to-red smear, a Standardsorte under German cheese rules. Only the low-fat version (under 10% fat in dry matter) exists.
- How it is made: Sour-milk quark (over 30% dry matter) mixed with sodium chloride, sodium bicarbonate and calcium carbonate, plus aged culture cheese. Ripened about 24 h at 30 °C, sprayed with Brevibacterium linens (red-smear culture), then 1–3 days at 10–14 °C; cold-stored afterwards, ripening continues from the outside in. The page names no coagulant, cutting or draining step.
- Texture / ripening: Smooth, rind-less surface with a golden-yellow to reddish-brown smear. Young cheese has a white crumbly core that turns translucent with age and can become runny. Mild and tangy when young, pungent with age; caraway is typical.
- Where / who: Originally the northern Harz foothills (Bad Harzburg, Vienenburg, Immenrode); today mostly made outside the Harz, in Hesse, Lower Saxony, Saxony, Saxony-Anhalt and Thuringia. Not a protected name.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harzer_Käse — origin, milk, process, texture, protected status; https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Käse — Harzer Roller listed as a Sauermilchkäse
- Unsure: The Harzer Roller article is about the canary breed of that name; the cheese entry uses only the Harzer Käse article.

## Handkäse (Hessischer Handkäse, Handkäs; Bauernhandkäse, Korbkäse, Stangenkäse, Spitzkäse)
- Animal(s): Not stated in the source; made from lean quark (Magerquark), presumably cow
- Kind: sour-milk cheese (Sauermilchkäse), yellow-smear or noble-mould variant
- What it is: A small, soft sour-milk cheese, traditionally hand-shaped into small round cakes, usually eaten marinated in onion, vinegar and oil ("mit Musik").
- How it is made: Quark broken up with a whisk; salt and sodium bicarbonate added; shaped by hand (now often by machine); about two days at 25–28 °C and 80% humidity, then brine spray with Brevibacterium linens for two more days. The Hausmacher variety gets a white mould (Geotrichum candidum). Noble-mould versions (Penicillium camemberti) ripen longer at lower temperatures. Caraway optional. No coagulant is named, draining is not mentioned.
- Texture / ripening: Mild when young with a white core; with ripening it gets stronger, turns transparent inside, loses its shape and becomes runny.
- Where / who: Historically widespread in Germany; today mainly the Rhine-Main area, Rheinhessen and the Palatinate. "Hessischer Handkäse" has been a protected geographical indication (g.g.A.) in the EU since 23 September 2010; it must be produced and packaged in Hesse, but the curd need not come from there.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handkäse — origin, process, marinade, protected status, history; https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sauermilchkäse — Handkäse variants and ripening types
- Unsure: Milk animal not stated; the curd is presumably cow's milk quark.

## Mainzer Käse (Mainzer Handkäs)
- Animal(s): Cow (Kuhmilch), made from lean sour-milk quark (Matte)
- Kind: sour-milk cheese (Sauermilchkäse), related to Harzer and Handkäse
- What it is: A sour-milk cheese invented in 1813 by a farmer named Kaul in Groß-Gerau (Hesse) and sold at the Mainz weekly market. In Mainz it is usually called "Handkäs" and often served marinated ("Handkäs mit Musik").
- How it is made: Salt and ripening cultures mixed into the quark, which is shaped into small loaves after a few hours; ripened about 24–26 hours. Mould not mentioned.
- Texture / ripening: A smooth, firm dough with a distinctive aroma; high in protein, low in fat, 60–73% water.
- Where / who: Gerauer Land, Hesse, with about 25 dairies around 1900. Protected status not mentioned.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainzer_Käse — origin, quark base, process, texture; https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sauermilchkäse — listed as a standard low-fat sour-milk type
- Unsure: Whether it is still made in quantity today is not stated in the sources.

## Tilsiter (Holsteiner Tilsiter)
- Animal(s): Cow (whole or partly skimmed milk)
- Kind: semi-hard cut cheese with a red-smear rind (Rotschmiere)
- What it is: A semi-hard cheese named after the former East Prussian town of Tilsit (now Sovetsk); made as a wheel or block.
- How it is made: Raw or pasteurised milk (raw-milk versions usually more flavourful). For about six months the thin brownish rind is rubbed with salt water and red-smear cultures. Coagulation, cutting and washing not described.
- Texture / ripening: Pale yellow, supple, very elastic and fairly moist; barley-grain-sized holes and slits. Mild and slightly savoury to sharp. 30–60% fat in dry matter.
- Where / who: Northern and central Europe; "Holsteiner Tilsiter" has a protected geographical indication (g.g.A.) across Schleswig-Holstein since 2013. "Tilsiter Switzerland" is a protected brand for Swiss-made Tilsiter.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tilsiter — composition, rind treatment, texture, protected status; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_cuisine — Tilsiter named as a breakfast cheese

## Limburger
- Animal(s): Cow (raw or pasteurised milk)
- Kind: soft, smear-ripened (Rotschmierkäse)
- What it is: A soft, smear-ripened cheese named after the former Duchy of Limburg; brick-shaped and closely resembles Romadur.
- How it is made: Liquids containing Brevibacterium linens smear bacteria applied at the start and during ripening. Coagulant, cutting and brine washing not described. Ripening two weeks to three months.
- Texture / ripening: Orange to reddish-brown rind, elastic and slightly sticky; pale, creamy interior with small irregular holes. Overripe cheese becomes greasy and shrinks.
- Where / who: German production began in 1830, when Carl Hirnbein started making soft cheese in Missen-Wilhams; concentrated in the Allgäu. Belgian "Fromage de Herve" (PDO since 1996) is a different, protected product.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limburger — origin, smear-ripening, texture, history, Belgian PDO; https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romadur — history and Allgäu link
- Unsure: Coagulant and salting steps are not given in the sources read.

## Romadur
- Animal(s): Cow
- Kind: soft cheese with a red smear rind
- What it is: A soft, stick-shaped cheese with a reddish, sticky rind; milder than Limburger. Originally Belgian; the German article cites Allgäu, where Carl Hirnbein introduced it in the 1830s.
- How it is made: Starter and rennet added; curd cut to hazelnut size and filled without reheating; turned repeatedly, then soaked in brine 12–16 hours; ripened 8–14 days at about 14 °C and over 90% humidity, smeared with red-smear bacteria (mainly Brevibacterium linens).
- Texture / ripening: Rind elastic, slightly sticky and grainy, orange to reddish-brown; interior matte and smooth, white when young and yellowing as it ripens. Fat in dry matter 20, 40 or 60%, absolute fat 8–20%.
- Where / who: Allgäu, Germany (introduced 1830s); widely produced. Protected status not mentioned.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romadur — process, texture, fat levels, history
- Unsure: Origin of the name is uncertain according to the source.

## Allgäuer Bergkäse
- Animal(s): Not stated; made from raw milk (the cow is presumed)
- Kind: mountain cheese (Bergkäse), hard or semi-hard
- What it is: A mountain cheese from the Allgäu, with loaves smaller and lower than Emmentaler's; ripened at least four months, flavour mild-aromatic and nutty, stronger with age.
- How it is made: Raw milk. Coagulant, cutting, cooking, pressing and salting not described. Thickening only by removing water; spices not allowed.
- Texture / ripening: Smaller holes than Emmentaler, darker paste; 45–50% fat in dry matter, weight 15–50 kg. Used as bread topping, in salads, gratins and Kässpätzle.
- Where / who: Allgäu (Bodenseekreis, Lindau, Oberallgäu, Ostallgäu, Ravensburg, Unterallgäu, Kaufbeuren, Kempten, Memmingen). Mostly small operations, alpine huts and valley dairies; 13 producers used the name in 2019. Protected designation of origin (PDO/g.U.) since 1997.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allgäuer_Bergkäse — origin, PDO status, production, flavour, uses
- Unsure: Milk animal is not stated; the article says raw milk only.

## Butterkäse
- Animal(s): Cow
- Kind: semi-hard cut cheese (halbfester Schnittkäse)
- What it is: A mild, slightly tangy yellow semi-hard cheese; reportedly first produced in 1928 at the Staatliche Molkerei Weihenstephan; derived from the Italian Bel Paese.
- How it is made: Mesophilic and thermophilic cultures; a large amount of rennet at a high setting temperature of 40–42 °C; curd formed into mostly round loaves and kept warm at 38–40 °C; brined; ripened 3–4 weeks at 3–7 °C. Cutting and washing not specified.
- Texture / ripening: Semi-firm, sliceable, little or no eye formation; 45–53% fat in dry matter depending on category.
- Where / who: Germany; now made in other countries too; Germany produced about 31,500 tonnes in 2012. No protected status mentioned.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butterkäse — process, fat content, history, production volume

## Allgäuer Weißlacker
- Animal(s): Cow (pasteurised milk)
- Kind: semi-hard cut cheese, rindless
- What it is: A rindless, pale, slightly crumbly semi-hard cheese invented in 1874 in Wertach, southern Allgäu.
- How it is made: Rennet; curd cut and ladled out; two-day brine bath, then regular salting and greasing; about three months in a cool room, then three to four more months wrapped in aluminium foil. Washing not mentioned.
- Texture / ripening: Few small holes, slightly crumbly interior, no rind.
- Where / who: Southern Allgäu (Swabia administrative district, Ravensburg and Bodenseekreis districts). Registered as a PDO in the EU since 26 February 2015; also on Slow Food's Ark of Taste.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weißlacker — process, origin, PDO, texture

## Allgäuer Emmentaler
- Animal(s): Cow (silage-free raw milk, Heumilch, required in the Allgäu version)
- Kind: hard cheese (Hartkäse)
- What it is: The Allgäu version of Emmentaler, with about 45% fat in dry matter; at least 3 months ripening under its rules.
- How it is made: Rennet; propionic acid bacteria added and produce large holes via a secondary fermentation; hay dust as a nucleus; about 12 l milk per kg cheese. Cutting, cooking, pressing and brining not described.
- Texture / ripening: Firm with large holes; long-aged cheeses may show salt crystals.
- Where / who: Allgäu. "Allgäuer Emmentaler" holds EU protected designation of origin status. In Germany, pasteurised versions are sold as "Hartkäse nach Emmentaler-Art", which is not a protected name.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allgäuer_Emmentaler — the page returned the general Emmentaler article, giving the Allgäu status, ripening and German labelling rules
- Unsure: The cited page is the general Emmentaler article; the Allgäu details come from its protected-status section.

## Schichtkäse (Sahneschichtkäse)
- Animal(s): Cow
- Kind: fresh cheese (layered curd)
- What it is: A gelatinous, firm, layered fresh cheese with a pure lactic-acid flavour, distinct from the pasty texture of quark. Higher-fat version (at least 40% fat in dry matter) sold as Sahneschichtkäse.
- How it is made: Milk standardised for fat; starter culture (2–4%) allowed to acidify, then a little rennet; coagulation 3–4 h, curd sets 16–18 h; ladled into moulds in layers, with the fattier middle layer between two leaner ones; drains about 22 h without turning. Modern methods use large vats.
- Texture / ripening: Smooth, glossy, few holes; milky white to cream yellow.
- Where / who: Germany; the source notes Müller stopped making it in 2024, with a few regional dairies left in the Odenwald and the Hannover region. Not protected.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schichtkäse — process, texture, fat levels, producers

## Kochkäse
- Animal(s): Not stated; based on sour-milk or rennet quark
- Kind: sour-milk spread (Sauermilchkäse), processed; legally a cheese product in Germany
- What it is: A viscous, spreadable sour-milk preparation served on bread, often "mit Musik" with onion marinade. Known as Cancoillotte in France, Kachkéis in Luxembourg, Brennkäse in the Odenwald, and Glundner Käse in Kärnten.
- How it is made: Quark drained several hours, mixed with baking soda and rested from one hour to several days until glassy; warmed while stirred to no more than 42 °C until liquid; butter, sometimes cream and egg yolk, salt and usually caraway added. Industrial versions use melting salts.
- Texture / ripening: Permanently viscous and spreadable; not shaped.
- Where / who: Common in Kärnten (Austria), Luxembourg and in German regions; protected status not mentioned.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kochkäse — preparation, texture, regional names
- Unsure: The source says it is not a cheese under German food law; included as an entry because it is an established German table cheese.

## Quark (Speisequark)
- Animal(s): Cow (presumed; the source gives no animal)
- Kind: fresh curd cheese (set mainly by lactic acid bacteria, with a little rennet)
- What it is: Curd made from coagulated milk protein, drained of whey; the basic German fresh cheese.
- How it is made: Skim milk inoculated with 1–2% lactic culture at 20–22 °C; rennet added after 1–3 h; coagulates at about 18 °SH after 6–8 h; whey removed by cloth or sack draining, quark finishers (Schulenburg, Roth, Berge systems), or centrifuge separators, then cooled to 5–6 °C.
- Texture / ripening: Pasty and fresh, unripened. Fat levels as share of dry matter: under 10% (Magerstufe) up to 85% (Doppelrahmstufe).
- Where / who: Germany; regional names include Topfen (Bavaria, Austria), Matte, Bibbeleskäs, Ziebeleskäs, Luckeleskäs, Weißkäse, Glumse (East Prussia). Sauermilchquark is the base of all sour-milk cheeses.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quark_(Lebensmittel) — process, fat levels, regional names

## Würchwitzer Milbenkäse
- Animal(s): Not stated in the source
- Kind: low-fat fresh cheese ripened by cheese mites (Tyrolichus casei)
- What it is: A low-fat fresh cheese seasoned with salt and caraway, shaped into sticks or hand-sized balls with an elderflower panicle inserted so the mites can enter.
- How it is made: Drained and dried fresh cheese; mites kept with the cheese and fed rye flour; their enzymes drive ripening from the outside in; ripened one to twelve months (rind yellow in about four weeks, reddish brown after three months, blackish after a year). The mites are still alive and eaten with the cheese.
- Texture / ripening: Flavour resembles Harzer cheese, from mild to very strong, with a slightly bitter, tingling aftertaste and a salmiak-like smell.
- Where / who: Würchwitz in the Altenburger Land, about 50 km south of Leipzig, and neighbouring areas toward Zeitz. Mites have been bred there since the Middle Ages; under the GDR the practice was banned by food law; revived by teacher Helmut Pöschel. Slow Food Ark of Taste since 2006. Protected status not mentioned.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milbenkäse — process, history, flavour, region
- Unsure: Coagulant not mentioned; milk animal not stated.

## Altenburger Ziegenkäse (g.U.)
- Animal(s): Mainly cow's milk, with at least 15% goat's milk
- Kind: soft, white-mould cheese (Camembert-like surface mould)
- What it is: A cylindrical soft cheese, about 11.5 cm across and 3 cm high (typically 250 g), with optional caraway; a local speciality named for its goat-milk content.
- How it is made: Rennet; curd cut to walnut-sized pieces; caraway added before shaping into loaves; about 10 days ripening. Raw or heated milk and draining/salting not stated.
- Texture / ripening: Pale yellow, smooth paste with few holes; mild but distinctive, with caraway clearly noticeable. At least 30% fat in dry matter; 45% and 60% versions also made.
- Where / who: Thuringia–Saxony border area (Altenburger Land, Greiz, parts of Burgenland, Leipzig, Gera); milk must come from that area. Protected designation of origin (g.U.). Produced by Käserei Altenburger Land in Hartha since 1897 and by Käsemanufaktur Falkenhain.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altenburger_Ziegenkäse — process, region, protected status, history; https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ziegenkäse — described as a registered PDO in the EU

## Olmützer Quargel
- Animal(s): Cow
- Kind: sour-milk cheese with a red-smear rind
- What it is: A small sour-milk cheese sold as sticks or short rolls, with a pungent smell similar to Harzer cheese.
- How it is made: Ripened 24–26 hours; coagulant, cutting, draining and salting not described.
- Texture / ripening: Mildly sour to strong and piquant, with or without a hole.
- Where / who: Originally near Olomouc (Olmütz) in Moravia; now made industrially in Loštice, Czech Republic. Germany and Austria produced it under the Olmützer name until 2015. Protected geographical indication (PGI) in the EU since 2010; only Czech-made cheese may use the name since 2015. Related varieties (Sächsische and Österreichische Quargel) are mentioned in the source.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olmützer_Quargel — origin, PGI, production history

## Bavaria blu (Weiß-Blau-Käse)
- Animal(s): cow (pasteurised milk from the Chiemgau)
- Kind: soft cheese, white rind with blue veins; branded product
- What it is: a German brand of the Bergader Privatkäserei in Waging am See, introduced in 1972. It combines a white camembert-type rind with a blue-marbled interior. 70% fat in dry matter; ideal ripeness four to six weeks. The article gives it a 13% share of branded blue cheese in Germany (2012 figure).
- How it is made: pasteurised cow milk treated with two noble-mould cultures, one for the white rind and one for the blue marbling. The general method for blue cheese, in the Blauschimmelkäse article: blue mould is grown on bread loaves, dried, ground and liquefied, injected into young cheese with a coarse syringe, and the cheese is pierced during ripening so that air reaches the inside. The Bavaria blu article does not say whether it uses the injection step.
- Where / who: Bergader Privatkäserei, Waging am See, Upper Bavaria. Sold in Germany, Austria and abroad.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bavaria_blu — brand, milk, cultures, fat, ripening, market share; https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blauschimmelkäse — general blue cheese method
- Unsure: the injection and piercing steps are cited from the general article, not from Bavaria blu's own process.

## Bergader Edelpilz
- Animal(s): cow (milk from about 1,850 farms in the Alpine foothills, per the article)
- Kind: blue-veined cheese (Blauschimmelkäse)
- What it is: a blue cheese developed by Basil Weixler in the 1920s, similar to Roquefort but made from cow milk. After he lost a court case over the name "Bayerischer Gebirgs-Roquefort", the cheese was sold as Bergader Edelpilz. The article says it has been produced since 1927 and was the company's first nationwide cheese.
- How it is made: not described in the source beyond being a blue-mould cheese.
- Where / who: Bergader Privatkäserei GmbH, Waging am See (and Bad Aibling), founded 1902. The company exports about 27% of its output (2014 figure).
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bergader_Privatkäserei — history, the Roquefort-like cheese, milk supply, export

## Cambozola
- Animal(s): cow (pasteurised milk, with added cream)
- Kind: soft-ripened blue cheese, in the style of a triple cream
- What it is: a German blue cheese with a rich, creamy texture and a mild blue flavour, milder than Gorgonzola piccante. Patented by the German company Hofmeister-Champignon and produced since 1983; the name evokes the protected name Gorgonzola.
- How it is made: cream is added to the milk; the article's composition list gives pasteurised cow milk about 58%, cream about 21%, and cultures (bacterial and mould) about 10% of the ingredients. Two moulds are used: Penicillium camemberti for the white rind and Penicillium roqueforti for the blue veins.
- Where / who: Hofmeister-Champignon, Germany.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambozola — origin, patent, moulds, composition

## Frischkäse (German fresh cheese)
- Animal(s): cow; goat fresh cheese also made (e.g. körniger Ziegen-Frischkäse)
- Kind: fresh cheese, unripened or barely ripened
- What it is: a fresh cheese used as a spread, in dips and creams, and in baking. German cheese rules give it a group of its own: water over 73% of the fat-free cheese mass, so it keeps only briefly and must be chilled. Made in all fat grades and sometimes with herbs or spices. Examples given: Quark, Hüttenkäse (cottage cheese, from skim milk with cream added), Rahm- und Doppelrahmkäse, Mascarpone, Burrata.
- How it is made: milk is heated and given lactic bacteria and a little rennet; the curd forms, is cut with a curd harp and drained to lean quark. Cream is added to reach the fat level, then the mix is passed through to give an even texture. Moulds and yeasts give a slightly nutty or mushroom flavour.
- Where / who: Germany; used as a spread, in dips and creams, and in baking.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frischkäse — German rules, examples, process
- Unsure: not every example's process is given; cottage cheese is only described briefly.

## Ziegenkäse (goat cheese)
- Animal(s): goat
- Kind: cheese family made from goat milk, fresh to hard
- What it is: cheese made from goat milk. In Germany, a cheese declared as Ziegenkäse must be 100% goat milk (Käseverordnung, §14, 8). Varieties run from fresh and soft through mould-ripened to hard, with flavours from mild and creamy to strongly aromatic. It has slightly less fat and casein than cow cheese.
- How it is made: the article gives no process; the one German protected example is the Altenburger Ziegenkäse entry above.
- Where / who: Germany and Switzerland (Geisskäse). The article's list of traditional goat cheeses is mostly French.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ziegenkäse — German legal rule, varieties, composition
- Unsure: the process is not described; the traditional-variety list is not German.
