## Brie (Brie de Meaux, Brie de Nangis, Brie de Melun)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: bloomy-rind, soft
- What it is: A soft cow's-milk cheese named after the Brie region around Seine-et-Marne; pale, under a white mould rind that is typically eaten.
- How it is made: Rennet added to raw milk, warmed to a maximum of 37 °C, curd cast into moulds (sometimes with a perforated ladle called a pelle à brie). Brie de Nangis: soutirage into vat, emprésurage, cutting, manual moulding, drying 4-6 days, then about 1 month affinage in a cellar.
- Texture / ripening: Creamy interior; rind grows a white mould. Brie de Nangis is ripened about one month (fr.wikipedia).
- Where / who: Brie region, Île-de-France / Seine-et-Marne. Brie de Meaux is AOC (1980) and made from raw cow's milk; Brie de Nangis is a regional brie.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brie — soft cow milk, rennet at up to 37 °C, pelle à brie; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brie_de_Meaux — AOC 1980, raw milk, 16 h tank fermentation, curdling about 1 h, cubed curd; https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brie_de_Nangis — make steps (emprésurage, tranchage, moulage), 4-6 days drying, 1 month affinage; https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liste_de_fromages_fran%C3%A7ais (raw list) — Brie AOC and regional brie names.
- Unsure: Brie de Melun is a separate AOC on the lists but its article redirects to Brie; treated as a Brie variant.

## Camembert (Camembert de Normandie)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: bloomy-rind, soft
- What it is: A moist, soft, creamy, surface-ripened cow's-milk cheese first made in the late 18th century in Camembert, Normandy.
- How it is made: Not fully detailed in the sources read. The first camembert was made from unpasteurized milk; the AOC Camembert de Normandie must use raw milk.
- Texture / ripening: Soft, creamy, surface-ripened with a white mould rind.
- Where / who: Normandy. AOC Camembert de Normandie is about 10% of output; most Camembert is made outside Normandy with pasteurized milk.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camembert_de_Normandie — soft surface-ripened cow cheese, late 18th c., AOC raw-milk rule and share of production.
- Unsure: Process steps not found in the article text I read.

## Comté (Gruyère de Comté)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: pressed cooked (hard)

## Beaufort
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: pressed cooked (gruyère family)
- What it is: A firm raw cow's-milk cheese of the gruyère family from the Alpine Savoie region (Beaufortain, Tarentaise, Maurienne).
- How it is made: Milk heated, acidified and coagulated; curds cast into a beechwood hoop that gives a concave shape; pressed about 24 h, unmoulded, then cooled about 24 h more.
- Texture / ripening: Firm; ripened (duration not read).
- Where / who: Savoie, French Alps; AOC.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaufort_(cheese) — raw milk, gruyère family, beechwood hoop, pressing and cooling times; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaufort_(cheese) (intro) — milk from Tarine or Abondance cows in one variety.

## Tomme de Savoie
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: pressed uncooked (tomme)
- What it is: An upland tomme from Savoy: mild, semi-firm, beige paste under a thick brownish-grey rind.
- How it is made: Usually made from skimmed milk left after cream is used for butter or richer cheeses; first pressed, then matured several months in a traditional cellar.

- Where / who: Savoie, French Alps; farm and dairy versions.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomme_de_Savoie_cheese — mild semi-firm, skimmed-milk base, pressing and cellar ripening.

## Cantal (Fourme de Cantal, Cantal Fermier, Cantal Laitier)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: pressed uncooked (firm)
- What it is: An uncooked firm cheese from the Auvergne (Cantal département and adjoining districts), made as a farm (fermier, raw milk) or a factory (laitier, pasteurised) version.
- How it is made: Curd pressed while in the mould, broken and salted, then pressed again in the mould (per the French article, "tome de cantal" is the pressed curd before the final step).
- Texture / ripening: Aged several months; young cheese is sweet and buttery, well ripened is nutty and tangy.
- Where / who: Auvergne, Massif central. Fermier and laitier versions exist; AOP.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantal_(cheese) — two types (fermier raw, laitier pasteurised), months of ageing, taste; https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourme_de_Cantal — pressed uncooked, raw or pasteurised cow milk, tome breaking, salting and re-pressing.

## Salers
- Animal(s): cow (Salers breed, or mixed in some versions)
- Kind: pressed uncooked (semi-hard)
- What it is: A semi-hard cheese from the volcanic Massif Central around Salers, in Cantal.
- How it is made: Pressed, uncooked; the "Tradition Salers" uses milk from the Salers cow only. Salers de Buron Traditional is made in summer in stone huts (burons), from Salers cow milk.
- Texture / ripening: Aged in caves at 6-12 °C for a minimum of 3 months, up to 45 months.
- Where / who: Cantal, Auvergne; made 15 April to 15 November in the tradition version.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salers_(cheese) — pressed uncooked, season, cave ageing temperatures and months, buron tradition.

## Laguiole (Tome de Laguiole)
- Animal(s): cow (Simmental or Aubrac breeds)
- Kind: pressed uncooked
- What it is: A pressed uncooked cheese from the Aubrac plateau (Aveyron), also called Tome de Laguiole or fourme de Laguiole.
- How it is made: Raw, unpasteurised milk collected May to October above 800 m; pressed, uncooked paste; salted.
- Texture / ripening: 45% fat; hazelnut then mild acid taste.
- Where / who: Aubrac, Aveyron, Cantal and Lozère; AOP.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laguiole_(cheese) — pressed uncooked, raw milk, May-October, 800 m, 45% fat; https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tome_de_Laguiole — AOP, Aubrac area of 73 communes.

## Bleu d'Auvergne
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: blue
- What it is: A French blue cheese from Auvergne, one of the cheeses with an AOC.
- How it is made: Blue mould (Penicillium) introduced in the curd; the article says Roussel found rye bread mould gave the veining, and pricking the curd with a needle let air in so the blue could grow.
- Texture / ripening: Blue veins through the paste.
- Where / who: Auvergne; AOC (1975 per the French list).
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bleu_d%27Auvergne — cow milk, AOC, rye-bread mould and needle-pricking history; https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liste_de_fromages_fran%C3%A7ais — AOC 1975.

## Fourme d'Ambert
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: blue (semi-hard)
- What it is: A semi-hard blue cheese, one of France's oldest, from the Auvergne (Ambert area); dates back to Roman times, per the article.
- How it is made: Raw or pasteurised cow milk; inoculated with Penicillium roqueforti spores; aged at least 28 days.
- Texture / ripening: Semi-hard, blue-veined.
- Where / who: Auvergne (Puy-de-Dôme and Loire region); industrial/coop production mostly from pasteurised milk; artisanal and farm (fermier) raw-milk versions are now made.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourme_d%27Ambert — raw cow milk, Penicillium roqueforti, 28-day minimum ageing, farm and raw-milk revival.

## Vacherin Mont d'Or
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: washed-rind, soft (wrapped in spruce bark)
- What it is: A cow's-milk cheese from the Jura/Doubs; the French form (mont d'Or) is distinct from Swiss Vacherin.
- How it is made: Wrapped in a spruce "sangle" band for at least about two weeks, which adds flavour. The French Vacherin du Haut-Doubs is unpasteurised; the Swiss version uses thermised milk.
- Texture / ripening: Soft, spoonable, wood-sangle flavour.
- Where / who: Haut-Doubs / Jura; made in winter when cows come down from pasture.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacherin_Mont_d%27Or — cow milk, spruce sangle, two weeks, unpasteurised French form, winter season.
- Unsure: Both French and Swiss forms share the name; this entry follows the French form.

## Époisses (Époisses de Bourgogne)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: washed-rind, soft (smear-ripened)
- What it is: A legally demarcated smear-ripened cheese from the village of Époisses, Côte-d'Or, between Dijon and Auxerre.
- How it is made: Whole milk heated to about 30 °C; coagulation at least 16 hours; washed in brine and Marc de Bourgogne.
- Texture / ripening: Red-orange rind, soft paste; round, 10 cm or 18 cm.
- Where / who: Burgundy; raw or pasteurised milk allowed.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89poisses — washed in brine and marc, 30 °C, 16-hour coagulation, sizes, raw or pasteurised.

## Maroilles (Marolles, Boulette d'Avesnes)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: washed-rind (pressed, cured in blocks)
- What it is: A cow's-milk cheese from Picardy and Nord-Pas-de-Calais, named after the village of Maroilles; sold as rectangular blocks with a moist orange-red rind and strong smell.
- How it is made: Curd shaped and salted, removed from the mould, then dried about 10 days in a ventilated room where a light bacterial coating forms; then brushed, washed and cellared at least five weeks (often up to four months).
- Texture / ripening: Strong smell; orange-red washed rind.
- Where / who: Nord / Picardy; AOC. Boulette d'Avesnes is a related name in the lists whose Wikipedia page redirects to Maroilles.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maroilles_cheese — block form, ten-day drying, washing, five-week to four-month cellaring; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boulette_d%27Avesnes — redirect to Maroilles.

## Neufchâtel
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: bloomy-rind, mould-ripened (soft)
- What it is: A soft, slightly crumbly mould-ripened cheese from the Pays de Bray in Normandy, one of the oldest French cheeses; it resembles Camembert or Brie but is saltier and sharper.
- How it is made: Mostly raw cow's milk, lactic (acid) curd, moulded and matured.
- Texture / ripening: Matured 8-10 weeks; 100-600 g; the heart-shaped (cœur) format is typical.
- Where / who: Neufchâtel-en-Bray, Seine-Maritime; AOC since 1969 per the French article.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuf%C3%A2tel_(cheese) — bloom rind, saltier than Brie, 8-10 weeks; https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/C%C5%93ur_de_Neuf%C3%A2tel — AOC 1969, raw-milk majority, lactic, at least 10 days affinage, 100-600 g.

## Banon
- Animal(s): goat
- Kind: natural-rind (wrapped in chestnut leaves), soft goat cheese
- What it is: A French goat cheese made near the town of Banon in Provence; also called Banon à la feuille.
- How it is made: Unpasteurised goat's milk; seasoned with salt and pepper, doused in vinegar and eau-de-vie, left in a cool cellar to ferment for about two weeks, then dipped in eau-de-vie and wrapped in softened chestnut leaves.
