## Tzfatit (Tzfat cheese; Hebrew גבינה צפתית, gvina tzfatit; also "safed cheese")
- Animal(s): sheep (original); also cow, goat, water buffalo (Israeli varieties)
- Kind: salted, semi-hard, brined-style pressed cheese (called "white brine cheese, similar to feta" in some English sources)
- What it is: A salty, semi-hard Israeli cheese first made in Safed (Tzfat) in the Upper Galilee, now sold in wedges with varying fat and salt levels.
- How it is made: Milk pasteurised at about 72 °C; curdled mainly with a casein-acting enzyme plus added lactic cultures; calcium chloride added to replace calcium lost in pasteurisation. Whey is drained, the curd is pressed into straw baskets (now often plastic ones imprinted with the same pattern) and turned several times over a few hours, then salted. Aged 3 to 12 months per English Wikipedia. Exact coagulant strength and brine times not given.
- Texture / ripening: mild, elastic, low fat when fresh; aged versions grated for cooking. Water-buffalo versions are usually flavoured with nigella seeds.
- Where / who: Safed (Tzfat), Israel. Founding producer HaMeiri Dairy (Arzoni family from Persia); Kadosh Dairy (Kadosh family from Morocco) also named. Jewish cheese production in Safed dates to at least the 16th century; the industry was disrupted by the 1837 earthquake. Not confirmed as a protected name.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tzfat_cheese — origin (~1840 at HaMeiri Dairy), milk types, process (72 °C, enzymes, calcium chloride, straw baskets), ageing 3–12 months, water-buffalo nigella version; https://he.wikipedia.org/wiki/גבינה_צפתית — Hebrew description (salty, semi-hard), cow or sheep milk, Kadosh Dairy, 16th-century history; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Israeli_cheeses — listed as an Israeli variety, goat or sheep
- Unsure: sources differ on the milk (sheep originally; cow, goat or buffalo in current varieties). Some English sources and the Israeli cuisine article describe it as "similar to feta".

## Gvina levana (גבינה לבנה, "white cheese"; also quark-like soft white cheese)
- Animal(s): cow's milk per English Wikipedia; Hebrew Wikipedia does not name the animal
- Kind: fresh, soft, unripened, spreadable; lactic-acid cheese
- What it is: A soft white Israeli cheese, similar to quark and cream cheese, one of the best-selling cheeses in Israel and a key ingredient in Israeli cheesecake.
- How it is made: Pasteurised milk cooled to about 30 °C, acidified with a mesophilic lactic starter to about pH 4.6 (casein's isoelectric point). Some producers add rennet. Part of the whey is drained by hanging in cloth or by centrifuge.
- Texture / ripening: loose, spreadable, mildly sour; no ageing.
- Where / who: Israel. Sold in 5%, 9% and ½% fat versions. English Wikipedia says the original recipes used pig rennet, later approved as kosher because the amount was negligible; the cheese may have come from Germany with the Templers, who called it Weißkäse. No producers named.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gvina_levana — milk, fat levels, Templer origin, rennet note; https://he.wikipedia.org/wiki/גבינה_לבנה — pasteurised milk, starter, pH 4.6, whey drainage, one of the best-selling Israeli cheeses; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Israeli_cheeses — soft spreadable, cheesecake use
- Unsure: Hebrew source does not name the animal; the English source does (cow).

## Gvina bulgarit (גבינה בולגרית, "Bulgarian cheese"; Brinza / Bulgarit)
- Animal(s): sheep, goat, or cow, or a blend (Bulgarian and Balkan tradition; in Israel listed as sheep per the list page)
- Kind: brined, pressed, crumbly; firmer than feta
- What it is: A brined, pressed white cheese from the Balkans, brought to Israel by Bulgarian Jews, and common in bourekas and sambusak.
- How it is made: pressed (kevisha) and brined; not detailed further in the sources read.
- Texture / ripening: crumbly, slightly rough, tangy, salty; fat content in Israel about 5% to 24%.
- Where / who: Balkans origin; made in Israel. Brinza in Israel is the same family (Meiri dairy named in the Israeli cuisine article as producing "Bulgarian cheese").
- Sources: https://he.wikipedia.org/wiki/גבינה_בולגרית — Hebrew name, sheep/goat/cow milk, pressed (kevisha), fat 5–24% in Israel, Balkan origin, distinct from feta; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Israeli_cheeses — sheep, Bulgarian Jews, bourekas/sambusak; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israeli_cuisine — "known in Israel as Bulgarian cheese," produced by Meiri dairy
- Unsure: milk type varies by source (sheep in the Israeli list; sheep, goat or cow in the Hebrew article).

## Akkawi (عكاوي; Akko cheese)
- Animal(s): cow's milk (usual); goat or sheep also used
- Kind: semi-hard, smooth, white brined cheese; does not melt well
- What it is: A white brine cheese named after the city of Acre (Akka), now produced across the region and popular among Arab Israelis.
- How it is made: milk heated to 35–40 °C, rennet added, curdled about one hour; whey squeezed out through cloth; curd packed by hand into square hoops; cured in salted whey brine for two days. Stored up to a year.
- Texture / ripening: mild, lightly salty; eaten plain or with fruit, used in pastries.
- Where / who: Acre (present-day Israel); English Wikipedia names Palestine as country of origin and says it is produced largely in Israel and Palestine. Palestinian mezze staple. Not a protected name in the sources read.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkawi_cheese — milk, process, two-day brine, one-year storage, Acre origin, production in Israel and Palestine; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Israeli_cheeses — cow or goat, popular among Arab Israelis
- Unsure: origin is contested between Palestinian and Israeli framings; included here because it is produced in Israel.

## Labneh (לבנה; labaneh)
- Animal(s): cow, goat or sheep (depends on the milk used); camel and other milks in the Middle East
- Kind: soft, spreadable strained-yogurt cheese; dried balls in olive oil also made
- What it is: A salted strained yogurt, thicker than Greek yogurt, eaten as a dip, mezze or sandwich filling; the Israeli list describes it as a soft, creamy, tangy spreadable cheese served with herbs and olives.
- How it is made: yogurt drained through cloth until soft-cheese texture, then salted; longer straining for labneh. Balls rolled and preserved in olive oil (labaneh biz-zayt) keep over a year and can take on za'atar or spice coatings.
- Texture / ripening: soft and spreadable; oil-preserved balls become sourer with age.
- Where / who: Levant-wide; listed among Israeli cheeses; Galilee dairy labneh production is only mentioned in an unopened Haaretz citation, so not confirmed here.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labneh — straining, salting, oil-preserved balls, Palestine mention; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Israeli_cheeses — soft, creamy, served with herbs and olives; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israeli_cuisine — labneh named in dairy section
- Unsure: the English Labneh article does not mention Israel; Israeli-specific production is only inferred from the Israeli cuisine and list pages.

## Jameed (ג'מיד; Hebrew: אבן יוגורט, "yogurt stone")
- Animal(s): goat or sheep (Bedouin; in Jordan goat's milk per Wikipedia)
- Kind: dried curd, a dried strained-yogurt or labneh ball (classed as a cheese in the kashk infobox)
- What it is: A hard, dried yogurt or labneh ball, used in Bedouin cooking, including mansaf, and kept without refrigeration.
- How it is made: yogurt hung in cloth to drain into labneh, shaped into balls and sun-dried for several days to about two weeks until hard. Modern versions boil, strain and salt sour yogurt.
- Texture / ripening: hard, dry; softens with a little water; keeps a long time.
- Where / who: Bedouin communities in Israel (mainly the Judea-Samaria area per the Hebrew article) and Jordan; also Egypt and Syria. Also called kashk in Iran.
- Sources: https://he.wikipedia.org/wiki/ג'מיד — Hebrew name, goat or sheep milk, drying process, Bedouin use in Israel and Jordan; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jameed — dried yogurt, drying method, Jordan use, Jerusalem souq caption; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bedouin_cuisine — goat-milk jameed in Bedouin cuisine
- Unsure: the Judea-Samaria location is a West Bank reference; the English Jameed page does not mention Israel in text.

## Halloumi (חלומי)
- Animal(s): sheep and goat (traditional); cow's milk in modern commercial versions
- Kind: semi-hard, brined, unripened cheese; chewy, grilled or fried
- What it is: Cypriot brined cheese, made locally in Israel and eaten fried or grilled.
- How it is made: rennet at 30–34 °C, curds pressed, then scalded in hot whey.
- Texture / ripening: semi-hard, chewy, creamy, salty; fresh and mature versions.
- Where / who: Cyprus origin; made locally in Israel per the list page.

## Kasseri (כשרי)
- Animal(s): sheep (up to 20% goat allowed)
- Kind: medium-hard pasta filata (stretched-curd) cheese
- What it is: A pale yellow Greek-origin cheese brought to Israel by Greek Jews and now made locally, used on salads and pizza.
- How it is made: milk heated to 36 °C, rennet, curd cut and cooked at 38–40 °C, drained, pressed, stretched in 70–80 °C water, salted, aged at least 3 months at 18 °C.
- Texture / ripening: smooth, chewy, hard rind; nutty, slightly sweet.
- Where / who: Greece origin; made in Israel per the list page.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kasseri — milk, stretching process, ageing 3 months; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Israeli_cheeses — Greek Jews, made locally
- Unsure: the Israel link comes only from the list page.
