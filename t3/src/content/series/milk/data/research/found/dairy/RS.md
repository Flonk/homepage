## Kajmak (кајмак; stari/zreli kajmak; also called kaymak)
- Animal(s): cow (most common in Serbia, per the 2024 Foods paper and the Serbian Wikipedia); sheep, goat or buffalo also named for kajmak generally
- Kind: cream skin from boiled milk, mildly fermented; a dairy spread (not a cheese)
- What it is: thick, creamy, about 60% milk fat; eaten at breakfast with bread (lepinja s kajmakom), in pljeskavica and Karađorđeva šnicla; in western Serbia it is a traditional household product sold at open markets.
- How it is made: boil raw milk slowly, simmer about two hours at very low heat (en.wikipedia); pour hot milk into shallow vessels and keep about 80 °C for the first two hours, then cool gradually; after about 12 hours skim the cream at about 10–15 °C (no warmer than about 18 °C) to avoid leaving fat in the milk; allow 24 hours or more, longer in winter; drain, salt, layer with salt between layers; ripen at about 15–18 °C for 15–20 days, then cool storage. Young kajmak is mild and keeps 1–2 weeks; mature kajmak (stari/zreli) is salted, sharper, yellowish, ripened up to a year, and can be sealed under clarified butter (sr.wikipedia, en.wikipedia).
- Where / who: central and western Serbia (Čačak, Užice, Kruševac, Zlatibor, Tara, Toplica, Kopaonik, Rudnik); the know-how is on Serbia's national register of intangible cultural heritage (2016). Mostly home or small-dairy production; the Foods 2024 paper describes it as sold by small-scale producers at open markets.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kajmak — production steps, Serbian uses, stari/zreli variants; https://sr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Кајмак — process temperatures, regions, heritage entry; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbian_cuisine — breakfast, pljeskavica, šnicla uses; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6999267/ — Serbian kajmak from cow's milk, can be eaten fresh or ripened up to a year; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11119881/fullTextXML — kajmak from cow's milk, sold at open markets, western Serbia fat-separation method, microbial survey of 112 samples
- Unsure: the en.wikipedia page says kajmak is made from buffalo, sheep or goat as well; for Serbia the sources name cow's milk. No Pirot or Leskovac kajmak tradition was found on the pages checked.

## Kiselo mleko (киселo млеко; kiselo mlijeko)
- Animal(s): cow (milk type not stated in the sources read; cow is the usual Serbian source)
- Kind: soured / fermented milk (drink and spoonable; in Serbian cuisine it is also a breakfast item and an appetizer base)
- What it is: the everyday soured milk of Serbia; the Balkan review calls it the local name for yogurt in Serbia and North Macedonia; Serbia is among former-Yugoslav countries where it is bought or made at home.
- How it is made: home method is to leave milk to sour for a day in a warm place, often near the stove (sr.wikipedia / en.wikipedia Soured milk); Balkan review gives incubation and cooling steps but no temperatures or times for Serbia; in the Serbian Wikipedia's cuisine linkage it is treated as a strained-yogurt-like product.
- Where / who: nationwide, home and industrial.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soured_milk — generic soured milk, traditional home method; https://sr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Кисело_млеко — Serbia home-made and bought, warm-day souring; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6999267/ — kiselo mleko as local name for yogurt in Serbia, incubation and cooling; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fermented_milk_products — kiselo mleko listed for Serbia and North Macedonia; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbian_cuisine — listed among appetizers and salads
- Unsure: Serbian and Bosnian usage overlaps (kiselo mleko / kiselo mlijeko); the sources do not give the animal milk for Serbia.

## Jogurt (јогурт; yogurt)
- Animal(s): cow (typical; the sources do not specify the animal for Serbia)
- Kind: fermented milk (yogurt; also sold as a drink)
- What it is: standard yogurt; served with burek and other baked goods in the Balkans, and listed in Serbian cuisine as a salad/appetizer base; yogurt is a staple across Southeastern Europe and the Balkans.
- How it is made: industrial: heat milk to about 85 °C, cool to about 45 °C, add culture (Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus and Streptococcus thermophilus), hold at 30–45 °C for 4–12 hours, chill; home: heat milk to 40–42 °C, add about 1 dl of yogurt per 2 litres of milk, keep warm 2–3 hours, then refrigerate (en.wikipedia Yogurt).
- Where / who: nationwide; home and industrial.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogurt — culture, temperatures, home method, Balkan staple status; https://sr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Јогурт — Serbian article, same process, no Serbian variants named; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbian_cuisine — jogurt in appetizers and salads, served at breakfast
- Unsure: the Serbian article describes no Serbian-specific yogurt variant (bakterijski napitak was not found on the page); Serbian home-process figures are the generic ones.

## Tarator (таратор; cold yogurt-cucumber dish)
- Animal(s): cow (via yogurt; not stated)
- Kind: dish where yogurt is the main ingredient (cold soup or dip)
- What it is: cold summer soup or liquid salad of yogurt, cucumber, garlic, oil, dill and sometimes walnut; a Balkan dish shared with Bulgaria, Turkey (cacık) and Greece (tzatziki).
- How it is made: yogurt mixed with grated cucumber, garlic, oil, salt, dill, optionally water; served chilled. No yogurt-making step for Serbia is given on the pages read.
- Where / who: Serbia (per the Serbian cuisine page); also Balkans.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarator — Balkan cold yogurt dish, ingredients (Serbia not named on the page itself); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbian_cuisine — tarator listed as yogurt with cucumber in Serbian cuisine
- Unsure: the tarator article does not name Serbia; the Serbian cuisine page is the only source tying it to Serbia.

## Kisela pavlaka and pavlaka (кисела павлака; sour cream; sweet cream)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: soured cream (kisela pavlaka); cream (slatka pavlaka)
- What it is: sour cream used in stews, pies, salads, and as a condiment with savory meats and cold cuts; in larger markets labelled kisela pavlaka with 15–30% fat, historically closer to clotted cream.
- How it is made: sour cream is soured cream (mesophilic culture, minimum 0.5% acidity; some use rennet); sweet cream is skimmed from unhomogenised fresh milk, while whey cream is from cheese whey. Serbian production temperatures were not found.
- Where / who: nationwide; mostly industrial in large markets, sold as smetana or pavlaka in rural areas.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smetana_(dairy_product) — Serbian kisela pavlaka 15–30% fat, uses, rural smetana/pavlaka naming; https://sr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Павлака — sweet cream from skimmed milk, whey cream, sour cream form; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fermented_milk_products — kisela pavlaka listed for Serbia; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbian_cuisine — sour cream/smetana eaten with bread at breakfast
- Unsure: fat levels and souring method for Serbian sour cream are from a generic source; no Serbian production steps found.

## Mlaćenica (млаћеница; buttermilk)
- Animal(s): cow (not stated for Serbia)
- Kind: buttermilk (churned or cultured)
- What it is: thin liquid left after churning butter from cream (traditional); now mostly cultured milk like yogurt, drunk and used in cooking and marinades; common in the Balkans.
- How it is made: traditional: cream left to rise, naturally soured before churning; cultured: pasteurised homogenised milk inoculated with lactic bacteria (en.wikipedia Buttermilk, sr.wikipedia Млаћеница). No temperatures or times given for Serbia.
- Where / who: Balkans and Serbia (Serbian article is the Serbian-language page of this item); home and industrial.
- Sources: https://sr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Млаћеница — traditional churned origin, cultured form, drinking and cooking; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buttermilk — cultured buttermilk method and Serbian interlanguage link
- Unsure: the pages do not name a Serbian-specific mlaćenica variant or Serbian use detail beyond general Balkan use.

## Surutka (сурутка; whey)

- Kind: whey (dairy by-product; drink or cooking liquid in Serbia)

## Kefir (кефир)
- Animal(s): cow, goat, sheep (typical; Serbia not named for animal)
- Kind: fermented milk drink (grains)
- What it is: a commonly consumed dairy beverage in Serbia that often accompanies savory pastries (per the Serbian cuisine page).
- How it is made: grains added at 2–5% to milk, ferment 12–24 hours at 20–25 °C, strain grains and reuse; freeze-dried cultures also used (en.wikipedia Kefir).
- Where / who: nationwide; home and industrial.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbian_cuisine — kefir drink with savory pastries; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kefir — production steps and cultures (Serbia not named)
- Unsure: Serbian kefir-specific sources not found; the en.wikipedia kefir page does not mention Serbia.

## Sutlijaš (сутлијаш; rice pudding in milk)
- Animal(s): cow (milk type not stated in the source)
- Kind: milk dessert (sweet rice in milk)
- What it is: sweet rice dessert simmered in milk, sugar added near the end, served warm or cold; common in Serbian, Bosnian, Macedonian and Bulgarian cuisines.
- How it is made: rice simmered in milk on low heat until the starch thickens it into a soft creamy mass, grains stay visible; cinnamon, vanilla sugar or lemon zest as flavour; no temperatures or times given.
- Where / who: Serbia and the Balkans; home.
- Sources: https://sr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Сутлијаш — ingredients, preparation, Serbian cuisine mention; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbian_cuisine — sutlijaš listed under desserts
- Unsure: milk type not specified.
