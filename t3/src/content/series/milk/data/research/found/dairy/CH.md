## Zigerkrapfen (Zigerkrapfen; Ziger filling)
- Animal(s): cow (probable, not stated by source)
- Kind: fried pastry / sweet (fritter) with a dairy filling
- What it is: Fritters with a sweet, flavoured filling made from Ziger, a whey-based fresh cheese. Known as a specialty of central Switzerland, made since the 18th century.
- How it is made: Ziger is made by heating whey to about 90 °C, acidifying it so the protein curdles, straining it into pierced moulds and selling it the next day. The source gives no dough, filling flavourings or frying method.
- Where / who: Central Switzerland; home and bakery tradition.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zigerkrapfen — what the fritter is, Ziger filling, central Swiss origin, 18th-century history; https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ziger — Ziger whey-process steps (about 92 °C, acid, moulds)
- Unsure: The page does not name the milk animal (probably cow, since the whey comes from Swiss hard cheese). Ziger itself is classed as a cheese, so it is excluded as an entry; this entry is kept because the fritter is a dish with a dairy filling.

## Gruyère cream (Crème de Gruyère; Greyerzer Doppelrahm)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: double cream (thick cream), eaten as dessert and savoury accompaniment
- What it is: A double cream from the canton of Fribourg, named after the Gruyères region. Fat content around 50%, above the 45% minimum for Swiss double cream.
- How it is made: The source does not describe production steps, temperatures or times.
- Where / who: Fribourg; sold in all dairies there and the default cream in the canton. Listed in Switzerland's culinary heritage.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gruyère_cream — fat content, Fribourg origin, serving with meringue, Bénichon meal, heritage listing, 19th-century documentation
- Unsure: The source's reference URL suggests a German name (Greyerzer Doppelrahm), but the article text does not state it. No production method is given.

## Nidel (Nidle, Nidelrahm; Rahm/cream grades)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: cream (Rahm); Swiss general name for cream
- What it is: Nidel is the Swiss-German word for Rahm (cream). In canton Bern "Nydle/Niidle" means cream in general, while in Obwalden it means whipped cream. Swiss fat grades: Halbrahm (about 15%), Vollrahm (about 35%), Doppelrahm (at least 45%). Cream labelled Sahne or Rahm must contain at least 10% fat.
- How it is made: Modern method is centrifugal separation, leaving skim milk with 0.03–0.06% fat; the cream is then standardised to the target fat content. Natural creaming (standing unhomogenised milk) is the older method.
- Where / who: Nationwide; used in Nidelfladen (cream tart/flat cake) and Nidelwähe (Vully-style tart, dialect "Nidlechueche").
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nidel — name, regional use, fat grades, separation method; https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schweizer_Küche — Nidelwähe and Nidelfladen as Swiss dishes, Nidlechueche dialect name
- Unsure: The Nidel article does not say how Nidel is skimmed or what it is used for beyond the name; the Nidelwähe/Nidelfladen uses come from the cuisine article, not from the Nidel article.

## Butter (Anken, Anke; Swiss grades)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: butter
- What it is: Swiss butter; the dialect name is Anken or Anke. Swiss rules define four grades: Vorzugsbutter (at least 82% fat, fresh cream, sold unstored), Kochbutter (frozen Vorzugsbutter, stored up to nine months), Käsereibutter, and Bratbutter (clarified butter for high heat). Switzerland produced 37,710 tonnes of cow's-milk butter in 2022.
- How it is made: Not described on the page beyond the grade definitions (fresh cream for Vorzugsbutter; Bratbutter is clarified).
- Where / who: Nationwide, industrial and dairy; summer cream is lower, so butter is stored for winter.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butter — Swiss grades, 2022 output, Anken/Anke dialect name, seasonal storage and imports
- Unsure: The Swiss butter section does not describe Alpine or cream-ripening methods. Ankenzelten (Glarus) is a butter-based dish, listed under leads.

## Condensed milk (sweetened and evaporated; Anglo-Swiss, Nestlé)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: sweetened condensed milk and evaporated (unsweetened) milk
- What it is: A concentrated milk product developed in Switzerland. Anglo-Swiss Condensed Milk Company founded 1866 at Cham; Nestlé added condensed milk in 1878, and Henri Nestlé is called one of its main creators.
- How it is made: Milk is clarified and standardised, heated to 85–90 °C for several seconds, water is evaporated, sugar is added to a 9:11 sugar-to-evaporated-milk ratio for sweetened versions, then cooled while lactose crystallises. Sweetened condensed milk typically contains about 45% sugar, 8% fat and 20% non-fat solids, according to a source cited on the page.
- Where / who: Industrial; Cham and Vevey.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensed_milk — Cham 1866 factory, evaporated milk process, heating and sugar ratio; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nestlé — condensed milk added in 1878, Henri Nestlé's role
- Unsure: The Condensed milk article does not mention Henri Nestlé; his role comes from the Nestlé article. Product names (Milchmädchen) appear only as a note on the condensed milk article, which describes it as the Swiss original of La Lechera, with no further detail.

## Farine lactée Henri Nestlé (milk-based infant food; infant formula)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: milk-based infant food (infant formula precursor)
- What it is: Milk-based baby food developed by Henri Nestlé and marketed from Vevey from 1867 under the name Farine lactée Henri Nestlé. The company kept the name until 1905 after Nestlé retired in 1875.
- How it is made: Not described on the page.
- Where / who: Industrial; Vevey; sold as a baby product from 1877 onwards on the company's listing.

## Rivella (whey-based soft drink; Mivella variant)
- Animal(s): cow (whey)
- Kind: carbonated soft drink made from milk serum (whey)
- What it is: A Swiss soft drink introduced in 1952 by Rivella AG, made from milk whey (milk serum) with lactose, lactic acid and minerals. Among Switzerland's most popular drinks. Flavoured lines include Rivella Red (1952), Blue (1959) and Green Tea (1999); discontinued lines include Yellow (2008–2011).
- How it is made: Whey is treated, and fruit concentrate, spices, flavourings and sugar are added. No temperatures or steps are given.
- Where / who: Industrial; Rothrist (moved from Stäfa). Developed from an earlier whey-based beer recipe by Robert Barth and a biologist.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rivella — whey base, 1952 launch, Rothrist, origin from whey beer recipe; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rivella — product line dates (same page, second read); https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molke — Rivella described as a milk-serum drink with fruit concentrate, spices and sugar
- Unsure: Migros's own-brand version, Mivella, was stocked until 2008 (same Rivella page). Prego, a 1956 Migros-revived drink, is not confirmed as whey-based.

## Kappeler Milchsuppe (Kappel milk soup)
- Animal(s): cow (milk)
- Kind: milk soup with bread (historic dish)
- What it is: A soup of milk and bread that Zug and Zürich soldiers shared at Kappel am Albis in June 1529, during the First Kappel War. Its pot became a symbol of negotiated peace and national identity.
- How it is made: The source gives no recipe, amounts, cooking steps or times. A linked Zug church recipe for 50 people is not available to me.
- Where / who: Kappel am Albis, cantons Zug and Zürich; still served to mark a settled dispute.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kappeler_Milchsuppe — historical account, 1529 event, modern symbolic use, ingredient attribution (milk from Zug, bread from Zürich)
- Unsure: The ingredient attribution is hedged ("sollen" — reportedly). The soup's recipe is not documented on this page.

## Joghurt (yoghurt) and Sauermilch (legal naming)
- Animal(s): cow (most), goat and sheep (not confirmed for Swiss yoghurt on the page)
- Kind: fermented milk (yoghurt)
- What it is: Yoghurt is eaten daily, often with muesli. In Switzerland a product may be called Joghurt only if it contains at least 10 million colony-forming units of micro-organisms per gram; otherwise it must be labelled Sauermilch. Four fat grades: whole, partly skimmed, skimmed, and cream-enriched.
- How it is made: Not described beyond the microbial threshold; the page mentions an Agroscope 2023 study on raw-milk yoghurt safety.
- Where / who: Industrial and dairy; raw-milk yoghurt also made.
- Sources: https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joghurt — legal threshold, Sauermilch labelling, fat grades, additives limit (30 g per 100 g), raw-milk note; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_cheeses_and_dairy_products — yoghurt eaten daily with muesli
- Unsure: Swiss-specific yoghurt history and brands were not found. Sauermilch is listed as the legal name for non-yoghurt fermented milk; no traditional Swiss sour milk recipe is described.

- Where / who: Swiss spa and resort towns (not named on the page); medicinal use.
