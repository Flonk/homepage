## Chakery (thiakry, thiacry; Wolof cakri; Fula bodé; Bambara dèguè)
- Animal(s): cow (milk, sweetened condensed milk or yoghurt as the liquid)
- Kind: sweet / dessert (millet couscous pudding)
- What it is: A sweet millet couscous dessert with dried fruit and spice, eaten as an after-meal sweet across West Africa. The Gambian and Senegalese versions are the ones the source describes first.
- How it is made: Millet or wheat granules are mixed with milk, sweetened condensed milk or yoghurt, then combined with raisins or dried fruit and desiccated coconut and spiced with nutmeg. No temperatures or times given.
- Where / who: Gambia and Senegal; earliest recorded recipe linked to the Fulani of northern Senegal, herders. Home cooking.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gambian_cuisine — lists chakery and its milk, condensed milk or yoghurt options; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chakery — names (Wolof, Fula, Bambara), milk-based preparation, Fulani origin.

## Lakh (ngalakh; lax in Wolof means "milk")
- Animal(s): cow (sour milk)
- Kind: sweet / porridge (dish with milk)
- What it is: A rolled millet porridge from Senegalese and Gambian cooking, served sweet. The Wolof name literally means milk.
- How it is made: Millet flour is sieved, water is dribbled in so it forms clumps that are worked into small granules, then boiled into a thin porridge. The source says it is made with sour milk and served with sugar. Lakh sow adds cream on top. Ngalakh is topped with a baobab-fruit, peanut and vanilla sauce (njineh jobe).
- Where / who: Gambia and Senegal; used at naming ceremonies and weddings, and for iftar and Eid among Muslims, and to break the Good Friday fast among Catholics. Home cooking.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakh_(food) — preparation, sour milk, cream variant, ceremonial use; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gambian_cuisine — Wolof name lax meaning milk, Serer historic version flavoured with honey and fermented milk before sugar was available; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC13656378/fullTextXML — food table: "Rui or Lakh (millet porridge); served with sour cream and milk" (Gambian adolescent diet study).
- Unsure: The Wikipedia article is about the Senegalese dish; the sour milk is stated, but whether every Gambian version uses it is not confirmed. The article does not give a source for the Gambian wording.

## Bouyi (bouye; Serer mbudaay-baak; baobab juice)
- Animal(s): cow (milk)
- Kind: drink
- What it is: A baobab-fruit drink with milk and sugar, drunk cold or at room temperature. Frozen, it is sold as "ice" on streets and beside buses.
- How it is made: Baobab fruit, milk and sugar. No quantities or steps given.
- Where / who: Gambia and Senegal, sold street-side. Home and street trade.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gambian_cuisine — ingredients, Serer name, frozen street "ice" form.
- Unsure: Only one source (the Gambian cuisine article). The baobab article did not confirm the milk version (it mentions no baobab milk drink at all), so treat the milk ingredient as stated by one source.

- Kind: dish eaten with milk (millet dish with fermented milk)
- What it is: A Serer millet dish, described as a favourite Serer food, eaten for breakfast with fermented milk, or with cream and sugar, much like a cereal.
- How it is made: No steps given in the source.
- Where / who: Serer, Senegal and Gambia; household breakfast.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serer_people — chere (saay) eaten with fermented milk, cream and sugar.
- Unsure: One sentence in the source. It is a millet dish with milk on the side, so it may fall under "dishes where milk is the main ingredient" only loosely. Local name and fermented-milk type not confirmed.

## Lait caillé (sour curdled milk; Senegal, shared with Gambia not confirmed)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: fermented milk
- What it is: The main traditional fermented milk in Senegal, naturally curdled. The Senegal milk review calls it the dominant product of that country's dairy market.
- How it is made: Raw or pasteurised milk left to ferment 12–24 h depending on the season, in vessels called lahals that are reused batch to batch with back-slopping. The fat is removed, and the curd is homogenised with a wooden stick called a burgal.
- Where / who: Senegal; informal dairy market, household and small-scale processors. Gambia not named in this source.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9656659/fullTextXML — Senegal fermented milk description, times, lahal and burgal.
- Unsure: Gambian use is not confirmed by any page read. It is listed here as a Senegal neighbour product that may also be made in The Gambia.

- Animal(s): not stated in the source (milk animal not named)
- Kind: fermented milk
- What it is: A yoghurt that Gambian mothers and grandmothers name among the animal-source foods for young children, alongside fish, milk and meat.
- How it is made: Not described in the source.
- Where / who: Household food in Keneba (rural) and Brikama (semi-urban); respondents say it is more available in the rainy season.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12423624/fullTextXML — focus groups listing yoghurt, milk and meat as animal-source foods; quote "During the rainy season we have yoghurt here."
- Unsure: Local name, animal and whether it is made at home or bought are not given. Only the focus-group discussion supports it; no production description was found.
