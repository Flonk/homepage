## Doogh (دوغ; also dugh, tan)
- Animal(s): cow, sheep, goat (yogurt base; the source does not name one milk for the drink)
- Kind: fermented milk drink (savoury, salted)
- What it is: A cold, savoury Iranian drink made from fermented milk, served with meals (e.g. dizi, chelow kabab); it is not simply diluted yogurt. Sold industrially in PET bottles as well as made at home.
- How it is made: Milk and plain yogurt mixed at about 15:1, heated gently until uniformly warm, salted, then kept in a closed container in a warm place for 3–4 days; longer fermentation makes it more savoury. Herbs such as thyme are sometimes added (per Katz and Nishinari, as cited on the article).
- Where / who: Across Iran; the article says it was drunk in ancient Persia. Both home and factory products (the 2025 BPA study tested three commercial brands).
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doogh — definition, production steps, serving context; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12375751/fullTextXML — three commercial doogh brands in PET bottles, sold in Iran; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11733677/fullTextXML — doogh salt content in an Iranian industrial fermented-dairy survey.
- Unsure: Wikipedia infobox lists water as an ingredient, but its production steps do not use it.

## Mast (ماست) — yogurt, incl. cow, buffalo, sheep and goat traditional yogurts
- Animal(s): cow, buffalo, sheep, goat (all four confirmed in a 2024 study of traditional yogurts from several regions of Iran)
- Kind: fermented milk (yogurt)
- What it is: The Persian word for yogurt; the base of doogh, ash-e doogh, and mast-o khiar. Traditional yogurts differ by region in milk, back-slopping or spontaneous fermentation, whey removal and grains.
- How it is made: Traditional method: back-slopping or spontaneous lactic fermentation (the 2024 study does not give temperatures). Medieval Arabic sources describe a related method: milk left to sour in an earthenware jar from morning until midday, with rennet added and beaten, then left overnight.
- Where / who: Local producers in many regions; each city has its own producers serving a nearby market.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11320151/fullTextXML — traditional yogurts from cow, goat, buffalo and sheep milk, native producers in various regions; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogurt — Persian word mâst (ماست), its ancient spread to the Iranian plateau, and the medieval laban māst description.
- Unsure: Animal-specific yogurt names and methods are not given in the sources.

## Kashk (کشک; also qurut, keshk, tow, jortan, lech)
- Animal(s): cow, sheep, goat, or mixtures (per a 2026 study of 33 Isfahan kashk samples: 28 artisanal, 5 commercial)
- Kind: fermented dried dairy (dried yogurt/curd); liquid form also made industrially
- What it is: A sour, dried dairy product made from yogurt or doogh, eaten as a staple, especially in winter; used to thicken and flavour soups and stews (e.g. kalle joosh, kashk-e bademjan, ash reshteh). Also made as a liquid industrial product.
- How it is made: Traditional: doogh (buttermilk left after churning yogurt in a goatskin) is boiled, shaped into walnut-sized balls, and sun-dried; in some villages doogh is drained through cloth into a firm curd called lech, which is rolled and dried. Dry kashk can also be made by dehydrating skimmed yogurt. Industrial liquid kashk: milk pasteurised, fermented with starter, homogenised, concentrated, then heated with salt and whey powder. Milk-to-kashk ratio in one industrial setting is about 3:1.
- Where / who: Across Iran (the 2026 study says Iran is the likely origin); regional names: tow (Kurdish areas, Lorestan Lurs), jortan (Azerbaijan), lech and shiraz (Lak-speaking areas).
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashk — definition, methods, Iranian varieties and uses; https://fa.wikipedia.org/wiki/کشک — lech, jortan, tow names and rural shaping/drying; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC13653850/fullTextXML — 33 Isfahan samples, origin in Iran, sheep/goat/cow milk, traditional and industrial routes; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12302924/fullTextXML — industrial liquid kashk process, 3:1 ratio, kashk whey as by-product.
- Unsure: The Persian article gives no milk animal; the 2026 study names cow for all high-performing isolates, but the sources differ on which animal dominates.

## Sar-e-shir (سرشیر; also kaymak, nemshak)
- Animal(s): cow (default; the source does not restrict to one animal)
- Kind: milk fat, clotted cream
- What it is: A thick, rich cream (about 60% fat) skimmed from heated milk, close to clotted cream, served at breakfast and as a dessert.
- How it is made: Milk heated or gently simmered for 1–2 hours, cooled gradually over several hours, and the cream that forms on the surface is skimmed off. Result is firm, not homogenised, with a stronger flavour than cream.
- Where / who: Iran, Azerbaijan, Turkey and neighbouring countries; sar-e-shir from Ardabil and Sabalan honey entered Iran's national intangible heritage list in 2019 (1398).
- Sources: https://fa.wikipedia.org/wiki/سرشیر — definition, 60% fat, method, serving, origin, heritage listing.
- Unsure: The source treats Persian sar-e-shir as close to kaymak; the source also raises a health caution about fat content.

## Kare-ye mahalli (local butter, کره محلی — script not given)
- Animal(s): cow (default); sheep, goat or buffalo milk give a light yellow butter (per source)
- Kind: butter
- What it is: Butter sold in Iran; "local butter" in dairy shops and in most of Iran means yogurt-based butter, which is whiter and sour, unlike cream butter, which is yellow. Also mostly eaten at breakfast and sold in packaged blocks; electric churns have largely replaced older methods.
- How it is made: Traditional churning of yogurt. Goatskin bags (mashk) are hung from a wooden tripod (malar) and swung until butter separates from the buttermilk (mashk-zani). Mazandarani people churn yogurt with a long wooden stick (telem-zani). Industrial butter is made by churning cream.
- Where / who: Nomadic and tribal groups (goatskin churning), and the Mazandarani (stick churning); industrial butter across Iran. The buttermilk (doogh) from churning is then used for kashk, lor and siraj.
- Sources: https://fa.wikipedia.org/wiki/کره_(لبنیات) — the two churning methods, types of butter, local-butter meaning, buttermilk uses.
- Unsure: The article names a third method (dashun-zani) without describing it. Note "kare" is also used in Iran for margarine and vegetable fats.

## Human donor milk (Iran hospital milk banks; Persian name not given in the source)
- Animal(s): human
- Kind: donor breast milk, pasteurised by milk banks
- What it is: Donated human breast milk held by hospital milk banks for preterm and low-birth-weight infants. Iran had 16 active milk banks in university-affiliated hospitals by October 2025, which the source calls the regional leader in milk-bank development.
- How it is made: Per the source's definition, a milk bank selects donors, collects, screens, pasteurises, stores and distributes the donated milk (no temperatures or times given).
- Where / who: Milk banks in university hospitals, from Tabriz (2016) and Ahvaz (2018) through Tehran Valiasr (2019), Mashhad, Shiraz, Yazd, Kermanshah, Kerman, Isfahan, Zahedan, Urmia, Qom and others (2025).
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC13338493/fullTextXML — milk-bank definition, the list of 16 Iranian banks with university and year of opening, Iran as regional leader.
- Unsure: Donor numbers and volumes not extracted; the Europe PMC search snippet "Implementing a National Human Milk Bank Network in Iran" (DOI 10.1177/08903344261452888) was not opened.
