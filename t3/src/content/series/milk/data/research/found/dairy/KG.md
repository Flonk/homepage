## Kymyz / kumys / kumis (кымыз, qymyz)
- Animal(s): mare (main); Kyrgyz Wikipedia also names cow, camel, and sheep or goat milk
- Kind: fermented drink (low alcohol, lightly fizzy)
- What it is: Fermented mare's milk, central to Kyrgyz and Kazakh steppe drink culture. Atlas describes it as tangy, lightly fizzy and probiotic.
- How it is made: Raw, unpasteurised mare's milk is fermented for hours or days while stirred or churned. Traditionally it was fermented in horse-hide containers. Controlled modern production starts with about 2–5 h at about 27 °C, then a cooler ageing period. Alcohol is about 0.7–2.5%. Kyrgyz Wikipedia (Кымыз): alcohol 0.2–2.5%, up to 4.5% for true mare's kymyz; it is fermented in a leather bag (сабаа) with a starter (kor or urp, the dried residue from earlier batches), and is sour at 60–120 °T.
- Where / who: Served on summer high pastures (jayloo) as part of hospitality, sipped from small handleless bowls (piyala). Sold roadside in mountain areas, and "fresh kymyz is only available in the summer" (Wikipedia). Pastoral and home-made; industrial versions often use cow's milk with sugar or modified whey.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyrgyz_cuisine — Kyrgyz name, summer availability, roadside sale; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kymyz — production method, piyala bowls, alcohol content, cow-milk industrial versions; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kumis — fermentation timings and temperatures (general, not Kyrgyz-specific); https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12608619/fullTextXML — Kyrgyz name "Kymyz (Kir)"; https://ky.wikipedia.org/wiki/Кымыз — mare, cow, camel and sheep/goat milk; leather-bag fermentation with kor or urp starter; alcohol and acidity figures; "Kymyz" on the Kyrgyz cuisine page is served to every guest.
- Unsure: The 2–5 h at 27 °C figure is from a general industrial account, not a Kyrgyz source.

## Qurut / kurut (курут)
- Animal(s): sheep, goat, cow (sources differ; cow confirmed for Kyrgyz kurut by a 2024 abstract); mare and camel named in Kazakh context
- Kind: dried fermented milk product (dried curd balls)
- What it is: Dried, salted, fermented curd shaped into balls, strips or chunks and dried until hard. Eaten as a snack or seasoning and dissolved into dishes. The Kyrgyz wiki calls it a sheep's-milk product. The Kyrgyz diet paper lists "dried suzmo (kurut)" among milk products.
- How it is made: Traditional: full-fat yogurt is mixed with water and churned in a goatskin sack hung from a tripod. Butter separates, the buttermilk is boiled and drained to curd, then sun-dried for several weeks. Modern: sour yogurt is blended, boiled and strained, fermented in a warm oven for several days, drained and salted.
- Where / who: Pastoral Kyrgyz and Kazakh households (Kyrgyz wiki and Kazakh-context paper). Kurut is also eaten as a separate meal (2021 Kyrgyz paper abstract).
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qurut — traditional and modern methods, Kyrgyz name and photo of production in Kyrgyzstan, sheep's-milk note; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8780699/fullTextXML — "dried suzmo (kurut)" in Kyrgyz diet list; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC13066772/fullTextXML — kurut made from sheep, goat, cow, mare and camel milk in parts of Kazakhstan; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:34719146%20AND%20SRC:MED&format=json&resultType=core — 2021 Kyrgyz paper abstract: kurut is a universal fermented milk product used as seasoning and eaten as a meal; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:38230544%20AND%20SRC:MED&format=json&resultType=core — 2024 metagenomic abstract: kurut from fermented cow milk, 84 samples from seven Kyrgyz regions; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=kurut%20whey&format=json&resultType=lite — 2025 abstract (IND609062249): kurut sourced from a manufacturing plant in Kyrgyzstan, made into a drink with water or whey.
- Unsure: The 2024 metagenomic abstract names cow milk for Kyrgyz kurut; sheep and goat are not confirmed for Kyrgyz kurut. The 2021 Kyrgyz paper's full text is not open.

## Ayran (айран)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: yoghurt drink
- What it is: Cold, salty yoghurt drink made from yoghurt, water and salt, sometimes with mint or carbonation. Listed on the Kyrgyz cuisine page as a traditional drink.
- How it is made: Yoghurt is mixed with chilled or iced water and salt. Carbonated versions exist. Kyrgyz Wikipedia (Айран): warm milk is soured with an ayran starter, covered and wrapped for about half a day; ayran is the base for suzma and kurut; ayran in soup is called ak serke or ala döng, and ayran mixed with water is called chalap.
- Where / who: Common across Turkic Central Asia and beyond. Diet survey lists "ayran (sour milk)" among Kyrgyz milk products.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyrgyz_cuisine — ayran as a Kyrgyz drink, base for chalap and jarma; https://ky.wikipedia.org/wiki/Айран — Kyrgyz method (starter, warm milk, wrapped half a day), ayran as base of suzma and kurut, chalap when mixed with water; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayran — method and ingredients; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8780699/fullTextXML — "Cow milk, ayran, kefir…" in Kyrgyz diet list.
- Unsure: The Ayran wiki page does not mention Kyrgyzstan. The Kyrgyz-specific preparation is not described.

## Chalap / tan (чалап)
- Animal(s): cow (via ayran)
- Kind: fizzy yoghurt drink
- What it is: A fizzy drink made from ayran (also sold as "tan"). The Ayran wiki lists "chalap" as an alternative name, and a Kyrgyz-language link titled "Чалап" exists.
- How it is made: Made from ayran, which gives the fizz. Kyrgyz Wikipedia (Айран) says ayran mixed with water is called chalap; no further method given.
- Where / who: Kyrgyz cuisine (per the Kyrgyz wiki page).
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyrgyz_cuisine — chalap (also sold as tan) is a fizzy drink made from ayran; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayran — "chalap" as an alternative name and the Kyrgyz interlanguage title; https://ky.wikipedia.org/wiki/Айран — ayran mixed with water is chalap.
- Unsure: Whether "tan" and "chalap" are the same product in all regions is not confirmed.

## Jarma (жарма)
- Animal(s): cow (via ayran)
- Kind: cereal-based drink
- What it is: A drink made from ground cereals mixed with ayran. It is made fizzy by mixing, not by fermenting.
- How it is made: Ground cereal is added to ayran; the mixture is not fermented, just made fizzy. Second source (Kyrgyz Wikipedia, Кыргыз ашканасы): talkan (roasted cereal flour) is boiled to a thick porridge, then mixed with ayran, suzma or milk and drunk. The same page says maksym is the fermented form of jarma, which conflicts with the "not fermented" note above.
- Where / who: Kyrgyz cuisine (per the Kyrgyz wiki page).
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyrgyz_cuisine — jarma described as a ground-cereal drink mixed with ayran; https://ky.wikipedia.org/wiki/Кыргыз_ашканасы — jarma from talkan boiled and mixed with ayran, suzma or milk; maksym as fermented jarma.
- Unsure: Cereal type and proportions not given. The two sources disagree on whether jarma is fermented (en: not; ky: maksym is the fermented form).

## Suzma / suzmo (сузьма)
- Animal(s): cow (Kyrgyz source does not specify)
- Kind: strained yoghurt (concentrated yoghurt)
- What it is: Concentrated, drained yoghurt. The Kyrgyz diet paper lists "suzmo (concentrated yogurt)" and "concentrated ayran (suzmo)" among milk products.
- How it is made: In Central Asia, suzma is made by draining qatiq (local yoghurt); further drying gives qurut. Strained yoghurt wiki gives the general method.
- Where / who: Central Asian home cooking; the Strained yogurt article names Afghan, Tajik, Tatar and Uzbek cuisines, not Kyrgyz.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8780699/fullTextXML — "suzmo (concentrated yogurt)" in Kyrgyz diet list; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strained_yogurt — suzma method in Central Asia (via the suzma redirect).
- Unsure: Kyrgyz use is supported only by the diet paper, and the Wikipedia article does not name Kyrgyzstan.

## Kaymak / kajmak (кайымак)
- Animal(s): not stated for Kyrgyzstan (wiki: buffalo, cow, sheep or goat by region)
- Kind: cream skin / clotted cream
- What it is: Thick, creamy milk-fat product, about 60% fat, similar to clotted cream. The Kaymak wiki lists Kyrgyzstan among its countries and says the word comes from Turkic "kanak" (skin on milk).
- How it is made: Raw milk is heated slowly and simmered about two hours over very low heat; the cream that forms is skimmed, chilled and mildly fermented for hours or days.
- Where / who: Served with bread, honey or jam (Kyrgyz cuisine page). Diet paper lists "cream (kajmak, smetana)".
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyrgyz_cuisine — kaymak eaten with bread, jam or butter; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaymak — method, fat content, Kyrgyzstan among countries and Kyrgyz etymology; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8780699/fullTextXML — "kajmak" listed among Kyrgyz milk products.
- Unsure: Kyrgyz method and animal are not described. The Kyrgyz diet paper spells it "kajmak".

## Sary may (сары май)
- Animal(s): not stated (cow is typical)
- Kind: clarified butter / ghee
- What it is: Clarified butter, used like butter for dipping bread. Listed among Kyrgyz traditional foods.
- How it is made: Kyrgyz Wikipedia (Сары май): washed butter or cream is churned until the fat separates from the buttermilk (чөбөгө).
- Where / who: Home use and dipping bread. Diet paper lists "ghee (sary maj)". Kyrgyz Wikipedia says that at a guest's visit, when food is first tasted, one must touch the sary may with the mouth.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyrgyz_cuisine — "Sary may" as clarified butter alternative to butter; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8780699/fullTextXML — "ghee (sary maj)" in Kyrgyz diet list; https://ky.wikipedia.org/wiki/Сары_май — churning method, guest custom.
- Unsure: Animal milk not confirmed. The Kyrgyz article describes churned butter, not clarified butter, so the "clarified / ghee" label is unconfirmed.

## Butter (май)
- Animal(s): not stated (cow is typical)
- Kind: butter
- What it is: Butter eaten with bread, often with jam or kaymak.
- How it is made: Washed butter or cream is churned until the fat separates from the buttermilk (Kyrgyz Wikipedia, Сары май). Kurut-making also yields butter, which separates from buttermilk in the churn.
- Where / who: Common table butter and home butter.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyrgyz_cuisine — butter eaten with bread, jam or kaymak; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qurut — butter separated in the goatskin churn; https://ky.wikipedia.org/wiki/Сары_май — churned butter and buttermilk.
- Unsure: The local Kyrgyz word for butter is not confirmed (likely "май", as in sary may).

## Kefir (кефир)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: fermented milk drink
- What it is: Fermented milk drink listed among Kyrgyz dairy foods.
- How it is made: Kyrgyz Wikipedia (Кефир): milk (the article says "ынак же көк" milk; the animal is not translated) is pasteurised at 85–87 °C for 5–10 min or 90–92 °C for 2–3 min, cooled to 19–23 °C, inoculated with 3–5% kefir starter, then held at 8–10 °C for 1–3 days, giving mild, medium or strongly fermented kefir (acidity 80–120 °T). Dairy plants make kefir at 2.5%, 3.2% and 6% fat and a 0.5% fat version, sold in bottles or paper/polymer-coated packs.
- Where / who: Industrial and home-made; diet survey lists it.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8780699/fullTextXML — "ayran, kefir" in Kyrgyz diet milk-product list; https://ky.wikipedia.org/wiki/Кефир — method, starter, temperatures and dairy-plant fat levels.
- Unsure: Whether kefir in Kyrgyzstan is mainly industrial or home-made is not stated; the Kyrgyz article describes the factory process only.

## Chöbögö / tybyt (чөбөгө, тыбыт)
- Animal(s): cow (from butter; animal not stated)
- Kind: by-product of butter making
- What it is: Sediment that forms when butter is boiled; protein-rich. Sometimes honey or sugar is added. Some regions call it tybyt. Kyrgyz Wikipedia (Курут) says tybyt (chöbögö) is added to kurut.
- How it is made: Forms when butter is boiled; no further method given.
- Where / who: Kyrgyz household food.
- Sources: https://ky.wikipedia.org/wiki/Кыргыз_ашканасы — chöbögö as boiling-butter sediment, protein-rich, honey or sugar added, called tybyt in some regions; https://ky.wikipedia.org/wiki/Курут — "эжигей же тыбыт (чөбөгө)" added to kurut.
- Unsure: The first pass gave tybyt as a herb (kurut with herbs ezhigei, tybyt). The Kyrgyz sources use tybyt for this sediment, so the herb reading may be wrong or the name is shared. Not resolved.

- Where / who: Mainly Batken oblast.
- Sources: https://ky.wikipedia.org/wiki/Кыргыз_ашканасы — chülü as kurut and apricot jam, mainly in Batken.
- Unsure: No method; the source milk for the kurut is not confirmed.
