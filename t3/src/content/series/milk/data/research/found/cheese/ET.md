## Ayib (ayibe, ayeb, ayib; Amharic/Gurage name, Ethiopian cottage cheese)
- Animal(s): cow (sour milk from cow's milk in the sources read)
- Kind: fresh cottage cheese (acid-heat curd from churned, defatted sour milk)
- What it is: A traditional Ethiopian cottage cheese, mild and crumbly, eaten widely across ethnic groups and often served beside spicy dishes (kitfo, kocho) to soften their heat. Described as a staple and a cheap, affordable product.
- How it is made: Milk is left to sour (fermented). The sour milk is churned until the fat separates, and the fat is removed, leaving defatted sour milk (arrera, a buttermilk). Arrera is heated in a clay or iron pot to about 50 °C (one paper: 50–60 °C for 40–50 min) until a curd mass forms and floats over the whey. The pot is cooled so the curd sets, and the curd is separated from the whey through a fine cloth or sieve. Some producers leave the curd only drained, not pressed. Spices, salt and herbs may be added. The remaining liquid is whey (aguat).
- Texture / ripening: Soft and crumbly; eaten fresh, not ripened. Composition (one source): about 76–79 % moisture, about 14 % protein, about 2–7 % fat. Shelf life is about 2–3 days at ambient temperature unless treated.
- Where / who: Highlands and across the country; used by many ethnic groups. Made at home and sold in local markets. Industrial or pilot versions with starter cultures are reported in research. No protected status found.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11521749/fullTextXML — (fermented-milk review: churn, defatting, heating to about 50 °C, curd separated from whey, Ayib as cottage cheese); https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11566870/fullTextXML — (process steps and composition: 76 % moisture, 14 % protein, 7 % fat, 2 % ash; use in Ethiopia); https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11978027/fullTextXML — (preparation method with heating to 50 °C for 55 min and curd drained; sensory and shelf life); https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC13171718/fullTextXML — (churning, heating to about 50 °C, cloth filtering, composition, cultural use with kocho and kitfo); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_cuisine — (Ayibe: mild, crumbly, similar to feta, served beside spicy food); https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC10192822/fullTextXML — (farm and retail cottage cheese, Oromia, SNNP and Amhara: ergo fermented 2–3 days, churned, cream removed, buttermilk gently heated and whey drained, no rennet or coagulant; pH 3.63–3.80)
- Unsure: Sources differ on the fat content (7 % vs 1.8 %). The Wikipedia "Ethiopian cuisine" page calls the cheese "Ayibe" and compares it with feta; the papers do not. Some papers say the curd is "not pressed" (Wikipedia) while others describe it as drained only.

## Metata Ayib (spiced Ethiopian cottage cheese; "Meteta Ayib")
- Animal(s): cow (buttermilk-based, as the sources describe it)
- Kind: spiced fermented fresh cheese (ayib spiced and left to ferment)
- What it is: A spiced variant of ayib, made in the northwest of Ethiopia and a well-liked beverage there. It has a longer shelf life than ordinary ayib, according to one source.
- How it is made: Ayib is mixed with locally grown spices and left to ferment spontaneously. The spices named are rue (Amharic "tenadam") and basil ("zekakibe"), and other spices are used. The fermentation lasts about 15 days in one source and about 20 days in another. Lactic acid bacteria in the mixture give it probiotic and antimicrobial activity.
- Texture / ripening: Ripened by spontaneous fermentation; a year-long shelf life is claimed for it compared with ordinary ayib. Composition given in one paper: moisture about 42 %.
- Where / who: Northwest Ethiopia (Amhara region). Made at home and sold locally. No protected status found.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11566870/fullTextXML — (spiced cheese made in the northwest; about 15 days of spontaneous fermentation with spices); https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC13171718/fullTextXML — (spices with Amharic names rue and basil, about 20 days' fermentation, shelf life); https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11521749/fullTextXML — (probiotic lactic acid bacteria isolated from metata ayib)
- Unsure: The fermentation time differs between sources (15 vs 20 days). The full 2015 paper by Seifu on metata ayib was cited but could not be retrieved. The full-text Europe PMC request for PMC4554648 failed.

## Ititu (concentrated sour milk; "milk curd")
- Animal(s): cow in the Borana/Oromia sources; camel in the Afar and Somali-region sources
- Kind: concentrated fermented milk (sour-milk curd), not a fresh or pressed cheese; kind of cheese doubtful
- What it is: A traditional concentrated fermented milk made by pastoralists, mainly Borana (southern Ethiopia) and Kereyu (eastern Oromia). The sources sometimes call it "milk curd" and class it among the fermented milk products.
- How it is made: Milk is fermented at about 27–30 °C for 15–30 days. Whey is repeatedly drained off and fresh milk added every 3–4 days, until the product is concentrated. Shelf life is about 2–3 months at room temperature.
- Texture / ripening: Concentrated, sour and thick; fat about 3.3–3.7 %, protein about 3.3–3.6 %.
- Where / who: Borana and Kereyu pastoralists in southern and eastern Oromia; the Afar and Somali-region sources name camel versions. No protected status found.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11521749/fullTextXML — (ititu described as concentrated sour milk, Figure 3, fermentation and whey removal steps); https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11566870/fullTextXML — (ititu produced by Borana pastoralists; composition values)
- Unsure: Whether ititu is a cheese is doubtful. The sources treat it as a concentrated fermented milk rather than a curd cheese. Whether it is made from cow's or camel's milk varies by source.

## Camel cheese (experimental)
- Animal(s): camel
- Kind: soft cheese (experimental, not a traditional product)
- What it is: cheese made from camel milk. In Ethiopia it is "largely experimental"; no traditional camel cheese was found.
- How it is made: not described for Ethiopia. The review says camel milk has lower κ-casein and weaker coagulation, which complicates cheese-making; soft cheese from camel milk has been made under optimised lab conditions.
- Where / who: laboratory and trial production; no market found.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC13172142/fullTextXML — Ethiopia row: cheese production "largely experimental due to coagulation limitations"; coagulation and κ-casein differences discussed in the cheese section
- Unsure: only experimental use is documented for Ethiopia; no commercial product found.
