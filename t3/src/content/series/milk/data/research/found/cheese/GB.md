## Caerphilly (Caerffili)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: hard, crumbly, pressed cheese (almost white)
- What it is: A hard, crumbly, mild, almost white cheese from the Caerphilly area of Wales, with a slight sour tang.
- How it is made: Rennet added to buttermilk to form the curd. The curd is cut and lightly pressed into a cylindrical mould. The rounds are submerged in brine for 24 hours to form the rind. Draining is not described. The acid made during production dries the curd and gives the crumbly texture.
- Texture / ripening: Ripening is given as "as little as two weeks" in the text and "up to ten weeks" in the infobox.
- Where / who: Originates around Caerphilly, Mid Glamorgan, Wales. Most mass-produced Caerphilly is now made in Somerset and Wiltshire, England.
- Protected status: Traditional Welsh Caerphilly and Traditional Welsh Caerffili hold EU PGI status.
- Unsure: The text and infobox give different ripening times.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caerphilly_cheese — method, texture, origin, PGI status; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_cheeses — Caerphilly named as Welsh cheese in the Kalimpong entry

## Stilton
- Animal(s): cow (milk must be pasteurised)
- Kind: blue (Penicillium roqueforti) and white (Stilton is also made as a white, semi-soft version)
- What it is: An English strong blue cheese; white Stilton is milder and semi-soft.
- How it is made: Pasteurised local milk. The crust is pierced with stainless steel needles to let air in and make the blue veins. Manufacture and ripening take nine to twelve weeks. Coagulant, cutting and draining are not stated.
- Texture / ripening: Semi-soft and crumbly, creamier with age.
- Where / who: Derbyshire, Leicestershire and Nottinghamshire, England.
- Protected status: PDO, granted 1996 by the European Commission; the article says the protection remains after Brexit under EU and UK law.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stilton — milk, piercing, ripening time, region, PDO; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_British_cheeses — white and blue variants, Stilton as PDO

## Cheddar
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: hard, natural cheese (off-white; orange when annatto is added)
- What it is: A natural, relatively hard cheese, first made in the village of Cheddar, Somerset.
- How it is made: Rennet curdles the milk; the curd is heated, then "cheddared" (kneaded, salted, cut into cubes to drain whey, stacked and turned). Milling and pressing are not described in the article.
- Texture / ripening: 3 to 24 months, depending on variety; vintage cheddar 15 months or more at constant temperature. Some cheddar is matured in Cheddar Gorge and Wookey Hole caves.
- Where / who: Originates in Cheddar, Somerset, South West England. Major UK makers named: Wyke Farms, Pilgrims Choice, Cathedral City, Davidstow, Dorset Drum, West Country Farmhouse (PDO), Applewood.
- Protected status: West Country Farmhouse Cheddar is PDO (EU 2007, UK after Brexit). Orkney Scottish Island Cheddar is PGI (EU 2013; applying under UK law). Generic cheddar has no PDO.
- Unsure: Vegetarian and kosher versions use microbial or mould chymosin instead of animal rennet.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheddar_cheese — cheddaring, ripening, region, PDO/PGI status

## Lancashire
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: hard to crumbly cheese in three varieties: Creamy, Tasty, Crumbly
- What it is: An English cow's-milk cheese in three varieties.
- How it is made: Creamy and Tasty use the traditional method, standardised in the 1890s by Joseph Gornall (the Gornall method): curd from two or three days' curd of different maturity is blended. Creamy is matured 4 to 12 weeks, Tasty 12 weeks to 24 months. Crumbly is made from a single day's milk (a 1950s development) and matured 6 to 8 weeks. Rennet, cutting and draining are not described.
- Texture / ripening: Creamy is fluffy and creamy and not stringy when melted; Tasty is mature and nutty; Crumbly is crumbly, fresh and high in acid.
- Where / who: Lancashire, England. Crumbly is the only variety made outside the county.
- Protected status: Only Beacon Fell Traditional Lancashire Cheese is PDO; milk must come from north of the River Ribble and it must be made there by a designated method.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lancashire_cheese — varieties, Gornall method, PDO

## Cornish Yarg
- Animal(s): cow (pasteurised milk from Friesian cows, per the article)
- Kind: semi-hard cheese with an edible nettle-wrapped rind
- What it is: A Cornish cheese wrapped by hand in nettles.
- How it is made: Pressed and brined, then nettles are coated onto the cheese by hand. The article does not describe rennet or the curd steps. The nettle coating changes the acidity of the rind, which affects how the curd breaks down, giving creamy under the rind and crumbly in the centre.
- Where / who: Cornwall, England. Made by Lynher Dairies Cheese Company, Pengreep Farm, Ponsanooth, near Truro.
- Also: Wild Garlic Yarg (a garlic variant, per the Cornish list).
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornish_Yarg — method, producer, region; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Cornish_cheeses — Wild Garlic Yarg variant

## Wensleydale
- Animal(s): cow (ewe's milk was used originally; a little is still mixed in for an open texture)
- Kind: medium, supple, crumbly cheese; blue variant historically dominant, now rare
- What it is: A cheese from Wensleydale, North Yorkshire, with a slight honey aroma.
- How it is made: Coagulant, cutting, draining and pressing are not described. Ageing 3 to 6 months.
- Texture / ripening: Supple and crumbly; medium.
- Where / who: Wensleydale, North Yorkshire, England. Most is now made in large commercial creameries across the UK.
- Protected status: Yorkshire Wensleydale holds PGI (2013), which may only be used if made in Wensleydale.
- Variants: White (most common), blue (rare now), cranberry (widely sold), ginger, and a Wallace and Gromit branded version made under licence.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wensleydale_cheese — milk, texture, ageing, PGI, variants

## Red Leicester
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: hard English cheese, crumblier than Cheddar, coloured orange with annatto
- What it is: An English hard cheese similar to Cheddar but crumblier.
- How it is made: The article lists acidification, coagulation, cutting, cheddaring, pressing and ageing. Annatto is added during manufacture for colour. It can be made without annatto. Rennet is not mentioned.
- Texture / ripening: About 6 to 12 months (infobox) or 4 to 9 months (text); traditional wheels are firm and friable; block-pressed creamery cheese is moister and creamier. Industrial versions are vacuum packed; farmhouse makers often mature in cloth.
- Where / who: Leicestershire, England. Originally made from surplus milk after Stilton production. Renamed Red Leicester to distinguish it from wartime White Leicester (1940s).
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Leicester — steps, colour, history, texture

## Derby (Sage Derby)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: mild, semi-firm, buttery cheese; Sage Derby is herb-flavoured; Port Wine Derby is also common
- What it is: A mild, semi-firm British cheese from Derbyshire.

## Stinking Bishop
- Animal(s): cow (Old Gloucester cow milk, often blended with pasteurised milk from Friesian herds)
- Kind: washed-rind, semi-soft cheese
- What it is: An English washed-rind cheese.
- How it is made: Curds are moulded into 2 kg wheels. During maturing it is immersed in perry every four weeks. Salt is added only after the cheese leaves the mould. Ripens about four months.
- Texture: smooth and creamy, 48% fat; colour white-yellow to beige with an orange-to-grey rind.
- Where / who: Dymock, Gloucestershire. Made by Charles Martell and Son at Hunts Court Farm since 1972 (when only 68 Old Gloucester heifers remained). Fame grew after the 2005 film The Curse of the Were-Rabbit.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stinking_Bishop — method, producer, history

## Shropshire Blue
- Animal(s): cow (pasteurised)
- Kind: soft-to-semi-soft blue cheese, orange with annatto
- What it is: An orange-coloured English blue cheese, sharper and creamier than Stilton, with a tangy aroma.
- How it is made: Vegetable rennet. Annatto for colour. Penicillium roqueforti for veins. Made similarly to Stilton; matures 10 to 12 weeks. About 48% fat.
- Where / who: Name refers to Shropshire, but the article says no link to the county. Origin is disputed: a Shropshire company claims 1970s origin; a Shropshire cheese factor (Dennis Biggins, 1930s) is said to have added annatto. Revived in the 1970s at Castle Stuart Dairy, Inverness, under the name Inverness-shire Blue. Later made by Colston Bassett Dairies. Ludlow Blue, a Shropshire variant, uses carotene instead of annatto.
- Unsure: Origin and naming history are contested between sources.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shropshire_Blue — method, history, region

## Swaledale
- Animal(s): cow, sheep and goat per the lead; ewe and cow per the infobox; unpasteurised milk
- Kind: full-fat hard cheese
- What it is: A hard cheese from Richmond, Swaledale, North Yorkshire.
- How it is made: Milk heated to 28 °C with a microbiological culture; rennet added after two hours; curd heated to 28 °C, cut, stirred, cut again, drained and stacked; blocks broken up, put in muslin-lined moulds, lightly pressed at 28 °C for 18 hours, turned once after four hours, then brined in 85% brine for 24 hours.
- Texture / ripening: Matured in humid cellars 3 to 4 weeks; moist, medium firm. Without natural wax, a grey-blue mould forms on the rind.
- Protected status: Registered under the EU Protected Food Names Scheme; the article lists it under PDO products in the UK and EU.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swaledale_cheese — full method, ageing, PDO

## Crowdie
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: soft, fresh curd cheese (cream cheese style)
- What it is: a Scottish soft cheese made from curds; traditionally a crofters' cheese of the Highlands, the Islands and Orkney.
- How it is made: raw skimmed milk left to warm until lactic acid thickens it, then gently heated until curds form; the curds are hung in muslin to drain the whey, then mixed with salt and sometimes a little cream. Modern crowdie is usually made from pasteurised milk with lactic acid added to start souring. Black crowdie is mixed with double cream, rolled in oatmeal and crushed peppercorns. Cutting is not described.
- Where / who: Scotland. Commercial production began when Susannah Stone, near Tain in Ross-shire, gave surplus crowdie to a local grocer in 1962; the article cites Highland Fine Cheeses for this history.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crowdie — milk, method, black crowdie, Tain producer, region
- Unsure: the producer history is taken from the article, which cites a producer page.
