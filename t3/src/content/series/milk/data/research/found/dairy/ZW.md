## Amasi (amasi, mukaka wakakora in Shona context; "maas" in Afrikaans; other names: soured milk)
- Animal(s): Cow (unpasteurised cow milk in the traditional method)
- Kind: fermented/soured milk
- What it is: Thick soured milk with whey separated out, traditionally eaten with pap (sadza-type maize porridge) and also drunk. Its name is used in Ndebele; Zimbabwe is named as one country where it is now a popular snack.
- How it is made: Traditionally, unpasteurised cow milk ferments 1 to 5 days in a smoked calabash or hide sack; whey separates and the thick remainder is amasi. Commercially, milk is fortified with skim milk powder and gelatine, pasteurised at 72 °C for 15 s, cooled to 30 °C, inoculated with Lactococcus lactis, and incubated 16 to 24 h.
- Where / who: Southern Africa; Zulu and Xhosa in South Africa are the main described peoples; Zimbabwe mentioned as a country where it is a popular snack. Home and industrial.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amasi — traditional and commercial method, cow milk, Zimbabwe and Ndebele mentions; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fermented_milk_products — lists amasi under South Africa and "lacto" under Zimbabwe; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kwekwe — Dendairy (Kwekwe) makes "maas (fermented) milk" commercially; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC10678836/fullTextXML — table of African fermented foods lists Zimbabwe "Amasi, cow milk" with its microbes
- Unsure: The Wikipedia list gives "lacto" for Zimbabwe, and the amasi article treats amasi as a Zimbabwean snack. Whether "lacto" and amasi are the same product in Zimbabwe is not confirmed by these sources.
