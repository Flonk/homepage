## Butter tart (tarte au beurre)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: baked pastry with a butter-based filling
- What it is: a Canadian pastry, primarily eaten in English-speaking provinces; the filling is mainly butter, brown sugar, maple syrup and egg in a pastry shell, with raisins, walnuts or pecans often added.
- How it is made: baked until the filling is semi-solid with a crunchy top; no temperatures or times are given in the source.
- Where / who: Ontario and central Canada; home and bakery. Earliest published Canadian recipe is from a Barrie, Ontario cookbook of 1900.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butter_tart — ingredients, origin, method, popularity; Canada Post stamp 2019 mentioned.
- Unsure: the butter tart is a pastry; the dairy content is the butter in the filling.

## Pouding chômeur ("poor man's pudding")
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: baked dessert (cake batter with hot syrup)
- What it is: a Quebec dessert of cake batter over a hot syrup of maple, brown or white sugar; the cake rises through the syrup and forms a layer.
- How it is made: hot syrup poured over the batter before baking; no oven temperatures or times given.
- Where / who: Quebec, created in the early Great Depression; home and sugar shacks, served cold or warm, most popular during maple sugaring season.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pouding_ch%C3%B4meur — origin, method, serving; the infobox lists butter and cream among main ingredients.
- Unsure: the article's infobox lists cream and butter but the body does not mention milk. The page says the batter was sometimes stale bread in the worst Depression years (uncited).

## Sucre à la crème (sugar cream)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: confection (fudge-like sweet)
- What it is: a Quebec confection of cream, sugar and brown sugar; butter, maple syrup, nuts, vanilla or chocolate are sometimes added. Originally made with cream and maple sugar.
- How it is made: the mixture is cooked, cooled and then kneaded; crystal size sets whether it is grainy or soft. No cooking temperatures given.
- Where / who: Quebec; sold in grocery and convenience stores, cut into small cubes and served at room temperature; especially popular at the holidays.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sucre_%C3%A0_la_cr%C3%A8me — ingredients, method, serving, context.

## Moose milk (lait d'orignal)
- Animal(s): cow (dairy component)
- Kind: alcoholic mixed drink
- What it is: a traditional Canadian alcoholic drink of dairy and spirit: whole milk, cream, condensed milk, eggnog or vanilla ice cream, with Canadian whisky, vodka or dark rum, Kahlúa, maple syrup or sugar, nutmeg and sometimes cinnamon. Egg yolks are sometimes added to keep it from separating.
- How it is made: the article does not describe preparation.
- Where / who: Canadian Armed Forces; served at New Year's levée and at navy gatherings ("moosers"). Royal Canadian Navy, Air Force and Army each claim its origin; the term is first recorded 1915–1920.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moose_Milk_(cocktail) — ingredients, military use, origin claims.

## London fog
- Animal(s): cow (steamed milk)
- Kind: milk tea drink
- What it is: a hot tea drink of Earl Grey tea, steamed milk and vanilla syrup or honey or maple syrup; some versions add cream on top with cinnamon, nutmeg or lavender.
- How it is made: the article lists the ingredients only; no step-by-step given.
- Where / who: invented in Vancouver in the 1990s by Mary Loria at Buckwheat Cafe; spread through cafés. Home and café.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_fog_(beverage) — ingredients and Vancouver origin.

## Eggnog (lait de poule)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: drink (raw or cooked egg and milk drink, often alcoholic)
- What it is: milk or cream, sugar, raw eggs, a spirit and spices such as vanilla or nutmeg; commercial versions use gelatin and other thickeners with less egg and cream.
- How it is made: the article describes gentle heating without boiling until it thickens; aged alcoholic eggnog (up to a year) is said to destroy pathogens, with at least 20% alcohol and refrigeration recommended; no Canadian-specific times.
- Where / who: drunk across Canada and the US at Christmas; homemade and commercial in grocery stores. A Canadian brand (Neilson) labels its carton "Lait de poule".
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eggnog — ingredients, heating and safety, Neilson French label.
- Unsure: the article gives no Canadian production history or Canadian spirit.

## Evaporated milk
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: shelf-stable milk (concentrated, sterilised)
- What it is: milk with about 60% of its water removed. Canadian evaporated milk must have at least 25% milk solids and 7.5% milk fat; vitamin C and D may be added, and disodium phosphate or sodium citrate with an emulsifier.
- How it is made: evaporated, chilled, stabilised, sterilised at 116–118 °C (240–245 °F) for 15 minutes, and packaged. The article does not confirm Canadian plant conditions.
- Where / who: industrial; Canadian brands Carnation (owned by Nestlé, licensed to Smucker in Canada) and Dairy Isle (distributed by ADL).
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evaporated_milk — Canadian composition rules, brands, process.

## Condensed milk (sweetened)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: sweetened concentrated milk
- What it is: milk concentrated with sugar; the first Canadian condensery was built at Truro, Nova Scotia, in 1871. Brands named in the article (Eagle Brand, Carnation, La Lechera, Nestlé) are not tied to Canada in the text.
- How it is made: raw milk heated to 85–90 °C (185–194 °F) for several seconds before water is evaporated; the article does not say whether this applies to Canadian production.
- Where / who: industrial; also used in donair sauce and moose milk.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensed_milk — 1871 Truro condensery (citing a Quebec encyclopedia entry), heating step.

## Milk powder (powdered milk)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: dried milk
- What it is: powdered milk, which Indigenous Canadians commonly consume today, a habit linked to colonial-era rations. Canadian powdered milk must have added vitamin D, enough to give 300–400 IU per reasonable daily intake.
- How it is made: usually spray-dried from skim, whole milk, buttermilk or whey; concentrated to about 50% solids in an evaporator, then sprayed into a heated chamber. No spray-drying temperatures given.
- Where / who: industrial; household staple, and in Indigenous diets across Canada.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_cuisine — Indigenous consumption and ration history; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milk_powder — Canadian vitamin D rule, process.

## Ice cream (Canadian standard)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: frozen milk dessert
- What it is: under Canadian food rules, "ice cream" must have at least 10% milk fat and at least 180 g of solids per litre; products with added cocoa, chocolate syrup, fruit, nuts or confections may have 8% milk fat. "Ice cream mix" is the pasteurised unfrozen blend of cream, milk and other milk products, at least 36% solids.
- How it is made: the article gives no process steps for Canadian products.
- Where / who: industrial and home; the article gives no dates for Canadian ice cream history.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_cream — Canadian standards for ice cream and ice cream mix.

## Pasteurised donor human milk (Canadian milk banks)
- Animal(s): human
- Kind: donor breast milk, pasteurised
- What it is: donor milk from Canadian human milk banks, supplied to babies in hospitals and to families in some trials. The Rogers Hixon Ontario Human Milk Bank (Mount Sinai, Toronto) pools milk from donors and pasteurises it; NorthernStar Mothers Milk Bank (Calgary, Alberta) also supplies accredited donor milk.
- How it is made: Holder pasteurisation at 62.5 °C for 30 minutes, following Human Milk Banking Association of North America guidelines; pooled milk stored at −20 °C afterwards. A 2026 study notes that Bacillus cereus can survive Holder pasteurisation.
- Where / who: hospitals and milk banks in Ontario, Alberta and Saskatchewan (a 2026 feasibility trial in Saskatoon supplies milk from these two Canadian banks).
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC13583780/fullTextXML — CanDo RCT, Rogers Hixon bank, Holder method and storage; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC13599818/fullTextXML — Saskatchewan protocol, NorthernStar (Calgary) and Rogers Hixon supply; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12114557/fullTextXML — Bacillus cereus survival in a Canadian milk bank's donated milk.
