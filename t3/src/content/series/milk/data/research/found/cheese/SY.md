## Shanklish (شنكليش; also shankleesh, chanclich, sorke/sürke, surke, eddesh)
- Animal(s): Cow or sheep milk
- Kind: Aged, dried curd balls (mould-ripened according to one review)
- What it is: A Levantine cheese made from strained, fermented yogurt curd, shaped into balls and aged. Syria's coastal plain around Tartus is praised for it, and a Syrian shanklish set a Guinness record for heaviest piece (2008).
- How it is made: Yogurt is curdled, strained, and fermented; the mass is shaped into balls of about 6 cm, coated with za'atar, thyme, or Aleppo pepper (chilli in Syria), aged, and dried. Fresh balls are soft and mild; long-aged ones are hard and pungent.
- Texture / ripening: Soft and mild when fresh, hard and very pungent when aged. Syrian versions are often near-white with a clean, strong flavour.
- Where / who: Syria (coastal plain around Tartus, and Akkar in Lebanon), Lebanon, Levant. Not a protected name.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shanklish — method, shape, spice, Syrian coastal and chilli details; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_cuisine — listed as a Syrian cheese; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12428452/fullTextXML — described as mould-ripened, aged, and sun-dried (Lebanese review)
- Unsure: The Wikipedia article does not mention mould; the Lebanese review does. Syrian and Lebanese production is not separated in these sources.

## Akkawi (عكاوي; also akawi, akkawieh)
- Animal(s): Usually pasteurised cow's milk; goat or sheep milk also used
- Kind: Brined white cheese (semi-hard, not melting)
- What it is: A white brine cheese of the Levant, named after the port of Acre (Akka). Mildly salty, smooth, eaten as a table cheese, grated into dishes, or used as a base for other cheeses. Keeps up to about a year.
- How it is made: Milk heated to 35–40 °C; rennet added and curd left about an hour; whey drained through cloth; curds hand-packed into square hoops; cured two days in salted whey brine.
- Texture / ripening: Semi-hard, smooth, mildly salty; does not melt easily; little ripening beyond brining.
- Where / who: Originated in the Palestinian region; produced on a large scale in Syria, Israel, Palestine, Lebanon, Jordan, Egypt, Cyprus. No protected status found.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkawi — method, milk, texture, origin; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Levantine_cheeses — states large-scale production in Syria; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Syrian_cheeses — "produced on a large scale in Israel, Jordan, Syria and Palestine"; Europe PMC PMC12663395 — Akawi made in Lebanon and Syria (second pass); https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12428452/fullTextXML — Lebanese use as table cheese and base for Karishi
- Unsure: Syrian production is stated only as "large scale", with no Syrian-specific method.

## Halloumi (حلوم / جبنة حلومي; also hallum)
- Animal(s): Traditionally sheep and/or goat milk; commercial versions use cow milk too
- Kind: Semi-hard, brined, unripened (may be aged for weeks or months); grilled or fried
- What it is: A brined, pressed, scalded cheese listed in the Syrian cuisine article and the Syrian cheese category. Its origin is given as Cyprus, with the name traced to Arabic and Coptic.
- How it is made: Warm milk at 30–34 °C with rennet until it curdles (30–45 min); curd cut, reheated, stirred, moulded and pressed; pieces boiled in hot whey at least 30 minutes (scalding); salted and garnished with spearmint; held in salted whey 1–3 days; mature cheese kept in brine at least 40 days.
- Texture / ripening: Firm and squeaky; stays firm when heated; mature forms are grated into dishes.
- Where / who: Cyprus (PDO restricts the name to Cyprus, 51% sheep and goat milk). Syria is listed as a related cuisine and category, but no source says it originated there.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halloumi — method and origin; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_cuisine — listed as a Syrian cheese; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12428452/fullTextXML — PDO and sheep milk
- Unsure: Whether Syrian halloumi is made to the same method or is a local version is not stated.

## Jibneh mshallaleh (جبنة مشللة / جبنة مصلوقة; also tresse, braided cheese, mshallaleh)
- Animal(s): Cow's milk traditionally; sheep or goat versions exist
- Kind: Brined, semi-soft string cheese (braided)
- What it is: A Syrian string cheese, described as originating in Syria, eaten plain or in pastries, and a substitute for Oaxaca cheese.
- How it is made: Curd flavoured with mahleb, often with nigella, anise, or caraway; left several weeks in brine; then stretched and plaited into braids (stretching step not described in detail).
- Texture / ripening: Semi-soft, springy, mozzarella-like; nutty aroma.
- Where / who: Syria; also made in Canada (Quebec). Not a protected name.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tresse_cheese — origin, milk, mahleb, brine, braiding, texture; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Levantine_cheeses — described as originating in Syria, a mix of mozzarella, feta and halloumi; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Syrian_cheeses — Jibneh Mshallale "originating in Syria" (second pass); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_cuisine — "braided cheese that originated in Syria"
- Unsure: The braiding and stretching steps are described only briefly.

## Jibneh Arabieh (jibneh baida, "white cheese")
- Animal(s): Now mostly cow's milk; originally goat or sheep
- Kind: Fresh, salty, medium-firm, meltable white cheese (also eaten as table cheese)
- What it is: A Middle Eastern white cheese, salty and meltable, widely made in the region; Syrian cuisine article lists "jibne baida" as a white hard cheese with strong salt.
- How it is made: Mahleb spice is added; can be boiled before eating.
- Texture / ripening: Medium-firm, meltable; salty.
- Where / who: Found throughout the Middle East, with ties to the Galilee and Negev. Syria is not named as its origin in the sources read.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jibneh_Arabieh — milk, texture, mahleb, boiling, other name; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Levantine_cheeses — milk and origin; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_cuisine — "jibne baida" as a Syrian white hard cheese
- Unsure: The Jibneh Arabieh article does not name Syria; the Syrian link is only through the Syrian cuisine article. The article gives "jibneh baida" under the same entry.

## Nabulsi cheese (جبن نابلسي)
- Animal(s): Mainly sheep; goat is an alternative; cow or cow-sheep blend (called mashmouleh) also used
- Kind: Semi-hard, brined, dense cheese (melts easily)
- What it is: A semi-salty cheese named after Nablus in the West Bank. It is used in Syrian and Levantine sweets (nabulsiyyeh, knafeh) and eaten in Jordan.
- How it is made: Milk heated, rennet added; curds pressed, cooled, cut, and boiled, then stored in brine with mastic and mahleb; traditionally made in spring.
- Texture / ripening: Dense, semi-hard; melts easily.
- Where / who: Palestine (Nablus); also eaten in Syria as a category listed under "Syrian cheeses"
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabulsi_cheese — milk, method, origin, Jordan; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Levantine_cheeses — Nabulsi entry; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knafeh — Nabulsi as the filling for knafeh nabulseyeh
- Unsure: The Nabulsi article does not say it is made in Syria; the Syrian link is only a category tag and the Syrian cuisine dessert list. Included as an entry because it is in the Syrian cheese category and the Syrian cuisine dairy list.

## Baladi cheese (جبنة بلدي / جبنة بلدية, "village" or "country" cheese; also green cheese)
- Animal(s): Goat, cow, and/or sheep (sources disagree: goat in the Name section; goat, cow, and sheep in Ingredients; sheep and goat in the infobox)
- Kind: Fresh soft white cheese (unripened, uncultured)
- What it is: A soft white cheese of the Arab world, made by shepherds in Lebanon's mountains, sometimes called mountain cheese.
- How it is made: Rennet (powder or tablet), optional yogurt cultures; drained in a basket or hoop; submerged briefly in brine; sometimes shaped into balls under 10 cm.
- Texture / ripening: No ripening; soft, creamy, no rind, mild and rich; shelf life about 3 days.
- Where / who: Lebanon and Arab world; listed in the Syrian cheese category. No Syrian origin stated.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baladi_cheese — method, texture, names, Lebanese shepherds; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Levantine_cheeses — milk mix, shelf life; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11735057/fullTextXML — raw-milk baladi cheese in the Bekaa
- Unsure: Milk type disagrees across sources; Syrian production is not stated in the sources.

## Labneh (labaneh malboudeh for the dried version)
- Animal(s): Cow's milk in most sources; camel and other milk also used in the Middle East
- Kind: Strained yogurt, soft cheese-like (balls in oil or dried)
- What it is: A strained yogurt, tarter than cream, with a cheese-like consistency, served in mezze and sandwiches. Listed in the Syrian cuisine article.
- How it is made: Yogurt drained of its whey in a cloth until soft and cheese-like, then salted. In Lebanon and Palestine, balls are stored in olive oil, sometimes coated with za'atar, sumac, or chilli. Bedouin cooks press it in cloth and dry it to a hard version (labaneh malboudeh).
- Texture / ripening: Soft; dried version hard; olive-oil balls keep over a year and grow sourer.
- Where / who: Levant; Bedouin cooks make the dried version. The Wikipedia labneh article does not describe Syria specifically.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labneh — method, Bedouin dried version, oil-preserved balls; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_cuisine — labneh in the dairy list; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11735057/fullTextXML — labneh balls in olive oil among goat products
- Unsure: Strained yogurt; included because the Syrian cuisine article lists it as a dairy product. Not a cheese in the strict sense.
