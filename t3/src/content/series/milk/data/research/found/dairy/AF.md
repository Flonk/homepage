## Qurut / quroot (Pashto قروت/توروې; Persian کشک kašk; also "kurt" in the Meiji text)
- Animal(s): sheep or goat (Afghan Wikipedia and the diaspora-heritage source); the Qurut article does not name an animal for its general method
- Kind: dried fermented milk (sour dried curd); eaten as a dried balls or a rehydrated sauce
- What it is: a sun-dried sour yogurt or buttermilk curd, dissolved in water to make sauces. Afghan Wikipedia calls it a hard, very sour cottage-type product.
- How it is made: traditional method: full-fat yogurt is thinned with water and churned in a goatskin hung from a tripod until butter separates; the buttermilk is boiled, the curd drained, then sun-dried for weeks. Modern method: sour yogurt blended, boiled, strained, fermented several days in a warm oven, drained, salted. Meiji describes it from the solid layer that forms on heated doogh, salted, shaped into balls and sun-dried. No temperatures given.
- Where / who: eaten across Central Asia and the Middle East. Afghan main dish: kichree qurut (mung beans, rice, qurut dissolved in water; with aushak, mantu, kichri). Dried and stored for later use. Kuchi/Kochi nomads are listed in Wikipedia as producers of qurut for the national economy.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghan_cuisine — dairy list and quroot description (sheep/goat buttermilk); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qurut — method, Afghan dish, Persian/Pashto names; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashk — Afghanistan named for kichree qurut; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuchi_people — qurut and ghee as Kochi products for the national economy; https://www.meiji.co.jp/smartphone/yogurtlibrary/en/laboratory/yogurt/asia/ — kurt description
- Unsure: the Afghan Wikipedia article calls the product a hard, sour cottage cheese; the task excludes cheese, so this entry is on the dried-curd side. Keep it in the dairy list unless the cheese researcher claims it.

## Maast / mast / labanyat (Persian ماست)
- Animal(s): sheep (central Afghanistan), camel (northern and southern mountains), cow (rest of the country), per the Meiji source
- Kind: fermented milk, plain yogurt
- What it is: the basic fermented milk; the starter curd for doogh and many sauces.
- How it is made: milk is boiled, cooled and fermented with a starter curd (mast). Meiji adds that the mast is churned in copperware or a leather sack to remove butter.
- Where / who: staple across the country; household-made. Yogurt sauces with mantu, aushak and kaddo borwani; a yogurt-based component of Murgh Afghani chicken and several qormas.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghan_cuisine — maast (labanyat) listed, yogurt as core dairy; https://www.meiji.co.jp/smartphone/yogurtlibrary/en/laboratory/yogurt/asia/ — animal milk by region, starter curd and churning
- Unsure: the Wikipedia Afghan cuisine article gives no animal milk for maast; regional animal milk comes from Meiji only.

## Doogh / shomleh / shlombeh / shromba (Persian دوغ; Pashto شړومبه; Kabul "triwai")
- Animal(s): cow, sheep and camel by region (from maast-based production in Meiji); generic milk otherwise not stated in Wikipedia
- Kind: fermented milk drink (savoury; cold)
- What it is: a chilled, salted, savoury drink of yogurt and water, often flavoured with dried mint and sometimes cucumber or lime; sometimes carbonated. Served with bolani, picnic foods and meals.
- How it is made: Wikipedia (Doogh): milk and plain yogurt mixed at ~15:1, gently warmed, salted, fermented 3–4 days in a warm closed container. Ayran article (Afghanistan section): yogurt shaken in a waterskin (maskah) to separate butter; the remaining liquid is doogh. Meiji: mast churned to remove butter, leaving buttermilk, which is doogh and is also used in cooking.
- Where / who: drunk across Afghanistan; the Pashto name shromba/triwai is given in the Afghan sources.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doogh — method; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayran — Afghan names, maskah waterskin, mint and cucumber; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghan_cuisine — shomleh/shlombeh and chakida; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pashtun_cuisine — Kabul name triwai, yogurt and water shaken with dried mint and salt; https://www.meiji.co.jp/smartphone/yogurtlibrary/en/laboratory/yogurt/asia/ — churning of mast to get buttermilk
- Unsure: the Doogh Wikipedia article is Iranian-focused; the Afghan method comes from the Ayran and Meiji pages.

## Butter (Afghan sheep/goat butter; yak butter in the highlands)
- Animal(s): sheep or goat (quroot and Afghan cuisine sources); yak in the Pamir/Wakhan (Butter tea page)
- Kind: butter
- What it is: a common product, churned from yogurt or doogh; used in chicken dishes and in the butter tea of the highlands.
- How it is made: churned from full-fat yogurt in a goatskin or leather sack, or from doogh in a waterskin (see Qurut and Doogh entries).
- Where / who: nomadic and household production; butter is listed as an ingredient in the chicken recipes of Afghan cuisine (animal not stated there).
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghan_cuisine — butter in chicken dishes and quroot buttermilk; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qurut — goatskin churning; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayran — waterskin churning
- Unsure: no Dari or Pashto name verified for butter; the source text does not name a local word for it.

## Qaimak / qaymaq (clotted cream; Persian sarshir; Afghan "khame" in one paper)
- Animal(s): cow or buffalo (Afghan variant); sheep or goat in the general Kaymak article
- Kind: cream (clotted-style), breakfast cream
- What it is: a creamy layer skimmed from simmered milk; thinner in Afghanistan than the Turkish kaymak.
- How it is made: milk boiled slowly, simmered about two hours on very low heat, cream skimmed and chilled for several hours or days while it mildly ferments (general method in Kaymak article).
- Where / who: eaten at breakfast with bread, often with honey, sugar or jam. Pashtun cuisine: malai (butterfat) and milk creams at breakfast, eaten also with boiled eggs.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaymak — Afghan qaimak description and method; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pashtun_cuisine — malai and milk creams at breakfast; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12482145/fullTextXML — "khame" (cream) in the Afghan dairy inventory
- Unsure: the Pashtun article lists malai without describing its method; the Kaymak article supplies method.

## Sheer yakh (Afghan ice cream; a disappearing dish)
- Animal(s): milk (inferred from name; not stated)
- Kind: ice cream / frozen milk dessert
- What it is: Afghan ice cream ("a traditional ice cream from Afghanistan"), listed in the Afghan cuisine article and sold in summer ice-cream shops called shir yakh feroshi.
- How it is made: second pass (Aramco World): cream and sweetened condensed milk whipped in a food processor for 3–4 minutes to stiff peaks; cardamom, rosewater and crushed pistachios folded in; frozen at least 10 hours in an airtight container with plastic wrap pressed on the surface. Churning is not described. Rose-and-pistachio version (serves 8: 1 litre cream, 500 ml condensed milk).
- Where / who: the diaspora paper lists it as a dish lost among newer generations.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghan_cuisine — sheer yakh listed; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12482145/fullTextXML — shir yakh as a disappeared dish; https://www.aramcoworld.com/articles/2023/flavors-shir-yakheh-gulab-rose-and-pistachio-ice-cream — "Shir yakheh is a traditional ice cream from Afghanistan"; no-churn recipe
- Unsure: the Aramco method is one (no-churn, home) version; the traditional Afghan method is not confirmed.
