## Matsun (մածուն; matzoon, matsoni, matsun)
- Animal(s): Cow (most often), goat, sheep, buffalo, or a mix
- Kind: Fermented milk (yoghurt)
- What it is: The main dairy product of Armenian cuisine. Used on its own, in soups, sauces and cakes, and as the base for tan, butter and chortan. Armenia is named as its place of origin.
- How it is made: Boil the milk, cool it, and set it with a spoon of matsun from the last batch (a "culture from previous productions"). The Armenian Wikipedia article gives no temperatures or times. Typical starter bacteria are Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus and Streptococcus thermophilus, with Lactobacillus acidophilus in the original version. Traditionally it was strained into "kamats" matzoon in cloth bags, then stored for a month or more in leather sacks or clay pots, with fat poured over the top in summer to keep out air. Stored matsun of 5–6 days is said to be antibacterial.
- Where / who: Armenia and Georgia (Georgian "matsoni", with a protected geographical indication since 2012). Armenia applied for a GI for "Armenian matsun" in 2025 and lists the tradition on its intangible cultural heritage list. Home and village production; also industrial. Regional names noted but not described: kamats, brnats, serov.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matsoni — origin, starter cultures, traditional storage, GI and heritage notes; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_cuisine — "typical dairy items", uses in soups and sauces; https://hy.wikipedia.org/wiki/Մածուն — boiling, starter, storage in pots with fat, medicinal uses, churn for butter; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_dairy_products — "a fermented milk product of Armenian origin"
- Unsure: The Armenian and English articles give no temperatures or times, so the process above is general.

## Tan (թան)
- Animal(s): Cow or goat (Armenian Wikipedia); camel milk is used for the starter in Central Asia
- Kind: Fermented milk drink (yoghurt drink, lactic)
- What it is: A savoury, lightly salted yoghurt drink. Armenian origin, widespread in the Caucasus mountains and Central Asia. Sold still or carbonated; often flavoured with herbs such as parsley, mint, basil or sumac, and vegetables such as cucumber.
- How it is made: Add a starter (mer) to milk, then add a little clean water and salt. Basil or sumac can be added to taste. Industrial "tan" is often yoghurt cut with chilled water.
- Where / who: Armenia; made at home and by industry. Industrial production began in the 1990s; Armenian Wikipedia notes that many shop products labelled "tan" are diluted and far from the traditional taste.
- Sources: https://hy.wikipedia.org/wiki/Թան — origin, home method with starter, salt and herbs, industrial history; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_cuisine — tan as a matsun drink, still or carbonated, with parsley, mint and cucumber; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayran — names tan as an Armenian yoghurt drink alongside doogh and lassi (diluted with water, sometimes carbonated, mint-flavoured)
- Unsure: Armenian Wikipedia says the industrial "tan" can differ strongly from the original.

## Chortan (չորթան; chort'an, charathan)
- Animal(s): Not stated (made from tan or matsun)
- Kind: Dried dairy product (dried strained yoghurt or buttermilk curd)
- What it is: Dried balls kept as a winter food. Armenian name, in the Armenian dairy list and in the Wikipedia infobox. Called a form of kashk on English Wikipedia.
- How it is made: Matsun is churned to separate butter; the buttermilk (tan) is boiled and churned to a ricotta-like curd, or the tan is strained or evaporated to a thick paste. The clots are shaped into balls and dried in the sun for over a week. The dried balls are crushed in water to make tan again and used in tanapur, galachosh and tarkhana.
- Where / who: Armenia (Armenian origin); also in Central Asia and the Levant according to the infobox. Home production, historically.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chortan — matsun churned to butter, buttermilk boiled and churned, curd dried, the Sasna Tsrer epic mention; https://hy.wikipedia.org/wiki/Չորթան — tan thickened and dried into balls, crushed back into tan for soups, regions, other names; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashk — "Armenian" kashk forms
- Unsure: Is it a cheese? The English article puts it among kashk and the Armenian article puts it in the dairy category; the drying method suggests a dried dairy product, not a cheese, but no source states this directly.

## Kefir (կեֆիր)
- Animal(s): Cow, goat, sheep
- Kind: Fermented milk drink (kefir grains)
- What it is: A fermented milk drink. The word is of North Caucasian origin and it is very popular in the North Caucasus. Armenian Wikipedia gives the Armenian name կեֆիր ([ˈkɛfir]).
- How it is made: Kefir grains are added to milk, which is left to ferment. The English article gives no temperatures or times.
- Where / who: Listed in the Armenian cuisine dairy section. Industrial and home production; the English article does not name Armenia as its origin.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kefir — North Caucasian origin, Armenian name, milk types; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_cuisine — kefir listed as a fermented milk drink in the dairy section
- Unsure: Armenia is not named as an origin; only its presence in Armenian cuisine and its Armenian name are sourced.

## Karag (կարագ; butter)
- Animal(s): Not stated (churned from matsun or cream)
- Kind: Butter
- What it is: Traditional butter churned from matsun, plus butter used in cooking and as a flavouring. Armenian Wikipedia's article on butter is a general article.
- How it is made: Matsun is beaten in a churn (khnotsi) to separate butter, with the buttermilk left as tan. The Armenian Wikipedia article gives no churning temperatures.
- Where / who: Armenia, home and village production and industrial. Butter is used in khoritz filling, keshkegh, spas, kololak broth and krchik soup.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_cuisine — butter churned from matzoon; butter in khoritz, keshkegh, spas, kololak and krchik; https://hy.wikipedia.org/wiki/Մածուն — matsun beaten in a khnotsi churn to make butter; https://hy.wikipedia.org/wiki/Կարագ — general butter article and types (village butter fat content, Vologda, clarified butter)
- Unsure: Clarified or ghee-style butter is not documented for Armenia in the sources read; the Armenian butter article describes clarified butter in general only.

## Sar / seruts (սար, սերուցք; kaymak-type cream)
- Animal(s): Not stated; kaymak in general is made from buffalo, cow, sheep or goat milk
- Kind: Cream (clotted cream style)
- What it is: A thick cream skimmed off heated milk, eaten with honey, bread or fruit, and used in desserts such as gata, kadaif and baklava. Mainly called sar or seruts in Armenian; the Armenian name in the kaymak article is ser.
- How it is made: Raw milk is heated slowly and simmered for about two hours over very low heat. The cream that forms is skimmed off after the heat is turned off and left to chill for several hours or days, during which it mildly ferments.
- Where / who: Mainly a village product, popular in Western Armenian communities. Also called kaymak in Adjara, Georgia (kaimaghi), in the Georgian and Caucasian regional list.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaymak — method, fat content, Armenian names and Western Armenian use, Georgian kaimaghi; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_cuisine — sar and serots listed in the dairy section (no description)
- Unsure: The kaymak article gives a general method, not an Armenian recipe. "Sar", "serots", "rezhan" and "shor" appear only as names in the English list.

## Armenian grain kashk (dried yoghurt grain mix)
- Animal(s): Not stated (made from strained yoghurt)
- Kind: Dried fermented dairy product (kashk)
- What it is: Strained yoghurt mixed into grain, fermented, dried in the sun and rubbed to a coarse powder. The English Kashk article links this method to Armenian, Arab and Turkish cooking.
- How it is made: Mix strained yoghurt into grain, leave to ferment, sun-dry for more than a week, then rub to a coarse powder.
- Where / who: Armenia; home production.

## Tarkhana
- Animal(s): Not stated (made from matsun)
- Kind: Dried yoghurt-and-flour product (soup base)
- What it is: A mixture of yoghurt (matzoon) and flour or bulgur, dried and ground to a powder used to thicken soups. Also called a flour and matzoon soup. Found on the English Armenian cuisine page.
- How it is made: Matzoon is mixed with eggs and flour or bulgur, dried and ground to a powder.
- Where / who: Armenia, home production.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matsoni — matzoon mixed with eggs and flour to make tarhana; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_cuisine — tarhana made from yoghurt and bulgur and used to thicken soups

## Spas
- Animal(s): Matsun (cow, goat or sheep, unspecified)
- Kind: Yoghurt-based soup (dish where matsun is the main ingredient)
- What it is: A traditional Armenian soup built on matsun, served warm in winter and chilled in summer. Matsun is the only core dairy ingredient; butter is added as well.
- How it is made: Hulled wheat berries and herbs (coriander, thyme, parsley, sometimes mint) are cooked; flour and an egg or egg yolk stop the matsun curdling; butter and onions or fried onions are added for richness. Spinach, meatballs, rice, bulgur or barley may also go in.
- Where / who: Armenia, home cooking.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spas_(soup) — ingredients, matsun as core dairy, flour and egg to stop curdling, serving temperatures; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_cuisine — spas as a matsun soup
- Unsure: None
