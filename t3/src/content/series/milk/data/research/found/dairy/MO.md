## Ginger milk curd (姜撞奶; Macanese "ginger milk")
- Animal(s): cow milk per the article; water buffalo milk is stated as traditional, but the article tags that claim as needing a citation
- Kind: milk dessert (set curd)
- What it is: a warm, set milk pudding flavoured with ginger juice; sold in Macau and Hong Kong and in Guangdong.
- How it is made: milk is heated and ginger juice is added while the milk is at a suitable temperature; the mixture is left still and sets almost at once. The ginger enzyme zingibain does the curdling, works best at 40–60 °C and clots most strongly at about 70 °C, and clotting falls off sharply at 75 °C and above. Traditional technique pours quickly from a height ("clash"). No set times or quantities given.
- Where / who: origin Shawan, Panyu, Guangzhou (Guangdong) per the article; listed under Macanese desserts. Macau-style version said to resemble the Hong Kong one.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ginger_milk_curd — method, enzyme temperatures, Guangdong origin, Macanese dessert listing; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macanese_cuisine — Macau ginger milk curd listed, with a caption saying the Macau version resembles the Hong Kong one.
- Unsure: origin is Guangdong; the article lists it among Macanese desserts, but it is not clear the Macau version is made locally from local milk.

## Hong Kong-style milk tea (silk-stocking milk tea; 港式奶茶)
- Animal(s): cow milk (evaporated milk)
- Kind: milk tea / drink
- What it is: strong black-tea blend mixed with evaporated milk, served hot or iced. Common in Hong Kong and in Macau's cha chaan teng diners.
- How it is made: a black-tea blend (Ceylon-based) is steeped in a cotton "silk stocking" bag with water at 96–98 °C poured from about 60 cm, left 12 minutes; once the leaves are half afloat the tea is "pulled" (poured back and forth through the bag) four times; kept at about 94 °C for up to an hour; about 30% evaporated milk is added before serving. Sugar is left to the customer in the standard version.
- Where / who: Hong Kong origin; cha chaan teng diners across Macau and Hong Kong and parts of Guangdong. Industrial/home versions use the same evaporated milk.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong-style_milk_tea — ingredients, steeping and pulling method, evaporated milk ratio; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cha_chaan_teng — milk tea and evaporated milk served in cha chaan teng, and the lead saying cha chaan tengs are commonly found in Hong Kong, Macau and parts of Guangdong.
- Unsure: the Hong Kong milk tea article does not name Macau sellers; the Macau presence is from the cha chaan teng article only.

## Cha jau (condensed-milk milk tea; 鴛鴦-style sweet milk tea)
- Animal(s): cow milk (sweetened condensed milk)
- Kind: milk tea / sweet drink
- What it is: a sweeter form of Hong Kong milk tea where sweetened condensed milk replaces the evaporated milk.
- How it is made: as Hong Kong-style milk tea, but condensed milk is used in place of evaporated milk to supply the sweetness (no sugar added). See the milk tea entry for the tea steps.
- Where / who: Hong Kong; cha chaan teng diners.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong-style_milk_tea — "cha jau" as condensed-milk variant; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cha_chaan_teng — condensed milk replacing evaporated milk in cha zau.

## Yuenyeung (鴛鴦; coffee and milk tea mix)
- Animal(s): cow milk (evaporated or condensed milk in the milk tea component)
- Kind: milk drink (mixed coffee and tea)
- What it is: a mix of brewed coffee and Hong Kong-style milk tea, served hot or iced.
- How it is made: the coffee and milk tea are mixed, commonly in a 3:7 ratio (coffee to milk tea) according to the Hong Kong Leisure and Cultural Services Department. Method varies by vendor.
- Where / who: Hong Kong origin; Starbucks stores in Hong Kong and Macau promoted a frappuccino version in 2010 (industrial, chain).
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuenyeung — ingredients, 3:7 ratio, Starbucks Macau promotion in 2010; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cha_chaan_teng — yuenyeung as a Hong Kong drink with milk tea.
- Unsure: the Starbucks version is a chain product; Macau-made traditional yuenyeung is not confirmed.

## Macanese egg tart (Macau-style pastel de nata; 葡撻)
- Animal(s): cow milk and butter (per infobox)
- Kind: milk-based custard pastry / dessert
- What it is: a Portuguese-derived custard tart with a caramelised, browned top, distinct from the Hong Kong style. Served fresh from the oven with cinnamon and icing sugar.
- How it is made: the page gives no method or temperatures; the custard is made from milk and butter (no cream named), with egg yolks as the main ingredient.
- Where / who: Macau; origin story: Lord Stow's Bakery in Coloane (1989) by Andrew Stow and Margaret Wong, adapting the 19th-century Portuguese pastel de nata; recipe sold to KFC in 1999, which spread the Macau variant to other countries.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egg_tart — Macau version, milk and butter in the custard, Lord Stow's Bakery history, KFC spread; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastel_de_nata — Macau variant, egg-yolk base, Macanese dessert listing.
- Unsure: dairy content is an infobox summary, not a recipe; this is a baked item, listed here because milk is a main ingredient.

## Sweetened condensed milk and evaporated milk (as used in local tea culture)
- Animal(s): cow milk
- Kind: heat-treated, concentrated milk (condensed or evaporated)
- What it is: concentrated milk products used in the milk teas, coffee and desserts above; widely used in Hong Kong tea culture.
- How it is made: condensed milk is clarified and adjusted to a fat-to-solids ratio, heated to 85–90 °C for several seconds, water is evaporated, sugar added to a 9:11 sugar-to-evaporated-milk ratio, then cooled to trigger lactose crystallisation.
- Where / who: industrial; Carnation and Eagle brands were introduced in Hong Kong in the early 1900s and held large market shares there; Macau-specific use is not mentioned on the source page.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweetened_condensed_milk — method, condensed milk as an integral element in Hong Kong tea culture, Carnation and Eagle in Hong Kong.
- Unsure: this is a generic product entry; Macau consumption is inferred from Hong Kong and cha chaan teng sources, not a Macau source.

## Serradura (layered cream and biscuit dessert; Macanese)
- Animal(s): cow milk and cream (condensed milk and whipping cream)
- Kind: chilled milk dessert
- What it is: a Portuguese-origin dessert that became famous in Macau, when Macau was under Portuguese colonial rule.
- How it is made: whipped cream and condensed milk are layered with crushed Marie biscuit in a container and chilled for about five to six hours before serving; thick cream sometimes replaces whipping cream, and vanilla or green tea powder can be whisked into the cream.
- Where / who: Macau; listed under both Portuguese and Macanese cuisine.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serradura — ingredients (condensed milk, Marie biscuit, whipping cream), method and chilling time, Portuguese origin and Macau fame.
- Unsure: the article does not name a Macau producer; it describes a common recipe, not local dairy production.
