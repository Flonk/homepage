## Strained Greek yogurt (giaourti; strangistó giaoúrti)
- Animal(s): cow; sheep (traditional in Greece, per Wikipedia)
- Kind: fermented milk / strained (thick) yogurt
- What it is: yogurt thickened by draining off most of its whey, keeping its sour taste. Greek cuisine eats it with honey, often with walnuts, and it is the base of tzatziki.
- How it is made: drained in cloth until soft (traditional; the Albanian method is a few hours to overnight). Commercial versions use ultrafiltration or centrifuge/membrane filtration instead. No temperatures or times found for Greek production.
- Where / who: all of Greece; industrial and traditional. Wikipedia says Fage began straining cow's-milk yogurt in Greece in 1975.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strained_yogurt — how it is made, Greek name, sheep and cow milk; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_cuisine — served with honey and walnuts, base of tzatziki; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_dairy_products — "Greek yogurt" is strained yogurt with an ancient precursor called oxygala (sour milk)
- Unsure: the "Greek" label is a legal term in the UK (Fage v Chobani ruling); Greece may seek PDO status. Wikipedia says Greek yogurt is traditionally sheep's milk but gives no inline citation for that.

## Greek sheep's-milk yogurt (strangistó giaoúrti protó proéleusis; ewe's milk yogurt)
- Animal(s): sheep (ewe's milk)
- Kind: fermented milk / yogurt
- What it is: traditional Greek yogurt made from ewe's milk. A 2024 study developed a probiotic version with Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus added to starter cultures S. thermophilus and L. bulgaricus; the paper describes the traditional product as the base.
- How it is made: pasteurised milk fermented with S. thermophilus and L. bulgaricus (control); the probiotic version adds L. rhamnosus at the start of fermentation. Stored at 4 °C for 25 days in the study. Full manufacturing steps not extracted.
- Where / who: Lesvos (the study's dairy partner is in Mantamados, Lesvos, and the authors are in the University of the Aegean, Lemnos).
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC10835171/fullTextXML — Greek sheep yogurt, starter cultures, storage at 4 °C, Lesvos dairy partner; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strained_yogurt — traditional sheep's yogurt in Greece
- Unsure: a research prototype, not a market product, per the paper. Cultures and storage are from the study, not from a traditional recipe.

## Bougatsa krema (semolina custard pastry)
- Animal(s): not stated (milk-based custard)
- Kind: sweet / custard pastry
- What it is: the cream version of bougatsa, a pastry of filo with a semolina custard. It is the most popular version and is sweet.
- How it is made: semolina custard placed in a dish with about five sheets of buttered phyllo, baked about 30–35 minutes until golden; cut into pieces, served hot, dusted with icing sugar and/or cinnamon. Oven temperature and custard milk quantities are not given.
- Where / who: northern Greece origin (Serres, Thessaloniki); sold in bougatsadika and bougatsopolia. Regional styles: Veria (very sweet), Thessaloniki (crunchy), Chania (with mizithra and sugar), Athens (creamy).
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bougatsa — custard filling and baking time, origin and regional styles
- Unsure: the milk-based custard is the source's "semolina custard"; milk content is not measured.

## Galaktoboureko (γαλακτομπούρεκο)
- Animal(s): not stated (milk-based custard)
- Kind: sweet / custard pastry
- What it is: a custard baked between filo sheets, served plain or with syrup. Its origin is Byzantine-era western Asia Minor (Smyrna), per tradition.
- How it is made: semolina custard (or muhallebi, a milk pudding flavoured with lemon, orange or rose) baked with about 20 filo layers above and below; cut into squares or 10 cm portions; coated with a clear, sweet syrup. No temperatures or times given.
- Where / who: popular across Greece; Athens and Thessaloniki versions noted; Cypriot "galatopourekko"; Laz form in the Turkish Black Sea region uses cornmeal.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaktoboureko — custard, filo, syrup, origin, regional names
- Unsure: the custard's milk content is stated only as "milk-based"; no quantities.

## Kefir (Greek commercial kefir)
- Animal(s): not stated in the paper; cow's milk likely in commercial products (not confirmed)
- Kind: fermented milk / drink
- What it is: commercial kefir sold in Greece. A 2026 study sampled 32 kefir products from eight regions (Koufalia, Kilkis, Drama, Thrace, Katerini, Larissa, Volos, Attica) and found the bacteria are mostly Bacillota, with Streptococcus thermophilus and Lactococcus cremoris dominant.
- How it is made: not extracted. The paper concerns the microbiota of commercial products, not the process.
- Where / who: industrial; eight regions of Greece.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC13683812/fullTextXML — commercial Greek kefir samples, regions, dominant bacteria ; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7416117/fullTextXML — Greek kefir grains from an artisanal producer in Athens and one in Crete; the grains were propagated in 1.5% UHT milk at 25 °C for 24 h in the lab (a study method, not a traditional one) (second pass)
- Unsure: a 2020 Greek kefir grains study (see leads) would describe traditional grains; not opened.

## Trahanas (trahana, tarhana; xynohondros)
- Animal(s): milk type not stated; yogurt or sour milk used
- Kind: dried fermented dairy-grain product
- What it is: a dried, fermented food of cracked wheat or couscous-like paste mixed with fermented milk or yogurt. Common in Greece and Cyprus (where it is often served with halloumi), and in Albania.
- How it is made: flour or grain mixed with yogurt or sour milk (optionally cooked vegetables), left to ferment, then dried, ground and sieved. Fermentation brings pH to 3.4–4.2; drying to 6–10% moisture. No fermentation or drying times or temperatures given.
- Where / who: Greece, Cyprus, Albania; home, traditional.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trahana — ingredients, method, pH and moisture, Greek names
- Unsure: the Wikipedia article does not class it as a dairy product; it is included because it is made with fermented milk.

## Kefir grains (Greek, Athens and Crete)
- Animal(s): not stated for the Greek producers; the study propagated them in low-fat (1.5%) UHT milk
- Kind: kefir grains (starter culture)
- What it is: the grains used by two artisanal kefir producers, one in Athens and one in Crete. Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens made up about 95–96% of the bacteria in both.
- How it is made: the study propagated the grains in low-fat UHT milk, incubated at 25 °C for 24 hours, five cycles. The traditional household method is not described.
- Where / who: Athens and Crete; artisanal.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7416117/fullTextXML — grain origins, bacteria, lab propagation method (second pass)
- Unsure: the paper is cited as a 2020 study in the leads list; the year was not checked in the text read.

## Skopelos goat's milk
- Animal(s): goat (Skopelos breed)
- Kind: raw milk (dairy goat)
- What it is: milk from the Skopelos, an indigenous Greek dairy goat breed adapted to dry, hot climates. In Greece it is used mainly for traditional cheeses.
- How it is made: not described. The study compared milk from an intensive farm in Attica and an extensive grazing farm on Skopelos and tested how the milk coagulates with rennet at 35 °C; this is lab measurement, not cheesemaking.
- Where / who: Skopelos island (grazing farm); Attica (intensive farm).
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12027251/fullTextXML — Skopelos breed, its use in traditional cheeses, the two farm systems, milk and rennet measurements (second pass)
- Unsure: no cheese made from this milk is named in the paper.
