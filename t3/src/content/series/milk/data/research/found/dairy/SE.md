## Filmjölk (fil; surmjölk in Central Sweden; kulturmelk/surmelk in Norway; skyr on Gotland formerly; filbryta/filbruttu in parts of Norrland)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: fermented milk (mesophilic, soured)
- What it is: Traditional Swedish soured milk, common across the Nordic countries, thickened by lactic acid bacteria. Sold industrially since 1931 (Arla); now mostly unflavoured, flavoured and probiotic versions.
- How it is made: Pasteurised milk is inoculated with a small amount of an active batch ("fil" culture; Arla's contains several Lactococcus lactis subspecies and a Leuconostoc strain) and left one to two days at room temperature or in a cool cellar. Temperatures and times are not given in the sources.
- Where / who: Nationwide in Sweden; industrial (Arla) and traditional. Written attestations of filmjölk and fil date from the 1700s.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filmjölk — what it is, culture, method, variants, history; https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filmjölk — sour culture genera, regional names (surmjölk, filbryta, skyr on Gotland)
- Unsure: Fat grades and variant list are from the English article; Swedish article gives no fermentation temperatures.

## Långfil (långmjölk)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: fermented milk (ropy/stringy variant of filmjölk)
- What it is: A very thick, stringy-textured soured milk, historically popular in Norrland and more common in northern Sweden; introduced by Arla in 1965.
- How it is made: Made with Lactococcus lactis subsp. lactis var. longi (or ssp. cremoris in the Swedish article), which forms long polysaccharides giving the stringy texture. No temperatures or times given.
- Where / who: Northern Sweden (Norrland); Norrmejerier still produces it.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filmjölk — långfil variant and 1965 introduction; https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filmjölk — long-polysaccharide texture, Norrland popularity, Norrmejerier production
- Unsure: The two articles name the culture strain differently (var. longi vs. ssp. cremoris).

## Tätmjölk
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: fermented milk (traditional, enzyme-inoculated)
- What it is: A traditional soured milk variant in which the container is rubbed with sundew or butterwort leaves, which carry protein-degrading enzymes, to thicken the milk.
- How it is made: The container is rubbed with sundew (Drosera) or butterwort (Pinguicula) leaves before the milk is put in. No temperatures or times given.
- Where / who: Traditional Swedish variant of filmjölk; described as a historic practice.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filmjölk — tätmjölk in the variants table with enzyme description
- Unsure: Only one source (the English article, in its variants table); not verified in Swedish sources.

## Filbunke (fil in Finland-Swedish and some Swedish dialects)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: fermented milk dish (pudding-like, eaten set)
- What it is: A breakfast dish of soured milk with a pudding-like texture, served chilled, plain or with fruit and berries; known in Sweden since at least the 1600s.
- How it is made: Traditionally raw, unskimmed milk left to sour on its own, with a cream layer forming on top. Modern: pasteurised milk heated almost to boiling, cooled to room temperature, stirred with a starter (filmjölk or a previous batch), and left overnight in a cool place. Sprinkled with sugar, ginger or cinnamon and crumbled crispbread in older practice.
- Where / who: Sweden (and Finland-Swedish areas); home-made and dairy.
- Sources: https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filbunke — method (traditional and modern), consistency, 1600s history, regional names, Finnish viili equivalent
- Unsure: None

## Viili
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: fermented milk (ropy, with surface fungus)
- What it is: A fermented milk with a ropy, "long" texture when left unstirred and a velvet-like surface from a fungus. Listed as Nordic in origin; most common in Finland and Sweden. The fermented-milk comparison lists it as originally from Sweden and as a Finnish specialty.
- How it is made: Modern viili is made from pasteurised milk with laboratory-grown cultures at a dairy plant, sold fresh. Cultures: Lactococcus lactis (incl. ssp. cremoris for the exopolysaccharide viilian), Leuconostoc mesenteroides, and traditionally the yeasts Kluyveromyces marxianus and Pichia fermentans; Geotrichum candidum grows on the surface.
- Where / who: Finland (mainly) and Sweden; industrial (natural and flavoured versions).
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viili — origin, cultures, surface fungus, Swedish names; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fermented_milk_products — viili listed as originally from Sweden, Finnish specialty
- Unsure: Origin is disputed in the sources (Nordic, originally Swedish per the list, now Finnish specialty). Included because viili is widely sold in Sweden and the Swedish article names filbunke as its closest Swedish equivalent.

## Gräddfil (sour cream)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: soured cream
- What it is: A Swedish soured cream of about 12% fat, homogenised, pasteurised and soured with a culture. Introduced in Sweden in the early 1950s, based on Central and Eastern European smetana.
- How it is made: Homogenised, pasteurised cream soured with a culture. No culture, temperature or time given.
- Where / who: Nationwide; industrial. Served with matjes herring and new potatoes at midsummer, then year-round from the 1970s; in Christmas buffets, cold sauces, dressings and dips.
- Sources: https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gräddfil — fat content, production, history, uses
- Unsure: None

## Kärnmjölk (buttermilk)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: drink / by-product of butter (buttermilk)
- What it is: The liquid left after churning butter from cream; under 1% fat, slightly sour and somewhat thick, thinner than filmjölk. Rich in phospholipids, so used as an emulsifier in cooking and baking.
- How it is made: Formed as the by-product when fat is separated from cream during butter churning.
- Where / who: Sweden: the last major Swedish dairy selling it to private buyers, Norrmejerier, stopped in the early 2000s; Wapnö still produced it (2024) for shops. Modern buttermilk has its culture added after churning, so it differs from the traditional product. Still sold in Denmark (kærnemælk) and Finland (kirnupiimä).
- Sources: https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kärnmjölk — definition, formation, Swedish availability, Danish/Finnish products
- Unsure: Sweden-availability statements come from a Swedish article current to 2024/2025; check before use as current.

## Kalvdans (calf dance, colostrum pudding)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: milk dessert / colostrum dish
- What it is: A traditional Scandinavian dessert made from colostrum, the first milk a cow gives after calving. It sets to a jiggly, pudding-like texture, hence its name.
- How it is made: Unpasteurised colostrum is mixed with water and heated carefully; the high protein content coagulates and hardens, similar to eggs. No temperatures or times given.
- Where / who: Sweden (long tradition, mentioned in an 1682 encyclopedia); unpasteurised milk can only be sold direct from farms, so it is rarely made in households today. Listed in the Slow Food Ark of Taste (2008). Similar dishes: ábrystir (Iceland), uunijuusto (Finland).
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalvdans — definition, method, Swedish tradition, 1682 mention, Ark of Taste; https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mjölk — kalvdans made from råmjölk (colostrum)
- Unsure: None

## Messmör (whey spread)
- Animal(s): cow (whey)
- Kind: whey product / sweet spread
- What it is: A soft, sweet, brown spread sold in tubes across the Nordic countries; a whey-based product closely related to Norwegian prim and distinct from brunost.
- How it is made: Whey is boiled down, without added milk or cream, for a shorter time than brunost (roughly a 10:1 reduction). No temperatures or times given.
- Where / who: Sweden and Norway; industrial. Such spreads are said to be common in Scandinavia for at least 2,500 years.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messmör — definition, method, origin; https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mjölk — messmör listed among Swedish dairy products
- Unsure: The English article's claim of 2,500 years is about such spreads in general, not messmör itself.

## Smör (butter)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: butter
- What it is: Swedish butter, a dairy fat made by churning cream; classed as salted, unsalted or clarified. Historically a cash item and tax payment; margarine has taken market share since the late 1800s.
- How it is made: Pasteurised cream is temperature-treated so the fat crystallises in the beta-prime form, then churned (modern: continuous butter machine), salt and culture added during working. Modern butter is often cultured after manufacture (IBS); earlier, butter was churned from soured cream. Buttermilk is the by-product.
- Where / who: Nationwide; industrial and traditional.
- Sources: https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smör — manufacture, salted/unsalted, history, sayings
- Unsure: The sv article does not confirm regional butter-export traditions (Bohuslän, Skåne, smörlandet), so they are not listed.

## Vispgrädde (whipping cream)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: cream
- What it is: A high-fat cream for whipping into stable foam, used for garnish, fillings and desserts (buns, cakes, waffles, pancakes).
- How it is made: Fat droplets bind air during whipping. Swedish whipping cream is usually 35–40% fat; cream below that generally contains carrageenan. Swedish dairies do not sell cream above 40% fat. Not the same as matlagningsgrädde (about 10–15% fat).
- Where / who: Nationwide; industrial.
- Sources: https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vispgrädde — fat content, whipping, uses, difference from cooking cream
- Unsure: None

## Consumer milks: standardmjölk, mellanmjölk, lättmjölk, minimjölk (heat-treated)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: drinking milk (heat-treated)
- What it is: The standard Swedish drinking milk is 3% fat (3.5% in the rest of the EU); the oldest type, introduced during the Second World War to give more cream for butter. Lower-fat grades are mellanmjölk (green pack), lättmjölk (blue pack) and minimjölk (yellow pack), with colour codes since 1985 and minimjölk since 1990. Helmjölk in Sweden means unskimmed milk.
- How it is made: Heat-treated before sale: the sv Mjölk article says milk sold in Sweden must be heat-treated, most often by pasteurisation (second pass). UHT is not confirmed.
- Where / who: Nationwide; industrial (Arla pack colour codes cited).
- Sources: https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standardmjölk — fat grades, history, pack colours, helmjölk; https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mjölk — heat treatment required for sale in Sweden, mostly by pasteurisation
- Unsure: Fat percentages for mellanmjölk, lättmjölk and minimjölk are not stated in the source; the UHT question is not verified.

## Kondenserad mjölk (condensed milk, sweetened and unsweetened)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: condensed milk / milk preserve
- What it is: Milk reduced by water removal to about 25% of its volume, brown and syrupy. The sweetened version (40–45% sugar) keeps without sterilisation; the unsweetened version (called evaporated milk in English) needs sterilisation.
- How it is made: Water is removed, for example by vacuum evaporation, then the product is packed in tin cans. Sweetened milk is heavily sugared. No temperatures given.
- Where / who: Sweden; industrial. Its use has fallen since powdered milk became common but it is still a raw material in food industry. Shelf life: 4–6 months at room temperature sealed; a few days open.
- Sources: https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kondenserad_mjölk — process, sugar content, uses, decline in Sweden, EU rules (LIVSFS 2003:16)
- Unsure: The article names no Swedish brands, so none are listed.

## Mjölkpulver (torrmjölk, milk powder)
- Animal(s): cow (also goat, less common)
- Kind: dried milk product
- What it is: Dried whole milk, skimmed milk, whey, or buttermilk powder; dissolved at about 125 g per litre.
- How it is made: Spray drying (Kronberg process): skimmed milk is heated, inoculated with lactic acid bacteria to convert lactose to lactic acid, and sprayed into a hot-air chamber; a weak electrostatic field keeps particles apart. Other methods: roller and freeze drying. About 6–7 litres of fresh milk make 1 kg powder.
- Where / who: Sweden; industrial. Invented (per the article) by Ninni Kronberg in 1933; first Swedish factory was Svenska Mejeriprodukter AB (SMP), later brand Semper.
- Sources: https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torrmjölk — process, types, history, SMP/Semper
- Unsure: The article does not name uses in baking, infant food or industry.

## Kefir (Arla kefir)
- Animal(s): cow (also goat, sheep, horse, camel, soy, coconut possible)
- Kind: fermented milk drink
- What it is: Kefir is a soured milk drink made with kefir grains. Arla launched a kefir in Sweden in 1978 at 3% fat; Norrmejerier makes one from northern milk at 2.5% fat.
- How it is made: Kefir grains are added to milk and strained out for reuse; no temperature or time given. Plastic, glass or wood utensils recommended (metal damages grains). Arla's product differs in taste from home kefir (fewer microbes, not naturally carbonated).
- Where / who: Sweden; industrial (Arla, Norrmejerier). Home-made kefir is also made; sale paused by Arla in March 2022 over packaging and resumed later.
- Sources: https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kefir — grains method, Arla launch, Norrmejerier product, 2022 pause
- Unsure: None

## Yoghurt (natural, fruit, drinking yoghurt, low-sugar, lactose-free, greek-style)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: fermented milk (yoghurt and drinkable yoghurt)
- What it is: Swedish yoghurt sold in natural, fruit, berry, vanilla and honey flavours, and as low-sugar, low-fat, organic, lactose-free, drinking yoghurt and cooking yoghurt. Greek-style (strained) is also sold.
- How it is made: Pasteurised milk and cream with live culture of Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus and Streptococcus thermophilus, held at about 42 °C until set. Bulgarian method evaporates milk to half before culturing (not Swedish). No time given.
- Where / who: Sweden; industrial. The Swedish article does not describe Swedish yoghurt history.
- Sources: https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoghurt — varieties, culture and temperature, Bulgarian method
- Unsure: The Swedish article is flagged as needing more sources, so the method is less certain.

## Ostkaka (Swedish cheesecake; curd dish and dessert)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: milk-curd dish / dessert
- What it is: Swedish dish and dessert made from curdled milk and wheat flour, traditionally baked; Småland and Hälsingland varieties. Served with jam, whipped cream, or cloudberries.
- How it is made: Milk is curdled with rennet to form the curd mass, mixed with flour, and baked (Småland version also with almonds, eggs, cream and sugar; Hälsingland version smoother and springier). Oven temperatures and times not given.
- Where / who: Småland and Hälsingland; home and traditional; documented in Sweden since 1538.
- Sources: https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ostkaka — ingredients, methods, uses, history, classification as dish and dessert
- Unsure: Made with rennet curd, so it sits on the cheese boundary; the Swedish article files it under both dairy products and cheese dishes. Included here as a milk dessert; the cheese researcher may also claim it.

## Äggost (egg and milk curd dish)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: milk-egg dish / dessert
- What it is: A typical Bohuslän dish made from milk and eggs, with vinegar, filmjölk or gräddfil, cooked until it curdles and then drained in a star-shaped mould. Historically a feast-food on smörgåsbord or breakfast; now mainly a dessert with sugar, cinnamon and cream, and lingonberry or cloudberry jam. Bohuslän's landskapsrätt since 2010.
- How it is made: The mixture is simmered slowly until it separates into whey and curd, then drained through a perforated, often star-shaped mould and ladled out, sometimes layered with sugar. No temperature or time given.
- Where / who: Bohuslän (west Sweden); traditional. Äggostens dag since 2013 at Säbygården on Tjörn.
- Sources: https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Äggost — ingredients, method, Bohuslän, serving history, landskapsrätt
- Unsure: The article does not state whether the cheese-like curd is intended as cheese; the Swedish name refers to it as ost.

## Risgrynsgröt (rice porridge)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: milk porridge (dish with milk as a main ingredient)
- What it is: Swedish rice porridge (round grain rice) traditionally eaten at Christmas but year-round; served with sugar, cinnamon, milk, butter, jam or sauce. Almond (mandel) tradition.
- How it is made: Cooked in water, milk, cinnamon (often whole), round rice and a little salt. Earlier porridge was made of barley or rye flour; rice spread in the 1800s. Cream, egg and butter added when affordable. No times or temperatures given.
- Where / who: Sweden (and Denmark, Iceland with similar Christmas porridges); home and traditional. The Swedish article notes Livsmedelsverket's advice on arsenic in rice.
- Sources: https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risgrynsgröt — ingredients, serving, traditions, history
- Unsure: Milk as cooking liquid is named but its share of the recipe is not quantified.

## Crème fraîche
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: soured cream
- What it is: A lightly soured cream, less acidic than gräddfil; can be boiled without curdling, whipped, or used as a dip. Flavoured variants exist. Introduced to Sweden in the early 1980s.
- How it is made: Unpasteurised cream is soured with Lactobacillus culture until thick; the process can be stopped by pasteurisation. No temperature or time given for the commercial method.
- Where / who: Sweden; industrial. Production site not stated.
- Sources: https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crème_fraîche — culture, uses, flavour, introduction in the 1980s
- Unsure: Origin of Swedish production is not confirmed (the article does not say where it is made).

## Glass (ice cream and milk ice cream)
- Animal(s): cow
- Kind: frozen milk dessert
- What it is: Swedish ice cream in several legal categories: hushållsglass (home dessert, 0.5–2 litres), mjölkglass (at least 3% fat, sometimes called lättglass), gräddglass (at least 8% milk fat), saftis, sherbet and sorbet. Swedish ice cream has no legal composition requirements since 2002; the industry works to an agreement via Euroglaces.
- How it is made: Not described in the source beyond the fat thresholds.
- Where / who: Sweden; industrial. Producers named include Hemglass, Guteglass, Noraglass, Sia Glass, Triumf Glass, Trollhätteglass, Åse-Glass, Lejonet & Björnen, Heartbrand (with Frisko and GB Glace), Diplom-Is, Ben & Jerry's and Häagen-Dazs. Records of glass making in Sweden from the 1700s (Cajsa Warg's cookbook, 1755). Per capita consumption about 12 litres (2025).
- Sources: https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glass — categories and fat thresholds, history, producers, consumption
- Unsure: The source names no traditional Swedish flavours (vanilla and berry are not confirmed as traditional).

## Chokladmjölk (chokladdryck; chocolate milk)
- Animal(s): Not stated (made with milk or water)
- Kind: flavoured milk drink
- What it is: A chocolate drink made from cocoa powder and sugar mixed with milk or water. Sold cold and ready to drink in cartons; Pucko is named as another brand of cold ready-to-drink chocolate milk, and one brand name has become a generic word for the drink.
- How it is made: At home, the cocoa is first stirred into the sugar and a little of the liquid, then the rest of the milk or water is added under stirring, because cocoa with high fat content clumps. Instant powders with fat-reduced cocoa and anti-clumping additives are added straight to the liquid and stirred.
- Where / who: Sweden; industrial and home preparation.
- Sources: https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chokladmjölk — preparation method, Pucko as a brand, brand name becoming generic
- Unsure: Industrial processing of the ready-to-drink product is not described.
