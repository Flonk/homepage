## Kurut (Курут; qurut, kurut, kurt)
- Animal(s): sheep, goat or cow milk (Kazakh variants also mare and camel); the Kyrgyz article describes it from suzma, which is made from ayran, so the animal is not stated there; the 2024 metagenomic abstract (PMID 38230544) names cow milk for Kyrgyz kurut
- Kind: dried curd (salted, sun-dried balls); also a boiled paste form and a semi-solid form
- What it is: a traditional Kyrgyz staple of the herding year, a dry, salty, sour fermented-milk product sold as balls and used as a snack, a seasoning, or dissolved in water as a drink (suusunduk) or in winter and spring as katyk.
- How it is made: milk is soured to ayran; ayran is poured into a cloth bag to drain the whey (sary suu) and make suzma; suzma is rolled between the palms into shapes; optionally salted and boiled, or left unboiled, sometimes with herbs (ezhigei, tybyt); the shaped pieces are dried on boards or drying racks in the sun or shade for several days to weeks. The Russian article describes the same chain from soured milk, boiling the strained whey-curd for 2–3 h for the boiled form. Modern versions are made in small workshops with apricot, beetroot or pumpkin added. No temperatures or times are given in the Kyrgyz article; a 2026 probiotic kurut study records a semi-solid version rolled at 28 °C and dried 3 days.
- Texture / ripening: hard when fully dried; salty and sour; the 2024 bacterial survey found mostly Lactobacillus, with Streptococcus, Enterococcus and Lactococcus.
- Where / who: Kyrgyz staple across the country (Chuy, Issyk-Kul, Talas, Naryn, Jalal-Abad, Osh and Batken regions sampled); widely eaten by schoolchildren (survey of 7251, about 80% have eaten it). Shared with Kazakh, Uzbek, Turkmen, Tajik, Tuvan and other Turkic peoples.
- Sources: https://ky.wikipedia.org/wiki/Курут — Kyrgyz method (suzma, drying, modern workshops, drink/katyk use); https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/Курут — kurut types, boiled version, Kyrgyz ball size 1.5–4 cm; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qurut — traditional and modern method (describes Kyrgyz qurut from full-fat yogurt in a churn, which differs from the Kyrgyz article); https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC13066772/fullTextXML — semi-solid and dried kurut process; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12608619/fullTextXML — Kyrgyz name and short description; PubMed 38230544 (via Europe PMC abstract) — bacteria in 84 Kyrgyz samples; PubMed 34719146 (via Europe PMC abstract) — salt and nutrition survey in the Kyrgyz Republic; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:38230544%20AND%20SRC:MED&format=json&resultType=core — 2024 abstract: kurut from fermented cow milk, 84 samples from seven Kyrgyz regions; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=kurut%20whey&format=json&resultType=lite — 2025 abstract: kurut made at a factory in Kyrgyzstan.
- Unsure: the source milk differs by article: English Wikipedia says Kyrgyz qurut comes from full-fat yogurt churned to butter; the Kyrgyz article says it comes from suzma (ayran drained). Russian Wikipedia gives sheep, goat or cow milk. The 2024 abstract names cow milk for Kyrgyz kurut; sheep and goat are still not confirmed for Kyrgyz kurut.

## Suzma (сүзмө)
- Animal(s): not stated (ayran from the local milk, cow or small stock)
- Kind: fresh strained curd
- What it is: the strained curd of ayran, drained in a cloth bag and used as the base for kurut; eaten fresh as a spread or made into balls.
- How it is made: ayran is poured into a cloth bag and hung so the whey (sary suu) drains off; the curd is shaped by rolling between the palms.
- Texture / ripening: soft, fresh, unripened.
- Where / who: Kyrgyz household product, mainly as the stage before kurut.
- Sources: https://ky.wikipedia.org/wiki/Курут — suzma as strained curd from ayran in a cloth bag, the first stage of kurut.
- Unsure: the Kyrgyz article gives suzma only as an intermediate product for kurut, not as a stand-alone cheese. Included because it is a named curd product with its own description; it may be better treated as a kurut step than an entry.

## Irimshik (Kyrgyz: Sir; Kazakh: irimshik)
- Animal(s): not stated in the atlas source; a Kazakh-origin curd
- Kind: boiled sour-milk curd (whey-type)
- What it is: a "national delicacy" made from curd of sour milk slow-cooked for several hours, with a golden-brown colour and sweet, creamy taste, per the atlas paper; the Kyrgyz name is "Sir", the same word as the generic cheese word.
- How it is made: sour milk is slow-cooked for several hours until the curd forms and browns; further steps not given in the source.
- Texture / ripening: golden-brown, sweet and creamy, per the atlas paper.
- Where / who: named in Kyrgyz as "Sir" in the Central Asian food atlas; described as a Kazakh national product.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12608619/fullTextXML — Kazakh name irimshik, Kyrgyz "Sir", slow-cooked sour-milk curd; https://ky.wikipedia.org/wiki/Сыр — Kyrgyz word for cheese, generic cheese article only (no irimshik).
- Unsure: a Kazakh product named in the Kyrgyz name list. Whether Kyrgyz households make it as irimshik or simply as "sir" is not confirmed. The Russian and English Wikipedia pages on irimshik returned 404, so no second source was read.

## Cheese sticks (Kyrgyz: Sýr tayakchalary; Kazakh: Irimshik taiaqshalary)
- Animal(s): not stated (mozzarella-type cheese per the atlas)
- Kind: fried or grilled cheese (breaded)
- What it is: breaded and fried cheese sticks, often made with mozzarella, served as a snack in cafes and restaurants.
- How it is made: cheese cut into sticks, breaded, deep-fried; method not further described.
- Texture / ripening: not stated.
- Where / who: urban snack in Kyrgyzstan and Central Asia, sold under the same Kyrgyz name in the atlas.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12608619/fullTextXML — Kyrgyz name and description of breaded and fried cheese sticks.
- Unsure: a modern snack made from a commercial cheese rather than a local type. Included as a fried cheese; drop if only traditional cheeses are wanted.
