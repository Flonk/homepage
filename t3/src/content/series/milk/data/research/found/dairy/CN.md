## Kumis / airag / 马奶酒 (mare's milk wine)
- Animal(s): horse (mare)
- Kind: fermented milk drink (lightly alcoholic)
- What it is: Fermented mare's milk, slightly alcoholic, historically consumed in northern China by Han, Khitan and Jurchen peoples and widely in Mongol-ruled territory. Chinese name 马奶酒 (also 奶酒).
- How it is made: Traditionally raw mare's milk is stirred or shaken for hours or days in leather bags; lactobacilli sour it and yeasts make it mildly carbonated and alcoholic (about 0.7–2.5%). Modern controlled version: about 2–5 hours at about 27 °C, then cooling. Most commercial versions use cow's milk, sometimes with added sucrose or whey. Distillation gives arkhi/araka.
- Where / who: Mongolia and Inner Mongolia; historically northern China and Yuan-era Mongol courts; also Uyghur and Kazakh areas. Industrial versions exist.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kumis — history, names, production, Chinese context (Han North China, Yuan); https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/马奶酒 — traditional and modern method, 0.7–2.5% alcohol, 27 °C for 2–5 h, Inner Mongolia; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_cuisine — kumis consumed in northern China historically.
- Unsure: Pre-Tang and Xianbei-era claims are in the Chinese cuisine article but not verified from another source.

## Nai lao / Beijing yoghurt (北京酸奶, 奶酪 nǎilào)
- Animal(s): not stated (milk)
- Kind: sweet fermented milk drink / set yoghurt in small bowls
- What it is: A traditional fermented milk drink popular across China, mainly associated with Beijing. The name nǎilào (奶酪) is also the Chinese word for cheese in some regions, which is a source of confusion.
- How it is made: Milk is heated and sweetened with sugar. Two kinds of nuts, raisins and glutinous-rice rice wine are stirred in. The mixture is poured into about fifty small bowls, stacked in a 酸奶桶 ("yoghurt barrel") with a central heat chamber; after roughly thirty minutes of heating the bowls are cooled.
- Where / who: Beijing and across northern and general China; traditionally sold in ceramic bowls returned by customers, now often in plastic cups. A US factory specialising in it opened in 2011.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nai_lao — steps, bowls and barrel, serving, sale; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_cuisine — listed as drinkable yoghurt traditional to northern China.
- Unsure: Steps are from a single article; no temperature given.

## Yoghurt / 酸奶 (suānnǎi, 老酸奶)

- Where / who: Mainland China widely; Taiwan uses 優格, Hong Kong 乳酪 (yoghurt) vs 酸奶 (lactic-acid drink).
- Sources: https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/酸奶 — naming, definition, protein thresholds, types; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_cuisine — yoghurt popular with Xianbei and Han in the Northern Wei era, used in Qinghai and Xinjiang, Yunnan's yoghurt possibly Mongol/Central Asian influence.

## Butter tea (酥油茶, suyu, po cha, bho jha)
- Animal(s): yak (traditional); cow butter increasingly used in cheaper versions
- Kind: butter-tea drink (salted, churned milk fat tea)
- What it is: Tea mixed with yak butter, salt and tsampa, drunk by most Tibetans, often several cups a day. Chinese name 酥油茶.
- How it is made: Tea is boiled or steeped until nearly black, strained into a wooden churn with salt (and optionally soda), a large lump of butter added and churned until it reaches the right consistency; kept warm in copper pots on a brazier. Premium: pu-erh boiled about half a day, then shaken with fresh yak butter and salt. Modern shortcut: tea bags, store-bought butter, blender. No temperatures given.
- Where / who: Tibetan Autonomous Region, Qinghai and other Tibetan areas in China; Himalayan and Central Asian regions. Uyghur etken chay is a related milk tea in Xinjiang.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butter_tea — ingredients, churning, monastery and town methods, modern shortcut, Chinese name 酥油茶; https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/酥油 — yak butter used for butter tea, Qinghai-Tibet Plateau.
- Unsure: none.

## Yak butter (酥油, sūyóu)
- Animal(s): yak (domesticated, Bos grunniens)
- Kind: butter / butter fat
- What it is: Butter from yak milk, a staple and trade good for herders on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau; about twice the fat of cow's milk; tastes more like cheese than cream. Used in butter tea, cooking, pastries, ritual sculpture and lamps.
- How it is made: Fat is separated from yak milk and the cream churned until it becomes butter (general method from a yak milk review, not China-specific; see Sources). Chinese churning details are not given.
- Where / who: Tibetans and herders on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau (Tibet, Qinghai, Sichuan, Gansu).
- Sources: https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/酥油 — yak source, fat content, uses, scarcity and cultural notes; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yak_milk — yak butter used in butter tea and burned in lamps and torma sculptures; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC10338857/fullTextXML — yak milk butter made by separating fat from yak milk and churning until it becomes butter.
- Unsure: Lamp and torma use is from the English yak article, not Chinese sources.

## Hong Kong-style milk tea (港式奶茶, silk-stocking milk tea, 絲襪奶茶)
- Animal(s): evaporated milk (animal not stated; presumably cow)
- Kind: milk tea
- What it is: Ceylon black tea with evaporated milk (or sometimes condensed milk), made in cha chaan teng diners. Originated mid-20th century under British rule; often attributed to Lan Fong Yuen (1952).
- How it is made: Blend of broken orange pekoe, fannings, dust and Lipton. Hot water at 96–98 °C poured from 60 cm, steeped 12 min; "pulled" through a cotton bag four times while the leaves are half afloat; held at 94 °C for up to an hour; evaporated milk (about 30%) added before serving. Iced version served in an ice bath.
- Where / who: Hong Kong; mainland China and Taiwan variants exist; the spread to the mainland is not covered by the source.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong-style_milk_tea — blend, temperatures, times, origin, evaporated milk ratio, iced version; https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/炼乳 — cha jau in Hong Kong uses condensed milk.
- Unsure: Whether it is made in mainland China in the same form is not confirmed.

## Double skin milk (双皮奶, shuāngpínǎi)
- Animal(s): buffalo (traditional); cow's milk also used
- Kind: milk custard / dessert
- What it is: A Cantonese dessert from Shunde, Guangdong; velvety milk custard, somewhat like panna cotta. Chinese name 双皮奶.

## Condensed and evaporated milk (炼乳 / 淡奶; 奶精 is a non-dairy substitute)
- Animal(s): cow (implied; not stated in the sources)
- Kind: concentrated and sweetened milk; evaporated (unsweetened) milk
- What it is: Milk concentrated to about a quarter of its water content and sweetened; unsweetened evaporated milk is 淡奶. Used in cha chaan teng milk tea, cha jau (茶走), desserts, drizzled on fruit and shaved ice.

## Milk powder and infant formula (奶粉, 乳粉; 配方奶粉)

- What it is: Milk powder made by processing and drying milk; forms include whole, skim, modified (调制), and infant/children's/adult/elderly/pregnant formulas. Infant formula is registered in China.

- Where / who: Nationwide. Chinese brands listed as examples: 飞鹤, 红星, 完达山, 秦俑, 开心羊 (goat/sheep), 圣元, 古城, 英雄, 光明, 惠民, 蒙牛, 雅士利, 伊利. The 2008 melamine scandal revoked some brands; a 2016 registration rule for infant formula took effect 1 Oct 2016 and became mandatory 1 Jan 2018.

## Shubat (fermented camel milk; no Chinese name given in the sources)
- Animal(s): Bactrian camel
- Kind: fermented camel milk drink
- What it is: Traditional fermented camel milk, a staple of nomadic communities in northern China for centuries. Shubat was also sampled in Inner Mongolia's Xilin Gol and in Mongolia in a fermented-dairy microbiome study.
- How it is made: Fresh camel milk ferments spontaneously at ambient temperature over several days, driven by lactic acid bacteria already in the milk; the bacterial community moves from Lactococcus dominance to Lactobacillus. No temperatures or vessel details are given.
- Where / who: Nomadic communities of northern China; Inner Mongolia Xilin Gol.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11478162/fullTextXML — shubat as traditional fermented camel milk in northern China, spontaneous fermentation over several days; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8535065/fullTextXML — shubat (fermented camel milk) samples from Inner Mongolia Xilin Gol and Mongolia.
- Unsure: The Lactococcus-to-Lactobacillus shift comes from one Bactrian camel study; whether all modern shubat is made this way is not stated.

## Bactrian camel milk and camel milk powder
- Animal(s): Bactrian camel
- Kind: pasteurised camel milk, fermented camel drinks, dehydrated camel milk powder
- What it is: A review says pasteurised milk, fermented beverages and dehydrated camel products are readily available on the Chinese market, and that powder has carried camel milk to urban consumers across China. China's camel herd was about 579,700 in 2023 (National Bureau of Statistics, quoted in the review), mostly Bactrian in mountainous and marginal regions. - Where / who: Northwestern China (Xinjiang, Inner Mongolia, Qinghai), sold across China.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11478162/fullTextXML — camel product types on the Chinese market, herd size, breeds, pasteurisation range, camel milk powder.

## Donor human milk (human milk bank, Southwest China)
- Animal(s): human
- Kind: donated breast milk for premature and sick newborns
- What it is: Donor milk is collected, pasteurised and distributed by a human milk bank (HMB). Southwest China's first HMB (First Affiliated Hospital of Army Medical University, Chongqing) follows the Chinese group standard T/CNSS2020-003 (Establishment and Management of Human Milk Banks in Medical Institutions). Donors give written consent, complete a lifestyle questionnaire and are tested for HIV, HBV, HCV and syphilis within six months.
- How it is made: Milk is thawed slowly at 4 °C for up to 24 h, pooled in a biosafety cabinet, pasteurised at 62.5 °C for 30 min in an ultrapure-water bath, cooled rapidly and stored at 0–4 °C for up to 72 h or at −20 °C or colder for up to 6 months. Microbiological tests are run before and after pasteurisation.
- Where / who: Chongqing, Southwest China; the study also refers to a first HMB in East China.
- Sources: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC13332609/fullTextXML — HMB processing steps, donor screening, Group Standard T/CNSS2020-003, Southwest China first HMB.
- Unsure: One hospital's longitudinal study; the Chinese name for the bank is not given in the source; no national count of banks.
