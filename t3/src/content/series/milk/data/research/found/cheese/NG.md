## Wara (warankasi; wara-kasi; wara; awara)
- Animal(s): Cow's milk (Zebu cattle per one recipe source; Oyo and Kwara studies cite cow milk; Ogbomoso survey says cow milk; Kwara 2024 survey says milk pooled from Fulani transhumance farms).
- Kind: Soft, white, fresh curd cheese (cottage-type); fried street-food form also common.
- What it is: A soft, unripened white curd cheese of Yoruba and northern-Nigerian Fulani tradition, sold in whey, in calabashes or in small bags, and eaten fresh, boiled, or deep-fried. Wikipedia calls wara a name also used for foreign cheeses in general (wara also means milk in Yoruba).
- How it is made: Fresh milk is heated slowly, stirred intermittently, and brought to a boil; a plant extract is added (Calotropis procera / Sodom apple leaves, juice or extract, the conventional coagulant). In a Tona 2014 Ogbomoso method: milk warmed to 45–50 °C over 30–40 min, about 4 ml leaf and stem extract per litre added, heated to about 95 °C until curd and whey separate (coagulation in 10–20 min), curd boiled a further 20 min, then drained through small raffia baskets that shape it; sold the same day or stored in whey for 2–3 days. In a 2017 Akure trial (LRRD), milk heated to 50 °C, extract added, heated until it boiled and coagulated, curds sieved and pressed under 2.5 kg for 45 min; salting and ripening not described. A 2025 review of West African soft cheese gives the same general sequence: warm the milk, add the plant extract, keep or raise the temperature until coagulation, then cook a few minutes to expel whey; Calotropis procera is the predominant coagulant in the studies it reviewed, and many studies do not report temperatures or times. Calotropis (leaf) coagulation temperatures reported between 55 and 80 °C across studies; a 2026 Tropentag abstract found higher temperature shortened coagulation but cut yield (only in a snippet, not read). A starter-mediated variant (1999, Sanni, Onilude and Momoh) uses a single Lactococcus lactis culture in place of plant extract (the screening temperature in the abstract was 30 °C); the abstract reports a 3% brine and a 5% NaCl whey dip that reduced microbial counts, and starter-made wara with 54% more protein than market samples.
- Texture / ripening: Soft and creamy with a springy bite when fresh, less elastic than mozzarella. Unripened; short shelf life, roughly 2–3 days (wara has poor shelf life and is prone to spoilage). A 2017 Ilorin and Ogbomoso study says fried wara "does not undergo any further treatment" before sale and reports unhygienic production.
- Where / who: Fulani pastoralists and Fulani women make it, and it is sold in markets, motor parks and by roadside vendors across northern, Kwara, Oyo, Ogun, Ondo and Ogbomoso areas; origin widely reported to lie in the north and spread with Fulani movement (a 2018 source, not read). Not protected (no GI status found). A 2024 Kwara survey bought 18 cheese samples from three rural markets (six per local government area), and milk for wara was collected from Fulani transhumance farms.

## Wagashi (FAO multi-country entry that lists Nigeria)
- Animal(s): Cow's milk.
- Kind: Soft, brine-pickled, rindless cheese with small eyes; slightly sour and salty.
- What it is: A West African soft cheese, listed by FAO among nine West African countries including Nigeria. Usually fried before eating and used as protein in soups and stews.
- How it is made: Milk is boiled, then coagulated with juice of crushed Bryophyllum stems (not rennet or starter). Curd is shaped into balls, salted and kept in 25% brine.
- Texture / ripening: Soft, with small eyes; brined.
- Where / who: Listed as produced in Nigeria among nine West African countries; no Nigerian producers named. Likely the same product as wara under a regional name (wagashi is used for wara in Nigerian sources).
- Sources: https://www.fao.org/4/t0251e/t0251e12.htm — FAO cheese catalogue, entry 1.14 "Wagashi", multi-country including Nigeria; Bryophyllum coagulant; 25% brine.
- Unsure: The Bryophyllum method and brining are not found in any Nigerian source; the entry may describe a West African (Ghanaian or Beninese) method. Treat the Nigerian claim as the FAO's, not a confirmed Nigerian practice.

## Country cheese (Wase and Wawa/Zange grazing reserves; Plateau and Bauchi States)
- Animal(s): Cow's milk (FAO entry; one search snippet did not name the milk).
- Kind: Hard cheese, short ripening, smoked rind, small eyes; cut into 1 kg square blocks.
- What it is: A hard cow's-milk cheese introduced by a UNDP/FAO rural-development project in grazing reserves; listed in FAO's cheese catalogue as a Nigerian country cheese.
- How it is made: Unpasteurised milk coagulated with powdered rennet; curd cut, stirred and pressed; blocks heat-treated in whey, dry-salted, smoked (2–4 hours), and vacuum-packed. Sealed packs keep several weeks at 4 °C.
- Texture / ripening: Hard; curd yellowish, taste slightly acid and salty; short ripening.
- Where / who: Plateau and Bauchi States; produced seasonally, with production described as developing steadily in the grazing reserves. Introduced by the project, not a traditional cheese.
- Sources: https://www.fao.org/4/t0251e/t0251e12.htm — FAO cheese catalogue, entry 1.4 "Country cheese", Nigeria; origin, process, packaging and seasonality.
- Unsure: The FAO entry is old. A search snippet suggested the same process may belong to a Sudanese cheese (the FAO remark on staple for the Sudanese population); unresolved. A second snippet described it as braided and stored in brine, which the FAO page does not say.

## Chukwi (chukuwi; camel or cow milk)
- Animal(s): Camel or cow milk (Daily Trust); most supply reported to come from Niger.
- Kind: Not stated; described as a cheese in the Daily Trust article; the detail does not say whether it is soft, hard or dried.
- What it is: A cheese popular in northern Nigerian states bordering Niger; eaten with dates in some places and considered nourishing (protein, calcium, fat). Recommended for children under 15 and people who want to gain weight, not those trying to lose weight, per the cited dietician.
- How it is made: Not described in the source.
- Texture / ripening: Not described.
- Where / who: Katsina, Sokoto, Zamfara, Kano, Jigawa; local production once in Jibia, Kaita, Batsari, Safana, Danmusa, Kankara and Sabuwa LGAs, declining after banditry drove herders away; mostly imported from Niger, small local output.
- Sources: https://dailytrust.com/?p=1370595 — Daily Trust report on chukwi: camel or cow milk; northern states; imported from Niger; production decline.
- Unsure: Whether chukwi is a cheese in the strict sense is not clear from the source; a Kano study of camel-milk cheese (chukwui) and soybean cake was found only as a search snippet (see leads). Camel-milk cheese recipe is not confirmed.
